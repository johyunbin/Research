A B S T R A C T 
Existing knowledge is limited concerning changes in behavior and preferences for urban outdoor physical ac­
tivities during the post-COVID-19 period, particularly in the long term before and after COVID-19. In this study, 
we collected GPS-based trajectory data from social media platforms prior to, during, and following COVID-19 
lockdowns (2017–2019, 2020–2022, and after 2023). We utilized multi-source data, multi-level models, inter­
rupted time series models, and seasonal prediction models to analyze changes in Chinese urban residents’ 
preferences for outdoor physical activities and the environment. The study revealed that residents’ activity 
preferences decreased each year during the COVID-19 lockdown policy but increased immediately following the 
lifting of restrictions, with activity levels significantly exceeding pre-pandemic averages. The study highlights 
that while safety was the primary concern during the pandemic, preferences shifted towards low-density areas, 
well-developed urban infrastructures, accessible green spaces, and ocean views in the post-pandemic period. 
These findings provide valuable insights for urban design aimed at enhancing physical activity and well-being in 
outdoor settings. 
1. Introduction 
The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 led to significant changes in daily 
lives. The implementation of stay-at-home orders and community lock­
downs during this period significantly limited residents’ daily activities 
(Burnett et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Ugolini et al., 2020). The environ­
mental exposures and residents’ health behaviors and preferences have 
changed due to the COVID-19 (Matasov et al., 2023), with studies indi­
cating that health-related behaviors like physical activity did not return to 
pre-pandemic levels even after the restrictions imposed by COVID-19 
policies were lifted (Koch et al., 2022; Salway et al., 2022). A lack of reg­
ular physical activity significantly contribute to various chronic diseases 
(Feng et al., 2010; Harris et al., 2013; Sallis et al., 2012). Research has 
shown that regular participation in physical activities such as jogging and 
walking significantly improves both physical and mental health. (Aliyas, 
2019; Berg et al., 2019; Hankey et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022; Mahdzir & 
Ahmad Roslan, 2023; Oja et al., 2017). However, the impact of the COVID- 
19 pandemic on residents’ outdoor activity behavior and environmental 
preferences remains insufficiently investigated. 
Particularly, lock down policies, including stay-at-home orders and 
community lockdowns, were intermittently enforced in Chinese cities from 
January 20, 2020, for three years until the end of December 2022. During 
this period, most public gatherings, exhibitions, and indoor entertainment 
were restricted. Numerous studies have examined the impact of green 
spaces and other factors on leisure behavior and psychology amidst the 
pandemic (Beckmann-Wübbelt et al., 2021; Muro et al., 2023; Soga et al., 
2021; Ugolini et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). However, few studies have 
investigated residents’ preferences for urban street environments, which 
are widely recognized as crucial for outdoor physical activities (Wang 
et al., 2019). Previous research has shown that the urban built environ­
ment, the eye-level street design, and people’s perceptions significantly 
influence outdoor activities, although the findings have been inconsistent 
and primarily based on cross-sectional surveys. Therefore, this study aims 
to investigate the effects of three consecutive years of lockdown policies on 
outdoor physical activity levels in Chinese cities and to explore the un­
derlying factors of post-pandemic changes in outdoor physical activity 
preferences, such as street design characteristics and built environment. 
We therefore employed trajectory data, street view imagery, and multi- 
source data to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on outdoor 
activities and residents’ environmental preferences. The contribution of 
this research is primarily manifested in three aspects. First, we analyzed 
spatial-temporal variations in outdoor physical activity using seven years

2. Literature review 
2.1. Application of volunteered geographic information data 
The volunteered geographic information (VGI), as shared on fitness 
platforms, represents an emerging approach for assessing outdoor ac­
tivity (Cui et al., 2021; Dagan & Wilkins, 2023; Dong et al., 2023; 
Nogueira Mendes et al., 2023; Norman et al., 2019; Venter et al., 2023). 
VGI encompasses user-generated content and associated geographical 
data, including routes, reports, and images disseminated online (Cui 
et al., 2021). The advantage of VGI lies in its remote accessibility which 
often proves more time- and cost-effective compared to traditional data 
sources. It provides precise GPS-based routes that accurately depict the 
actual occurrence of physical activities making it widely employed in 
conjunction with other datasets. Moreover, VGI enables the comparison 
of access patterns across various spatial and temporal scales (Huang 
et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). 
With the exponential growth of VGI, numerous studies have focused 
on park visits, for example, using the number of routes posted on plat­
forms, such as MapMyFitness, Strava, and Wikiloc, to assess outdoor 
activities in national parks (Norman et al., 2019; Norman & Pickering, 
2019), jogging in urban parks (Liu et al., 2021a), recreational behavior 
in suburban parks (Nogueira Mendes et al., 2023), and the relationship 
between running satisfaction and natural exposure (Huang et al., 2023). 
Moreover, research has focused on the use of VGI in urban environments 
to study outdoor activities, finding that an action-based approach is 
more effective than a residential buffer approach for accurately 
reflecting the sports environment (Huang et al., 2022). 
Although extensive research has explored the relationship between 
the built environment and outdoor activities in urban settings, results 
continue to show considerable variability. For example, while some 
studies report no significant associations between population density 
and running activities (Huang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022a), others 
document a clear negative correlation between urban density and 
running frequency, but a positive correlation with running intensity 
(Huang et al., 2022). Additionally, enclosed environments and densely 
built areas have been found to hinder running (Dong et al., 2023). In 
contrast, design-related features such as proximity to green and blue 
spaces have shown more consistent positive associations with running 
intensity. Numerous studies have confirmed that blue spaces or water 
features enhance running intensity (Huang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021b; 
Yang et al., 2023b). Furthermore, Huang et al. (2023) demonstrated that 
the Green View Index (GVI), which assesses street greening, more 
accurately represents runners’ exposure to greenery than the top-down 
greenery evaluation provided by the Normalized Difference Vegetation 
Index (NDVI). Other environmental features, such as terrain slope 
(Campbell et al., 2019), traffic volume, and intersections (Nixon, 2012), 
also significantly impact the running experience. 
Previous studies have consistently shown that several socio- 
environmental features significantly influence running activities. Ele­
ments such as sidewalks, streetlights, amenities, urban furniture, trees, and 
sky views are known to promote running (Jiang et al., 2022). Additionally, 
a better perceived quality of these SE features can facilitate sustainable 
physical activity and enhance public health. Safety emerges as a critical 
factor in promoting outdoor activities; studies suggest that wider and more 
open streets, perceived as safer, significantly enhance the likelihood of 
engaging in running and other outdoor activities (Dong et al., 2023). These 
preliminary insights into the impact of socio-environmental features on 
running are well-supported by existing research. While many studies have 
employed Volunteered Geographic Information (VGI) to examine spatial 
patterns of physical activity, its use in analyzing outdoor behaviors on 
urban streets over extended periods, such as the three-year span influenced 
by COVID-19 isolation policies, remains limited. 
2.2. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic 
The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly affected the global com­
munity. The short- and long-term consequences of this crisis are evident 
across various domains of human society, particularly influencing in­
dividuals’ health behaviors (Nigg et al., 2023). Researchers have 
observed that health behaviors, such as physical activity, have not 
returned to pre-pandemic levels (Koch et al., 2022; Salway et al., 2022). 
Beyond its impact on psychosocial health and behaviors, the pandemic 
has also altered how individuals utilize urban and natural environments 
(Matasov et al., 2023). 
Numerous studies have examined the impact of COVID-19 on leisure 
behavior in green spaces, which are crucial for influencing physical 
activity patterns and providing sanctuary (Muro et al., 2023; Ugolini 
et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021). However, findings regarding the effects 
of the COVID-19 pandemic on environmental utilization remain incon­
sistent. For instance, some studies have reported an increase in visits to 
urban and peri-urban forests during the pandemic (Beckmann-Wübbelt 
et al., 2021), as well as heightened usage of trails near urban areas 
(Power et al., 2023). In contrast, others observed a decrease in time 
spent visiting green spaces following the restrictions(Burnett et al., 
2021). Additionally, research has explored the association between the 
urban built environment and health outcomes. Residents in compact 
communities reported lower levels of well-being during COVID-19 
(Mouratidis, 2022), while proximity to various local amenities, lower 
community density, distance from city centers, and larger housing were 
linked to improved health and well-being (Mouratidis & Yiannakou, 
2021). Commonly used methods such as multiple linear regression (Liu 
et al., 2021), multilevel modeling (Huang et al., 2022), and spatial 
modeling (Yang et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023) primarily focus on spatial 
dimensions and often fail to capture temporal changes. Additional ap­
proaches to address temporal variations are thus necessary. 
Research on the long-term effects of the COVID-19 pandemic before, 
during, and after its peak remains relatively scarce. Matasov et al. 
(2023) emphasized the critical role of green spaces during the pandemic 
and observed changes in citizens’ recreational preferences and park 
usage behaviors. Zhang & Li, (2023) employed mobile phone location 
data and a quasi-experimental method to explore the causal impacts of 
urban park access on local residents during the COVID-19 outbreaks, 
lock down policies, and the subsequent resumption of work in 2020. 
Their findings indicate that high-quality green spaces significantly 
increased park visits. Additionally, Yang et al. (2021) conducted natural 
experiments revealing that in areas with extensive greening, the 
reduction in leisure-time physical activity was less pronounced. More­
over, Muro et al. (2023) assessed the psychological benefits of forest 
trekking using a control group design, finding significant improvements 
in mental health, such as reduced stress and enhanced mood, particu­
larly during the pandemic in Mediterranean regions. 
Despite considerable attention to COVID-19 lockdown policies, the 
focus on urban environments and behavioral activities during their 
implementation and subsequent lifting has been relatively limited. The 
impact of these studies predominantly covers short-term effects; however, 
the extensive three-year lock down policy in China introduces un­
certainties regarding its profound influence on urban residents’

Therefore, it is imperative to understand how restrictive policies and

3. Method 
3.1. Study design 
This study’s research framework, as illustrated in Fig. 1, is executed 
in three sequential steps. Initially, representing outdoor activities, we 
collected publicly available KML data shared by users on the social 
platform 2bulu, which is widely used among outdoor enthusiasts in 
China for its location-based services. KML, an XML-based file format, 
facilitates the display of geographic information. Following this, we 
acquired and processed multi-source data that includes sociodemo­
graphic factors (e.g., Property Value, Construction Time, Population 
Density), macro-environmental attributes (e.g., Density, Diversity, 
Design, Destination, Traffic), street-level environment factors (e.g., 
Design Quality, Infrastructure, Perception), and natural elements (Green 
Space, Blue Space), all of which may influence outdoor physical activity 
levels. Additionally, Street View Images (SVI) were collected to compute 
eye-level street design characteristics and urban perception indicators. 
In the second step, the independent variables were preprocessed using 
XGBoost for feature extraction and evaluated with Variance Inflation 
Factor (VIF) to assess multicollinearity. Lastly, the study developed a 
comprehensive suite of models to address both spatial and temporal 
effects, incorporating a multilevel model and two time series models. 
3.2. Study area 
This study was conducted in Dalian, China, a city uniquely charac­
terized by its geographical setting and urban structure. Dalian is a 
coastal city surrounded by the sea on three sides and features a hilly 
terrain (Fig. 2). The gentle slopes, between 5◦and 10◦, are interspersed 
with numerous low mountains and hills, enriching the urban area with 
substantial green assets. However, the city’s hilly nature imposes limi­
tations on the development of bicycle lanes, leading to a reliance on 
pedestrian travel for non-motorized transportation. The research scope 
is confined to four central urban districts encompassing 39 administra­
tive regions, which collectively cover an area of approximately 596 km2 
and house a population of 2.58 million. These districts share similar 
socioeconomic backgrounds. 
The study delineates the pre-, mid-, and post-pandemic periods based 
on changes in national-level COVID-19 prevention and control policies. 
The pre-pandemic period is defined as the time before January 20, 2020, 
when the Chinese National Health Commission designated novel coro­
navirus pneumonia as a statutory infectious disease, triggering the 
implementation of prevention and control measures. Subsequent years 
saw various lockdown orders, culminating on December 22, 2022, when 
the national government announced the cessation of COVID-19 lock­
down measures. Accordingly, the period before the pandemic encom­
passes the years 2017 to 2019, the pandemic period covers 2020 to

3.3. Dependent variable 
In this study, we employed a Python-based application to extract all 
non-private, downloadable user tracks from the past seven years from the 
web version of 2bulu (https://www.2bulu.com/), which categorizes 
tracks by walking, cycling, driving, and other modes of transportation. 
However, distinctions among hiking, jogging, running, and walking ac­
tivities within the application’s track classification system are not 
detailed. Due to the limited availability of bicycle lanes in the central 
districts of Dalian, most recorded outdoor activities consist of walking, 
jogging, and running, which we collectively refer to as ‘outdoor activities. 
Data collection commenced in 2017 and continued until July 2023, 
resulting in the accumulation of 5315 complete trajectory datasets, with 
an average trajectory length of 9605 m. These trajectories were specif­
ically selected using the names of the four central administrative dis­
tricts as search keywords. User information was not accessible due to 
privacy restrictions. 
Data cleaning involved removing data points that fell outside the 
predefined study area boundaries. Further refinement using ArcGIS to­
pology check tools and manual reviews helped identify and eliminate data 
points that did not exhibit typical human activity trajectory characteris­
tics, such as isolated points and trajectories that deviated substantially 
from the established urban road network, thus classified as ‘erratic’. 
For the analysis, we treated the number of trajectories passing 
through each street as the dependent variable. The study area was 
divided into 8481 road segments, each averaging 249 m in length. 
Trajectories were decomposed into these street segments. To gauge the 
intensity of outdoor activities on each street segment, we established a 
60-meter buffer centered on the road median and calculated the total 
number of trajectory lines within this zone. This approach segmented 
the data into 128,135 trajectory segments, with a distribution of 60,089 
pre-pandemic, 44,673 during the pandemic, and 23,373 post-pandemic 
trajectory segments. 
3.4. Independent variable 
We structured a matrix of variables quantifying the sociodemo­
graphic factors, neighborhood-built environment, street design charac­
teristics, and natural environment as shown in Table 1. The calculation 
of all independent variable indicators was standardized, mirroring the 
method employed for the dependent variable, whereby each was 
quantified per street segment. The data were mainly sourced from a 
combination of governmental data repositories and open platform data, 
including the National Natural Resource and Geospatial Basic Informa­
tion Database, Lianjia real estate platform, Baidu Map, Open Street Map, 
and Tencent Street View (TSV) images from the Tencent Online Map. 
3.4.1. Macro-level built environment 
Socioeconomic background factors encompassed property values, 
construction time, and population density, which was obtained from 
real estate platform Lianjia (http://www.lianjia.com). A Python script 
facilitated the extraction of data for 1799 residential communities 
located in the central district of Dalian. Population density was quan­
tified by the number of households. The preprocessing of collected data 
involved the removal of missing values, erroneous entries, and outliers, 
such as atypical construction times and resident population figures. 
The macro-built environment was characterized by variables such as 
density, diversity, design, destination accessibility, and traffic condi­
tions. Building density was defined by the count of buildings within the 
area. Diversity was measured by the variety of land use within the buffer 
zone, indicated by the number of facility types. Intersection data, 
including fork roads and crossroads, were derived from Open Street 
Map. Destination accessibility was assessed based on the availability of 
daily living facilities, with facility point data sourced from Baidu Maps, 
which provides detailed location and facility type information. Traffic 
conditions were evaluated through vehicle counts for cars and buses, 
extracted from Baidu Map and analyzed through street interface ob­
servations using Tencent Street View (TSV) images.

3.4.2. Street-level built environment 
The built environment at street level includes design quality, infra­
structure and subjective perception. Streetscapes design features encom­
passed transparency, enclosures, the green view index, the sky view index, 
sidewalk ratio, and street infrastructure. These streetscape elements were 
defined according to the urban design elements framework by Ewing et al. 
(2015) (Ewing et al., 2015), with detailed definitions and measurements 
presented in Table 1. All the streetscape indicators were computed using 
images from Tencent Street View (TSV). Sampling points for TSV image 
retrieval were strategically generated from road data at various in­
tersections, endpoints, and at 100-m intervals along each street. Co­
ordinates for these TSV sampling points were input into a Python script, 
and for each point, four TSV images (480 × 320 pixels) were downloaded 
facing four directions (0◦, 90◦, 180◦, and 270◦). 
To extract environmental elements from the TSV images, two 
advanced deep learning algorithms were employed: semantic segmen­
tation and instance segmentation. Environmental elements at each 
sampling point were quantified by averaging the pixel values from im­
ages in all four directions, using the DeepLabv3 model (Chen et al., 
2017) trained on the ADE20K dataset. The model achieved 77.31 % 
mIoU on the Pascal VOC 2012 validation dataset. Next, we used the 
YOLOv3 model (Redmon & Farhadi, 2018) to perform instance seg­
mentation to measure street furniture. Street furniture was calculated as 
the total number of streetlights, traffic lights, traffic signs, and seats. The 
accuracy of the model exceeded 92 % from manual verification (Fig. 3). 
In this study, we explored six urban perception dimensions—wealth, 
safety, liveliness, beauty, boredom, and depression—drawing on the Place 
Pulse 2.0 framework by Dubey et al. (2016). These perception indicators, 
encompassing both positive and negative aspects, have been previously 
used to gauge general human perceptions (Zhang et al., 2018). Notably, 
perceptions of safety and liveliness are particularly crucial to runners’ 
experiences (Deelen et al., 2019). These individual and emotional 
perceptions are highly relevant to residents’ daily lives and may signifi­
cantly influence human behavior and physical activity (Biljecki & Ito, 
2021). However, the Place Pulse dataset primarily targets urban environ­
ments globally, which might introduce biases in the context of China’s 
densely populated urban settings. Therefore, this study utilized a domes­
tically trained dataset tailored for urban perception in China. Volunteers 
rated displayed street view images on a scale of 1 to 100 across the six 
perception categories. A random forest algorithm was employed to classify 
images based on single perception labels, achieving an average accuracy of 
over 90 % (Yao et al., 2019). This approach was applied to street views in 
Dalian and has been validated in previous research (Sun et al., 2023a), 
including consistency checks with questionnaire-based environmental 
perceptions. The perceptions of safety and beauty showed high consistency 
with findings from traditional survey studies, validating the reliability of 
using street view images for perception assessment. 
3.4.3. Natural environment 
The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) was calculated 
by utilizing reflectance data from two bands of the electromagnetic 
spectrum: Landsat Band 4 (Near Infrared) and Landsat Band 3 (Red). For 
this analysis, cloud-free images covering the survey area were meticu­
lously selected, with a Landsat 8 OLI (Operational Land Imager) image 
dated July 16, 2018, ultimately chosen for its clarity and relevance. 
The calculation approach for blue space visibility was based on the 
weighted field-of-view aggregation of representative grids of sites in the 
ocean. To determine visible locations within the ocean area, the sea 
region was divided into numerous regular hexagons with a center rep­
resenting each grid. Adjacent locations were spaced at intervals of 1 km. 
Based on previous literature, a maximum visible range of 20 km was 
selected due to limitations imposed by Earth’s curvature and atmo­
spheric conditions (Qiang et al., 2019). Given the geographic specifics of 
our study area, which includes non-isolated islands within central

3.5. Data processing and modeling 
3.5.1. Data processing 
In the data preprocessing stage, we implemented a comprehensive 
strategy encompassing three critical steps: data cleaning, data correc­
tion, and data validation. Initially, during data cleaning, we employed 
multiple measures to ensure data quality. We excluded outliers and 
missing values, focusing particularly on trajectory data, which under­
went meticulous manual review and pruning. Our pruning criteria 
included eliminating trajectories that extended beyond the study area, 
lay outside designated road buffer zones, or exhibited irregular move­
ment patterns or disorderliness. These measures aimed to remove 
inaccurate or irrelevant data points potentially caused by collection 
errors, transmission issues, or external factors. Subsequently, in the data 
correction phase, we concentrated on enhancing the accuracy and 
consistency of the data. We meticulously corrected the geographical 
coordinates to ensure precise correspondence and uniformity across 
different data sources and layers. This step is crucial for ensuring the 
comparability of subsequent data analyses and the reliability of in­
terpretations. Finally, during the data validation phase, we performed 
normality tests to assess the suitability of the data distribution and 
applied Z-score normalization to all data. This standardization method 
mitigates the impact of varying scales and measurement units, ensuring 
data comparability and the reliability of analytical results. 
3.5.2. Variable selection 
We employed variance inflation factor, VIF calculations to identify 
variables with high multicollinearity (VIF > 10), which were then 
removed while retaining other variables. Additionally, we utilized the 
XGboost method for feature extraction – an advanced algorithm based 
on gradient-boosting decision trees that offer improved performance 
compared to classical GBDT models. To determine the relevance of 
environmental indicators in explaining outdoor physical activity levels, 
Scikit-learn in Python was used to calculate feature importance scores 
and rank them accordingly. Finally, a combination of literature review, 
VIF analysis results, and feature importance scores were used to filter 
out irrelevant variables and facilitate further analysis. 
3.5.3. Regression model with spatial effects 
Previous studies have demonstrated that the correlation between 
physical activity and the built environment exhibits spatial autocorrelation 
(Yang et al., 2022b), which may lead to bias or inefficiency in OLS esti­
mates. Multi-level modeling (MLM) provides a robust framework for 
separating the effects of individual factors from contextual factors at the 
neighborhood level, which is essential for accurately assessing health 
outcomes (Schüle & Bolte, 2015). Consequently, we developed a multi- 
level model in which road attributes are considered at a single level. In 
this model, categorical variables were incorporated as fixed effects to 
control for their potential impact on outdoor physical activity trajectories. 
The formula for the multi-level model is as follows: 
Ygij = μ + αg + βXgij + ug + egij 
Ygij represents the number of outdoor physical activity trajectories 
for individual j on road i, where g indicates the road type. 
μ is the overall intercept, representing the average number of out­
door physical activity trajectories across all types of roads; αg represents 
the fixed effects of road types; β is the coefficient for the street envi­
ronment characteristics Xgij, indicating the influence of the street 
environment on the number of outdoor physical activity trajectories; ug 
is the random effect for road types, capturing unobservable heteroge­
neity within each road type; egij is the error term, representing random 
fluctuations at the individual level. 
3.5.4. Regression model with time series 
In this study, the primary advantage of Interrupted Time Series (ITS) 
design lies in its ability to infer causal effects. ITS models are particularly 
suitable for quantifying the immediate and long-term effects of in­
terventions such as public health policies, by comparing behavioral 
changes before and after policy implementation to more accurately 
assess their impact. Unlike other quasi-experimental designs, ITS design 
compares the intervention effects within the same group before and 
after, rather than relying on a control group, providing a robust method 
for evaluating the immediate and long-term effects of COVID-19 lock­
down policies. Additionally, this design allows for within-group

The formula of the model is as follows: 
Yt = β0 + β1 time + β2intervention + β3post + β4intervention2 + β5secondtime 
The variable Yt represented the observation at time t, where time was 
the temporal variable spanning from the beginning to the end of the 
study. The intervention variable indicated whether a policy has been 
implemented, while post denoted another temporal variable starting 
from the promulgation of the policy until the end of the study. By 
employing least square method, we can obtain coefficients for a piece­
wise regression model. β0 served as an intercept term representing the 
initial level of the outcome variable. β1 signified the slope prior to policy 
enactment, reflecting pre-existing trends. β2 captured the outcomes 
upon policy implementation. β3 quantified changes in slope before and 
after policy promulgation, illustrating its sustained impact. Further­
more, β4 accounted for level changes following policy cancellation and 
β5 measures alterations in slope before and after cancellation occurs. The 
two interval nodes were set at 2020 and 2022. 
In this study, we employed the Seasonal AutoRegressive Integrated 
Moving Average model with eXogenous regressors (SARIMAX), an exten­
sion of the traditional ARIMA model, to enhance our understanding of the 
long-term trends in outdoor physical activity preferences among urban 
residents during the COVID-19 pandemic. The SARIMAX model was spe­
cifically chosen to account for and mitigate the effects of seasonal varia­
tions on outdoor activities in urban environments, providing a more 
nuanced analysis of how these patterns have shifted over the course of the 
pandemic. During the model construction process, we first performed 
seasonal decomposition of the time series data to assess the presence of 
seasonal components. Subsequently, a stationarity test was conducted to 
ensure that the time series met the requirements of the SARIMAX model. If 
the original data were non-stationary, necessary differencing operations 
were performed until the data became stationary. Autocorrelation analysis 
aided in identifying any skewness in the time series and guided the selec­
tion of appropriate autoregressive (AR) and moving average (MA) orders. 
Finally, the assessment of the pure randomness in the model residuals (i.e., 
white noise) was conducted by examining the p-values associated with the 
Q statistics, as well as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian 
Information Criterion (BIC) values.

A B S T R A C T 
Urban green and blue spaces provide mental and physical health benefits for their surrounding communities, but 
the key to many of these benefits is the utilization of the urban greenspace for recreation or relaxation. For some 
communities, use is limited by physical access to the greenspace while for other communities, the characteristics 
of the greenspace play an important role in encouraging or discouraging use. What drives communities to use 
their local greenspace with respect to all other available greenspace in an urban area remains an underexplored 
question. To fill this gap, we leverage a large human mobility dataset to track a year of park visitation by res­
idents across the Phoenix, AZ, metropolitan area combined with a spatial autoregressive model using spatially 
lagged dependent variables. Our results suggest that it is not vegetation but the presence of water features within 
a park that encourages higher visitation to local parks. Water features also influence visitation to parks outside a 
communities local area; its presence within a local park decreased how often individuals visited non-local parks 
as a proportion of their total park visitation. We discuss the implications of these finding in the context of a desert 
city that lacks both greenspace and water. 
1. Introduction 
Urban green and blue spaces benefit the social, mental, and physical 
health of a community. Urban greenspace can be parks, woodland, 
cemeteries, community gardens, green allotments, and other features 
dominated by a green vegetation characteristic (Taylor and Hochuli, 
2017). Urban greenspace has been associated with reductions in mental 
health risks and cardiovascular disease (Richardson et al., 2013), an 
increase in community cohesion (Weinstein et al., 2015), and stronger 
social ties (Ka´zmierczak, 2013). Moreover, greenspaces serve as 
important mitigators of climate hazards through reductions in pollution 
and particulate matter, localized flooding from stormwater, and urban 
heat islands (Lei et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022). Bluespace, on the other 
hand, is an all-encompassing name for water features within the urban 
environment such as natural or artificial rivers, streams, lakes, and 
ponds. Like greenspace, bluespace also provides health benefits, 
particularly for mental well-being (Smith et al., 2021; V¨olker and Kis­
temann, 2015). 
To realize these benefits, a community needs to have access to and 
regularly utilize their surrounding green and blue spaces. The World 
Health Organization (WHO) recommends that individuals residing in 
urban areas have access to at least .5 – 1 ha of greenspace that is no more 
than 300 m from their residence (WHO, 2017). European countries such 
as Berlin and Italy follow similar planning guidelines, suggesting in­
dividuals have access to greenspace areas ranging from 6 m2 to 9 m2 per 
person (EEA, 2022). In addition to physical proximity to sufficient 
greenspace, a growing body of research examines features of urban 
greenspace that influence access and visits. For instance, Kim and col­
leagues (2023) examined how the spatial distribution of local parks 
related to park visitation among different social groups based on age, 
income, race, and ethnicity. They found that the percentage of the 
elderly population in a neighborhood showed significant access and visit 
disparities: the increase in the percentage of the elderly population in a 
neighborhood decreased the access to local parks but increased the visits 
to local parks. 
Often local greenspace (e.g., greenspace within walking distance) is 
touted as a mechanism for enhancing greenspace access opportunities in 
urban areas because the presence of greenspace in closer proximity to a 
community decreases the amount of time and resources needed to utilize 
the greenspace (Mears et al., 2020). Relatedly, communities that have

points, it has predominately focused on the entire constellation of 
greenspace that exists within a city, metropolitan area, or region. There 
exists a lack of distinction between local and regional greenspace visi­

greenspace or opt to travel to greenspace further away. 
To better understand the extent to which characteristics of green­
space drive communities to utilize local greenspace, we evaluate the role 
of greenspace access, availability, and quality on greenspace use. Spe­
cifically, we evaluate local greenspace visitation patterns to determine 
what factors promote local greenspace use and how those factors change 
with respect to visits to all greenspace in an urban area. We focus on two 
quality metrics of greenspace as the primary drivers for use, the overall 
vegetation health and the presence of a water feature, while controlling 
for access and availability measures. Distinguishing between the effects 
of greenspace and bluespace on visitation patterns becomes increasingly 
salient in the context of hot and arid urban environments, such as 
Phoenix, Arizona, where both vegetation and water features are scarce 
but can serve as important mitigators of urban heat effects. Our research, 
therefore, seeks to understand what the critical drivers of local green­
space visits are in the context of a hot and arid urban city. 
1.1. Greenspace access 
Whether communities of different socioeconomic make-up have 
adequate access to greenspace and its benefits is a question of ongoing 
inquiry. Access is generally considered as the ease with which an indi­
vidual can interact with greenspace, measured in terms of distance and 
overall availability (Comber et al., 2008). Under certain conceptuali­
zations of access, some high-minority communities do have more access 
to greenspace when compared to their White counterparts (Boone et al., 
2009). However, a large body of work suggests that access to greenspace 
is lower in low-income and high-minority communities (Chen et al., 
2020). These findings hold at the regional and national-level scales 
within the United States (Klompmaker et al., 2023) and in other coun­
tries (EEA, 2022). 
Access to greenspace has been predominantly based on measures of 
geographic proximity, likely reflecting the definitions suggested by 
authoritative bodies such as the World Health Organization (WHO, 
2017). Using Euclidean or network distance to measure the distance 
from a community to the nearest greenspace is the de facto choice for 
estimating access (Chen et al., 2020; Comber et al., 2008) although some 
have utilized more nuanced approaches using gravity-based models that 
consider both the supply (i.e., the amount of greenspace available) and 
the demand side (i.e., the number of people trying to access greenspace) 
of access. Still, there is almost always some consideration of distance as 
the primary factor of greenspace accessibility (He and Li, 2022) – 
whether that be Euclidean distance (Chen et al., 2020), network distance 
as a walkability score (Juul and Nordbø, 2023), or something else. In 
short, distance to greenspace is often considered a key determinant for 
researchers examining whether a community can take advantage of the 
benefits that greenspace offers. Indeed, when asked about their prefer­
ences for greenspace and its use, surveyed individuals often mention 
proximity as a primary driver (or barrier) of greenspace use (del Saz 
Salazar and García Men´endez, 2007). Therefore, it is important to note 
that among the research that investigates greenspace access, there is an 
assumption (either implicit or explicit) that the closer a community is to 
available greenspace, the more they will use it. 
1.2. Greenspace availability 
There are ongoing questions of whether distance alone is the primary 
driver of greenspace use (Jones et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2023). Although 
related, there is an important conceptual difference between greenspace 
access and greenspace availability that is not always made. Greenspace 
availability can be thought of as the amount of greenspace located 
within a given distance of a community. In this sense, researchers 
focused on availability operate under the assumption that more green­
space (in terms of area or count) would allow for more individuals to 
utilize the greenspace (Rey Gozalo et al., 2019), and therefore it is more 
available for the community. Availability measures can be as simple as 
asking how many greenspaces exist within a given community or 
whether some form of greenspace is within a certain distance. Others 
have taken the availability measure a step further with simultaneous 
consideration of greenspace area. Schipperijn et al. (2010) consider area 
and location as indicators of greenspace availability, which provides 
important nuance for their questions of community engagement with 
the greenspace. They found that while most of the study participants 
(62 %) reported some form of greenspace existing within 300 m of their 
residence, only 41 % had greenspace of at least 1 hectare within 300 m 
while less than 20 % had a greenspace greater than 5 ha within 300 m. 
More importantly, their results indicate that most of the participants do 
not engage with their closest greenspace and suggest that greenspace 
area may be driving this behavior (people seek larger greenspace more 
than close greenspace). This same logic of greenspace availability has 
also been applied when measuring the quantity of greenspace (the 
number of greenspace locations) that a given community can reach 
within a short walk or drive (You, 2016). 
1.3. Greenspace use 
Access and availability of greenspace may not guarantee its use 
(Jarvis et al., 2020). Indeed, the relationship between access and use is 
more complicated than is often assumed and driven by a variety of 
contextual factors associated with the greenspace (Ahn et al., 2020; 
Corley et al., 2018). For example, Cronin-de-Chavez et al. (2019) iden­
tified multiple barriers to greenspace use that went beyond that of total 
area, transportation, or distance. Specifically, they noted that the 
availability of amenities, access to natural environments, the existence 
of recreational activities, and potential for social interactions were 
major determinants of whether a family would use a greenspace. Other 
findings have echoed this result, suggesting that the primary drivers for 
use include suitable pathways, restroom facilities, playgrounds, and 
“pleasant green areas” (Maruthaveeran, 2010). These studies suggest 
that although a greenspace may be accessible, whether the greenspace is 
used also depends on characteristics of the greenspace that attract or 
promote use. The features of a greenspace and its overall attractiveness 
have previously been defined as the quality of the greenspace (Corley 
et al., 2018; Ahn et al., 2020). 
1.4. Greenspace quality: vegetation and water features 
The quality of greenspace is multifaceted and can be subjective 
(Zhang et al., 2017). Some individuals may place a strong emphasis on 
park amenities when thinking about quality while others may judge 
quality by the level of safety and cleanliness of a greenspace (Stessens 
et al., 2020). Likewise, others may consider both along with factors such 
as smell, maintenance, and overall comfort. Measurement of park 
quality often involves surveys or observational field exercises to capture 
the full breadth of features that contribute to a greenspace’s intrinsic 
quality. While better for capturing a variety of qualitative factors, this 
type of data collection limits the scope of a study to a smaller region, a 
subset of available greenspace, or a subset of users (Brindley et al., 2019; 
Zhang et al., 2017). To alleviate some of the limitations that data 
collection can introduce, scholars often use proxies for quality that are

more widely available and correlate with other variables that contribute 
to greenspace quality and encourage its use (Yang et al., 2021). 
Among the features that determine the quality of greenspace, vege­
tation is a key factor that plays a particularly important role when it 
comes to use (Wang et al., 2019). Indeed, people visit nature because it 
provides opportunities to encounter, experience, and enjoy plants, ani­
mals, and natural landscapes (Cronin-de-Chavez et al., 2019), all of 
which have been shown to have positive mental and physical benefits. 
More importantly, recent studies emphasize the importance of the 
naturalness of a greenspace on its perceived quality (Fongar et al., 
2019), which is often measured with respect to the biodiversity, species 
richness, and abundance of vegetation within the greenspace (Shuvo 
et al., 2021). Proxies for vegetation health and quality generally rely on 
metrics generated using remotely sensed data such as the Normalized 
Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) (Wang et al., 2019; S. Zhang and 
Zhou, 2018) or through multi-item measures gathered by surveys of 
park users (Zhang et al., 2017). In these studies, higher greenspace 
quality (i.e., greater greenness) is often associated with more greenspace 
use (Fongar et al., 2019). 
Urban water-related elements also play a significant role in 
providing mental health benefits which may reflect the quality of the 
greenspace (Smith et al., 2021; V¨olker and Kistemann, 2015). Specif­
ically, urban bluespace promotes mental relaxation through passive 
activities such as sitting and hearing the sound of water (Vierikko et al., 
2020; V¨olker and Kistemann, 2015). It is more expensive to maintain 
and therefore can be a sign of higher investment in the area which would 
correlate with better quality overall. According to the findings from 
Vierikko et al., (2020), bluespace is also associated with more green­
space visitation. Specifically, water features such as fountains and arti­
ficial lakes within greenspace showed a strongly positive correlation 
with the satisfaction of urban residents and greenspace visitors. Addi­
tionally, a survey of park users in Europe, found that the presence of 
water was one of the more desirable qualities of a greenspace (Bijker and 
Sijtsma, 2017). 
1.5. Greenspace use and visitation 
Determining whether greenspace access, availability, and quality are 
associated with use requires a measure of visitation. Previous studies 
have often relied on surveys for this purpose. For instance, Cohen et al., 
(2010) collected survey information from nearly 4300 users in southern 
California to explain the drivers for park use. Others have investigated 
use through more intimate data collection strategies, such as interviews 
with park users, to gather more nuanced information on both the bar­
riers and facilitators of use (Cronin-de-Chavez et al., 2019). With 
increased access to big data from a variety of social media sites (e.g., 
Twitter) as well as other forms of mobile data, georeferenced informa­
tion from cell phones is gaining traction as a viable indicator of green­
space use. By utilizing cell phone location data, researchers quantified 
the distance between individuals and greenspace locations over a given 
period of time (Song et al., 2018) or specifically counted the number of 
times individuals visited a greenspace over a given time period (Kim 
et al., 2023; Song et al., 2022). 
While most studies have looked at total greenspace visitation pat­
terns, they do little to distinguish between drivers of local visitation as it 
relates to visit patterns to all greenspace within an urban area. For 
example, access and availability studies generally focus on regional 
greenspace locations and factors such as proximity to evaluate differ­
ences in visitation (Jones et al., 2009), while studies that focus on 
greenspace quality are generally broader in geographic scope and show 
a willingness by visitors to travel to greenspace that is of higher 
perceived quality (Bijker and Sijtsma, 2017). Understanding how local 
park visitation patterns vary in the context of an individual’s overall 
greenspace visits can provide a unique lens from which to make de­
cisions surrounding how to facilitate more greenspace exposure. For 
example, when Vierikko and Colleagues (2020) interviewed 1474 
visitors across 33 parks in four European cities, they found that moti­
vation to visit greenspace was strongly related to high structural di­
versity of the greenspace. That is, variation in the park’s features (i.e., its 
qualities) made it more desirable to visit regardless of distance. On the 
other hand, the same study also found that visitors to parks with fewer 
qualities did so primarily because of their (closer) proximity. 
Thus, it is still an open question whether being close to greenspace is 
enough to encourage visitation, or whether greenspace needs to also 
include certain qualities for it to be fully utilized. The objective of this 
work is to examine the quality of the park as measured by its greenness 
(vegetation abundance and health) and blueness (the presence of water) 
to deepen our understanding of what promotes local greenspace visita­
tion and what discourages it. This research therefore fills a gap by 
looking at both local park visitation patterns on their own and in the 
context of visits to all available parks in an urban area to build a more 
complete picture of greenspace visitation patterns. 
1.6. Hypotheses 
The quality of a greenspace defined by its environmental charac­
teristics (i.e., vegetation and water features) matters. Recent analyses 
note that “pleasant green areas” and “natural environments” are key 
promoters of use (Cronin-de-Chavez et al., 2019), while others find that 
the greenness level of a park in combination with other characteristics 
such as water (bluespace), is associated with higher visitation (Zhang 
and Zhou, 2018). We expect that high-quality local greenspace 
measured by vegetation health and the presence of bluespace will have a 
positive effect (increase) on local visitation, all else equal. 
H1.
: As the quality of local greenspace increases, visitation from the 
community surrounding that greenspace also increases, all else equal. 
H1a.
: Census Block Groups that have local parks with higher greenness 
values will have higher visitation to their local parks. 
H1b.
: Census Block Groups with local park bluespace will have higher 
visitation to their local parks. 
It follows that if proximity to local greenspace increases visitation 
frequency, the share of visits to other parks in the region by that same 
community will decrease, especially if they have access to high-quality 
local parks. Therefore, we expect visits to local greenspace to increase 
relative to total greenspace visits, especially when the quality of the 
local greenspace is high. This leads to our second hypothesis: 
H2.
: As the quality of local greenspace (greenness or bluespace) increases, 
the share of visits that Census Block Group residents make to parks outside the 
local area with respect to visits to all parks in the metro region will decrease, 
all else equal. 
2. Methods and data 
2.1. Study area 
We investigate the distribution of parks as a form of local greenspace 
in an area of the United States that is particularly susceptible to the 
effects of climate change and where greenspace plays an important 
mitigating role: Phoenix, Arizona. We constrained our study to focus on 
parks located within the geographical scope of the Phoenix MSA and to 
Census Block Groups (CBGs) that make up the Phoenix-Mesa urban area 
(Fig. 1). To define parks, we followed Trust for Public Land’s (TPL) 
criteria, encompassing publicly accessible open spaces, including pub­
licly owned local, state, and national parks, trails, and open spaces (TPL, 
2023). We use the geographic boundary file from TPL to define park 
locations across the study area.

We leverage recent data on human mobility to measure visitation 
patterns to parks in the study area. We obtained park visitation data 
from Safegraph, a data company known for providing anonymized and 
aggregated location data and offering insights into visitation patterns 
and visitation trends to various places of interest (POIs). Specifically, we 
used Safegraph’s monthly patterns dataset, which records visitor flow 
between CBGs as origins and POIs as destinations (Safegraph, 2022). 
Safegraph data was initially marketed as a customer segmentation tool 
to help understand consumer spending patterns, however, it has been 
growing in use for academic research focused on human mobility 
(Haraguchi et al., 2022). Although tests of validity and representative­
ness of the Safegraph data as a proxy for place visitation are beyond the 
scope of this work, it has been previously documented in a number of 
studies (see Parolin and Lee, (2021) and Yabe et al., (2021) for 
examples). 
We pulled Safegraph visit data from the months between January 1 
and December 31, 2019 (before COVID-19 shutdowns). Prior to calcu­

each CBG i from which a resident visits a park. We account for multiple 
visits from a CBG to a park from a single park visitor by multiplying by 
the visit-to-visitor ratio (Eq. 1).

The Safegraph data provided us with the normalized visit that each 
CBG made to parks recorded in the Safegraph dataset. Next, using the 
geographic boundary file of each park within the TPL data set and the 
centroid of each CBG (Fig. 1), we performed a proximity analysis around 
each CBG to define the local park(s) associated with each CBG. For this 
study, we define local park as those where any part of the park boundary 
is located within a 1 km radius of the CBG’s centroid. We use a 1 km 
radius to capture the parks that someone living within each CBG could 
reasonably reach within a 10-minute walk. While the threshold for how 
far someone would walk to get to a park varies, previous studies on park 
accessibility often use the 10-minute cutoff for the measurement of 
equitable access (Kim et al., 2023; Koohsari et al., 2013; Nesbitt et al., 
2019). Individuals who live within a 10-minute walk of a park are 
associated with more use of that park (Toftager et al., 2011). For each 
park within the 1 km radius of the CBG, we added an indicator to 
establish whether the CBG-park pair was local or not. This allowed us to 
determine the total number of visits made to local parks as well as the 
total number of visits made to all other parks in the study region from 
wach CBG. Where the latter is concerned, we define the total number of 
visits to all parks (inclusive of local parks) as the metro park visit count 
(Eq. 2). When determining which parks qualify as local for each CBG we 
also recorded the exact distance between each CBG centroid and the 
nearest park (whether local or not) and included this as an additional 
measure in the dataset.

dividing it by the CBG population. The resulting value was right skewed 
with the majority of CBGs visiting parks infrequently while a small 
subset of CBGs visited parks at a much higher frequency. Prior to

It is important to note that not all parks within the TPL dataset are 
represented within the Safegraph POI dataset. When constructing the 
dependent variables, we therefore excluded CBGs that were either 
missing local parks or those with local parks missing visitation data in 
the Safegraph dataset. We did this for two reasons. First, our primary 
interest was in how park quality impacts local park visits. If a given 
CBG/community does not have a local park, then we can’t say with any 
certainty whether they would visit a local park given a certain level of 
park quality. Second, Safegraph’s visit data is somewhat limited by the 
POIs that they track visitation to. We opted not to make any assumptions 
about visits (e.g., by imputing the data) to local parks that did not have 
any Safegraph visitation data. In recognition that CBGs missing local 
parks may have an impact on the visitation metrics to local parks from 
each CBG, we did include a control variable describing the percentage of 
local parks that were missing for each CBG. In other words, following the 
identification of the number of local parks associated with each CBG 
based on TPL data, we proceeded to compare this list of local parks with 
those featured in the Safegraph visitation data. This enabled us to 
calculate the degree of overlap in local parks present in both datasets for 
the CBGs. After excluding CBGs that did not have any local parks or 
visitation data in the SafeGraph dataset, the final dataset included 1637 
CBGs (about 77 % of CBG’s in the study area). 
2.3. Independent variables 
Our primary interest is in the effect of park quality on local park 
visitation as measured by the vegetation and water features. To measure 
the vegetation of parks, we utilized the Normalized Difference Vegeta­
tion Index (NDVI) which quantifies the vegetation abundance and health 
for each park within the TPL dataset (Stuhlmacher et al., 2022; Tucker, 
1979). Remotely sensed data of the study site was acquired using Google 
Earth Engine (GEE), a cloud-based platform for multi-scale geospatial 
analysis (Gorelick et al., 2017). GEE serves as both a mechanism for data 
access and data analysis facilitated using JavaScript or Python API’s. For 
our analysis, we utilized GEE to obtain Sentinel-2 imagery from 2019 
which was first filtered to remove images with greater than 20 % cloud 
cover. Next, a cloud mask was applied pixel by pixel using QA (Quality 
Assessment) band values. Park NDVI was calculated from the resulting 
cloud-free composite image. We calculated the average NDVI value for 
each park using the bounds of the TPL park polygon data file (.shp). For 
CBGs with multiple local parks, we calculated the average NDVI value of 
the local parks and assigned that to the CBG as its measure using the 
pair-wise CBG-park table. 
When calculating the NDVI we considered two different time periods 
to account for the seasonality of vegetation cover in the Phoenix region. 
Our first NDVI calculation was based on the average NDVI values 
collected over the course of a full year. However, given the rather 
oppressive temperatures and lack of rainfall that Phoenix experiences 
during the summer months, NDVI can be significantly lower during this 
time of year. This would be especially true for parks that aren’t irrigated 
and may be considered as lower quality. To reduce the bias that seasonal 
temperatures may have on park NDVI, we also considered a second 
NDVI measure based on the 6-month period spanning October – April 
when temperatures are more comfortable, and parks are less likely to be 
drought-stricken (Appendix Fig. 1). We evaluated the annual NDVI 
values for our primary analysis but reported the results of the 6-month 
analysis in the Supplemental Info (SI Tables 2 and 3). To determine 
whether parks contained a water feature we used the 2019 National 
Land Cover Data Set’s open water class (Dewitz & USGS, 2021) to 
calculate the proportion of each park polygon covered by open water 
pixels (Table 2). For the statistical analysis this variable was simplified 
to a binary presence/absence. 
Fig. 2 shows the distribution of local park NDVI values associated 
with each CBG, along with the location of block groups containing 
bluespace. NDVI values range from −1–1. Negative values and those 
closer to 0 indicate very sparse vegetation or vegetation that could be 
unhealthy. Values closer to 1 indicate healthier and more abundant 
vegetation. Therefore, darker green colors in Fig. 2 indicate areas where 
the local park NDVI values are higher (healthier and more abundant 
vegetation) while lighter green areas indicate lower NDVI values (less 
abundant vegetation and/or not as healthy). Blue circles show which 
CBGs have bluespace in their local parks. 
In addition to the park’s NDVI values and presence of water, we also 
included several other control variables related to park characteristics. 
We included controls for distance to the nearest local park as a measure 
related to park access by each CBG using the Euclidean distance to the 
nearest boundary of the local parks from the CBG shape centroid. For 
CBGs with more than one local park, we only recorded the shortest 
distance. Since some local parks may be larger and designed to serve 
users outside the region (e.g., national parks), we added together the 
acreage of a CBGs local parks to control for the total local park areas 
available. Finally, we recorded the total number of local parks available 
to each CBG (within the 1 km buffer).

2.4. Statistical analysis 
We focused on identifying the factors associated with park visitation 
patterns and their influence on whether communities visit their local 
park, with special attention paid to park characteristics that reflect 
quality, namely, the abundance and health of vegetation and the pres­
ence of water within the park. Prior to running a full statistical analysis, 
we performed several tests for spatial autocorrelation to determine 
whether spatial effects would need to be accounted for in our modeling. 
The Moran’s I test resulted in a value of.241 and was significant (p<.01), 
indicating the data, and more specifically our dependent variable, was 
spatially clustered. Thus, a conventional OLS regression model was not 
appropriate. Operating under the assumption that an increase in local 
park quality (either greenness or water features) would increase local 
park visits, while an increase in local park quality would decrease visits 
to parks outside of the local area, we applied a spatial autoregressive 
(SAR) model using a spatially lagged dependent variable with a first- 
order queen contiguity weights matrix to evaluate the relationship be­
tween our dependent and independent variables of interest. The model 
was implemented in Python using the spreg analysis library. The same 
SAR model was used to assess the relationship between park charac­
teristics and visitation patterns for both dependent variables. 
3. Results 
The spatial distribution of our two dependent variables, per capita 
local park visits and metro park visit ratio, is plotted in Fig. 3. To reit­
erate, the local park visits are those made to parks within 1 km of the 
CBG centroid while the metro park visit ratio (Eq. 2) reflects the number 
of local park visits out of all visits to metro parks made by individuals in 
a CBG. The distribution of the values is somewhat opposite one another. 
Areas with high per capita local park visits (Fig. 3A) tend to have lower 
values of the metro park visit ratio (Fig. 3B), suggesting that CBGs with 
higher rates of local park visits will visit parks outside of this local area 
at a lower rate. Generally speaking, per capita local park visits were 
higher in the central and north-central CBGs of Phoenix as well as in 
north Mesa and parts of Glendale (west Phoenix). The spatial distribu­
tion of the metro park visit ratio values was more heterogeneous with 
high values spread throughout central Phoenix, in portions of east and 
west Mesa, and in North Scottsdale. Per capita visits across all CBGs 
tended to be lower (mean of 2.5), with only a few CBGs having signifi­
cantly higher per capita local visits in east Mesa and in portions of 
central Phoenix.

For models 1 and 2, per capita local park visits appear to be driven by 
access and availability which is consistent with previous studies. Spe­
cifically, the minimum distance to the nearest local parks plays an 
influential role, with per capita visits significantly increasing as the 
distance to the nearest local park decreases. The number of local parks is 
a positive and significant factor in local park visitation, indicating that 
communities with a higher number of local parks will have a higher 
local park per capita visitation rate on average. The amount of park 
space is also a key factor. The positive and significant relationship 
suggests that communities with larger local parks visit them more 
frequently. 
The NDVI variable measuring vegetation health and abundance is 
only significant in Model 1 when we do not account for the presence of 
water. Moreover, the relationship between NDVI and park visitation is in 
an unexpected direction but only to a small degree. In Model 1, we find 
that as the NDVI of the local park increases, local park visitation de­
creases. The directionality of this relationship persists when we control 
for the presence of water (Model 2), however, the relationship between 
NDVI and park visitation becomes statistically insignificant. Water 
presence does have a positive and significant effect on local park visi­
tation, indicating that the number of local visits increases significantly 
when a local park has a water feature. 
There are several characteristics of the communities that also appear 
to influence the frequency of local park visits. Specifically, we find a 
positive and significant relationship between local park visits and in­
come, suggesting that wealthier communities visit local parks more 
frequently, all else equal. We also find that communities with higher 
proportions of Hispanic residents visit local parks more frequently. The 
age of the neighborhood and the median age of the community members 
are not significantly related to local park visitation, nor is the population 
density of the community.

4. Discussion 
Planning guidelines for greenspace provision are largely focused on 
equitable access and availability of greenspace (EEA, 2022; WHO, 2017). 
Solutions to inequitable greenspace distribution are primarily focused 
on integrating more local greenspace into communities with minimal 
access and availability. However, irrespective of access and availability, 
local greenspace use may differ depending on the neighborhoods’ de­
mographic characteristics (e.g., Kim et al., 2023) or the local parks’ 
features. Although the park may be accessible from a purely geographic 
proximity perspective, the community use of the green space could be 
limited if it does not provide the features and qualities that the would-be 
park users look for in greenspace (Knapp et al., 2019). As a result, 
community members will either not visit local greenspace, or seek out 
greenspace that is further away (and at less frequent intervals). The 
result is less exposure to local greenspace with fewer opportunities for 
the social and health benefits they provide. In this paper, we evaluated 
the visitation patterns of parks in a large metropolitan region with 
respect to two important drivers of park use (greenness and the presence 
of bluespace) to deepen our understanding of how and why greenspace 
is visited. There are several important contributions of this work. 
We leveraged a temporally and spatially expansive dataset 
describing patterns of human movement. This is an important departure 
from much of the greenspace use literature that can often be limited by 
data collection strategies that rely on surveys or interviews. Although 
these survey or interview data can provide important context, they are 
limited in spatial scope, participants, and the number of parks consid­
ered. To get around that, we use mobility data to track the normalized 
number of visits made to a major subset of parks across the entire 
Phoenix-Mesa metropolitan area. We tracked these visits for every 
Census Block Group in the region. After controlling for several important 
confounding variables related to park accessibility (area, distance, and 
number), we found that vegetation health and abundance (NDVI) were 
associated with a significant decrease in local park visitation. This result 
supports a growing body of work that suggests that vegetation quality is 
less of a reason to visit a park when compared to other characteristics

A B S T R A C T
Urban soundscapes indicate biodiversity and environmental conditions, including urban stressors. Although 
relationships among soundscapes, biodiversity, and mental health effects exist, better quantification is needed to 
inform biodiversity-based health interventions. We present the pilot phase of the CitySoundscapes project, a 
transdisciplinary effort to develop methods for surveying biodiversity and human wellbeing associated with 
urban soundscapes. This approach involved citizens and practitioners across conservation, planning and health 
sectors as well as various urban contexts. In this initial one-year phase of our research, we investigated how 
soundscape characteristics relate to green space structural complexity, how to measure biodiversity using 
acoustic monitoring (here, vocal bird species), and people’s acoustic perceptions. We also piloted participatory 
approaches to identify where there are places of high biodiversity, high acoustic comfort and perceived resto­
ration. Our goal was not to deliver exhaustive ecological results, but to test and combine methods from ecology, 
environmental psychology, and urban planning for feasibility and complementarity. We present these methods 
and an approach to assess relationships among sound, biodiversity and wellbeing in urban spaces of varying 
vegetation structure. This case study provides a perspective on how green spaces and their structural features 
could relate to soundscapes and acoustic comfort and restoration, as well as how green spaces offer habitat for a 
diversity of sound-producing organisms. This methodological and conceptual contribution offers the groundwork 
for ecological and inter- and transdisciplinary research. The preliminary insights can inform ecological under­
standing and practical strategies for healthier, more biodiverse cities.
Introduction
A ‘soundscape’ can be defined as the entire collection of sounds 
produced by humans (anthrophonic sounds), by the living environment 
(biophonic sounds) and by the physical environment (geophonic 
sounds) (Krause, 2013). In cities, a milieu of various audible stimuli 
come together in the soundscape to influence the lived experience of 
urban residents and human health in relation to the built environment,

Interdisciplinary perspectives on soundscapes
The field of ecoacoustics investigates the (ecological) role of sounds 
from both natural and human sources in ecosystems, in which sound can 
be both a subject of study to understand ecological processes as well as a 
tool for monitoring biodiversity, assessing habitat characteristics, and 
determining the effects of human activity on the environment (Farina, 
2018). From an urban ecological perspective, natural soundscapes 
(biophonic and geophonic sounds) indicate trees, wind, and water, as 
well as sound-producing organisms (e.g., birds, bats, grasshoppers, 
frogs) that rely on sound to communicate and survey their environ­
ments. The biophonic component of soundscapes can be used to assess 
which species are present within a space and to identify the urban green 
space structures and also what urban green space structures may be 
associated with species presence. For example, dense understory vege­
tation relates to a high diversity of bird sounds whilst also absorbing 
anthropogenic noise (Uebel et al., 2021). As cities densify and lose 
vegetation, soundscapes become less audibly diverse and less biophonic 
due to increasing anthropogenic sounds (Hasegawa et al., 2022; Joo 
et al., 2011), likely reflecting the decline of sound-producing organisms 
and the homogenization of urban animal communities. Vegetated spaces 
can support plant and animal life in cities that are often characterized by 
imperviousness and green space loss (Perillo et al., 2017). Vegetation 
structural complexity — "the degree of heterogeneity in biomass dis­
tribution in three-dimensional space" (Ehbrecht et al., 2021) — can be 
an important predictor of urban biodiversity (Beninde et al., 2015).
In the cognitive-psychological dimension, the soundscape concept 
focuses on how an individual person perceives and experiences the 
acoustic environment (Kang et al., 2018; Moebus et al., 2020). The in­
ternational standard definition ((ISO 12913-1:2014)) defines a sound­
scape as an acoustic environment that is perceived, experienced and/or 
understood by one or more people in the respective spatial context. 
While this standard provides a widely applied operational framework, 
soundscape research more broadly is embedded in multiple epistemo­
logical traditions, ranging from cognitive psychology and (eco)acoustics 
to public health and urban planning. From this cognitive psychology 
perspective, a soundscape exists specifically in the moment it is 
perceived by an individual, where preferences for different sounds are 
variable (Moebus et al., 2020). From a public health perspective, some 
sounds (traffic, construction) can induce stress and anxiety, and are 
considered “noise” with both temporary and permanent negative im­
pacts on human health and associated social inequities ( Goines et al., 
2007). Residents may seek out spaces that are quieter with lower levels 
of noise pollution from traffic or construction. On the other hand, some 
sounds (birds twitching, trees in the breeze, flowing water) can facilitate 
calmness, stress recovery, nature connection, perceived restoration 
(Berto et al., 2010; Francomano et al., 2022) and in turn enhanced 
cognitive performance (Buxton et al., 2021). Thus, soundscapes are an 
important dimension of urban landscapes that relate to perceptions of 
nature and human health outcomes in cities (Radicchi et al., 2021).
More recent conceptualizations of soundscapes in the humanities, 
arts and social sciences consider the agency of more-than-human species 
in the active shaping of shared acoustic worlds, and the responsibility 
and ethical obligations of humans in the planning of those environments 
(Ruiz Arana, 2021, 2024). This transdisciplinary perspective and 
approach to soundscapes aims to de-center soundscape approaches that 
narrowly focus on human interest and acknowledge that sounds can be 
meaningful and have effects in a multiplicity of ways across species and 
individuals (Ogden et al., 2013; Tsing, 2015). As Jordan Lacey proposes 
in their reflection of ‘the posthuman listener’, “a soundscape is 
more-than-human insofar as it always exceeds any one person’s appre­
hension, while at the same time being differentiated by each sensing 
body into a diversity of listening experiences … Accordingly, sensory 
attentiveness – ‘the cultivation of skills for both paying attention to 
others and meaningfully responding’ (Van Dooren et al., 2016) – is 
positioned here as the ethical and practical foundation for understand­
ing soundscapes” (Lacey, 2024: 274).
Interdisciplinary research on soundscapes
The various disciplinary work on soundscapes has resulted in a 
diverse body of basic and applied research (Pijanowski, 2024). How­
ever, there is still relatively little integrative, inter– and trans­
disciplinary research on soundscapes. Green and blue space structure 
and complexity likely influence how auditory sensory experiences relate 
measures of human wellbeing to measured sounds of biodiversity 
(Francomano et al., 2022) and thus more biophonic soundscapes (Fisher 
et al., 2021). But very few empirical studies have linked health metrics 
related to human-perceived sound of environmental characteristics (e. 
g., green space complexity, landscape imperviousness) with biodiversity 
metrics related to sound (Buxton et al., 2021; Markevych et al., 2017). 
The analysis and systematic evaluation of soundscapes from a human 
health perspective (e.g., ISO 12,913 series of standards (Sun et al., 
2019)) is also still in its infancy. The Perceived Restorativeness Sound­
scape Scale (PRSS) is one of the few attempts to evaluate the psycho­
logical recovery of people through a soundscape (Payne & Guastavino, 
2018). Analysis and evaluation are challenging as soundscapes cannot 
be described by numerical metrics alone but depend largely on human 
perception. Furthermore, while few studies have found correlations 
between acoustic indices and perceived biodiversity (bird richness) 
(Rozario et al., 2025), metrics that are used in ecoacoustics to quanti­
tatively describe soundscapes may not necessarily reflect human per­
ceptions.

very differently by the listener (e.g. 50 dB sound of bird call vs. machine 
noise) (Jennings & Cain, 2013). Participatory methods and tools with 
people such as citizen science, crowdsourcing and interactive workshops 
are also important to assess and understand the influence of the auditory 
environment on the individual (Payne & Guastavino, 2018). Trans­
disciplinary approaches in research and praxis that integrate biodiver­
sity, ecoacoustics and public health are lacking, but are needed to 
measure and promote biodiversity, restorative experiences, and human 
wellbeing in urban landscape planning and management.
Several studies argue that solutions lie in integrating the concern for 
soundscapes and focusing on positive effects of soundscapes into the 
early stages of urban planning and green space design (Farina, 2014; 
Jaszczak et al., 2021; Lippold & Lawrence, 2019). Yet, most research 
continues to focus on traditional approaches to noise reduction, avoid­
ance, and abatement (Chen et al., 2022), rather than on soundscapes as 
the intersection of biodiversity conservation and human health. Never­
theless, the soundscape approach is gaining attention as an innovative 
method for urban planning with identifying, analyzing, and evaluating 
existing soundscapes (Haselhoff et al., 2022; Hosseini & Kowkabi, 2023; 
Jennings & Cain, 2013). Methods implemented include soundwalks, 
surveys, measurements, mixed reality, and participatory tools (Chen 
et al., 2022). For example, the ‘Sounding Brighton’ project, launched by 
the Noise Abatement Society in the UK aims to strengthen the consid­
eration of soundscapes in urban planning and to raise awareness among

the public, stakeholders and policy makers (Lavia et al., 2012). Some

parks with vibrant public spaces (Jia et al., 2020; Steele et al., 2019a, b). 
Despite such promising efforts, urban strategies for soundscape planning

measures that relate the topics of ecoacoustics, biodiversity and human 
wellbeing from a soundscape perspective.
Our research focuses on soundscapes as one quantifiable sensory 
aspect of urban landscapes to predict the relationship between biodi­
versity and human restoration in urban areas (Markevych et al., 2017). 
This relationship is relatively understudied from a combined research, 
praxis, and public engagement perspective (Fig. 1). This study presents 
workflows and methods that enable interdisciplinary research into the 
social-ecological aspects of urban soundscapes. Our questions are: (1) 
How are the characteristics of urban soundscapes related to urban green 
space and their proximal structural complexity, as well as to local 
biodiversity (here, the activity and richness of bird vocalisations)? (2) 
How are urban soundscapes related to human acoustic comfort and 
restoration? (3) Where are places with high biodiversity, acoustic 
comfort and restoration effects? And (4) How can such spaces be fairly 
promoted in urban planning and administrative processes? To answer 
these questions, we integrate methods from ecoacoustics and environ­
mental psychology, as well as urban planning and management with the 
goal to develop recommendations for city planners and policy makers on 
how to design green spaces that can promote both biodiversity and 
human wellbeing.
The purpose of this article is to present the initial phase in which we 
tested and integrated diverse methods including acoustic indices, spe­
cies detection, vegetation surveys, soundwalks, and community partic­
ipation to assess their feasibility and complementarities. We position 
this work as exploratory: while some of our research questions are 
addressed empirically with pilot data here, others are explored through 
preliminary insights and other studies. Together, these outcomes aim to 
provide a foundation for developing an interdisciplinary framework for 
future research. We demonstrate how we tested methods to characterize 
soundscapes, and to quantify the relationships among biodiversity in 
green spaces with a focus on birds, vegetation structural complexity, as 
well as a method to measure soundscape perceptions and restoration. In 
addition, we provide first suggestions for public engagement that inte­
grate biodiversity and wellbeing promotion to identify good practice 
examples that can inform tools to enhance the integration of biodiversity 
and wellbeing in urban planning and green space management. We 
reflect on our approaches and make recommendations for their 
improvement for future soundscape studies. With the rise of interest in 
social-ecological studies in basic and applied urban ecology, the purpose 
and strength of this manuscript is in the methods and concepts presented 
that we hope to be a valuable contribution to an interdisciplinary 
ecological audience.

Establishment of study sites
Munich has a long history of urban green space development 
including parks and gardens of various ages, sizes and structural char­
acteristics as well as human use (Landeshauptstadt München, 2020). To 
investigate such effects on biodiversity and wellbeing, we established 93 
research sites (44 research plots in 33 green spaces (Arzberger et al., 
2024), 14 city squares (Fairbairn et al., 2024), and 32 reference point 
locations in the built surroundings (‘REF’) within the central ring (inner 
city) of Munich as our study system (Fig. 2). The research plots represent 
a gradient of local vegetation structural complexity as well as urban 
landscape context and, except for the reference points, were located at 
least 300 m in distance between one another to reduce spatial auto­
correlation (Fontana et al., 2011).
Green space structural complexity, bird diversity and soundscapes
To collect information about vegetation structural complexity and 
diversity, soundscape characteristics and bird diversity, we piloted the 
combination of using mobile laser scanning (MLS) with passive acoustic 
monitoring in urban green spaces (parks) and city squares. The vege­
tation structure was recorded in each of the green space plots and city 
square plots using a hand-held MLS (ZEB HORIZON, GeoSLAM Ltd, UK; 
see Arzberger et al. (2024) for full description of methodology). Vege­
tation structural complexity was assessed within a 15 m radius around 
each recorder. We chose this plot size for MLS data collection due to the 
small size of the squares and because the MLS delivers high-resolution 
data only on a small scale and is not an appropriate method to cover 
larger spatial scales. Furthermore, we focused on smaller scale vegeta­
tion features here as previous studies on vocal bird diversity in Munich 
using similar acoustic methods showed the importance of local vegeta­
tion features within 100 m rather than the surrounding landscape (i.e., 
based on NDVI in larger buffers of up to 1 km; Fairbairn et al., 2024, 
2025b). Potential limitations around overall habitat complexity and 
landscape connectivity are discussed below, and subsequent phases of 
the project will incorporate larger spatial buffers and landscape 
composition and connectivity measures.
The raw data was processed using the manufacturer-specific soft­
ware GeoSLAM Connect (version 2.2.0) and the open-source software 
CloudCompare (CloudCompare, version v2.13.alpha, https://www. 
danielgm.net/cc/, France). The processed data was analyzed with the 
software R (version 4.2.6), using RStudio (version 2023.12.1) and the R- 
package lidR (Roussel et al., 2020). To characterize the vegetation 
structural complexity of each plot, we calculated ten MLS-metrics hy­
pothesized to predict soundscape characteristics as well as (bird) 
biodiversity (Table 1). With these vegetation structure metrics, several 
of which were correlated, we performed a principal component analysis 
(PCA). The vegetation metrics on the principal components (PCs) were 
computed to assess which vegetation metrics contributed the most to 
their respective PCs. Two principal components were calculated to 
represent vegetation volume and height (PC1) and vegetation vertical 
structural heterogeneity, from the low shrub layer to higher canopy 
layer (PC2). The scores of PC1 and PC2 were extracted for each research 
plot to be used as explanatory variables in our analysis.

We conducted continuous 24-h acoustic recordings within each of 
the research plots during two recording rounds of seven days each in all 
study sites including urban green spaces, city squares and reference 
points (Round 1: 11.08.2023 – 18.08.2023; Round 2: 24.08.2023 – 
30.08.2023 (168 h of recordings for each plot)). We recorded in August 
as part of our coordinated pilot phase; we acknowledge that bird vocal 
activity often peaks in spring and early summer, however we can still 
measure the vocal bird community for a relative assessment across 
sampled sites. Relating vegetation structure to acoustic indices in this 
dataset is therefore exploratory and provides information on bird 
vocalization and thus bird communities in the time we surveyed. We 
used passive acoustic monitoring devices from Frontier Lab (BAR & BAR 
LT) placed three to four meters above the ground on lamp posts or trees 
depending on available infrastructure and physical conditions. Re­
cordings were taken at a sample rate of 48 kHz, a bit depth of 16 and a 
gain of 40 dB (Fairbairn et al., 2024b). During preliminary analyses we 
identified that one site was dominated by running water in a nearby 
fountain; this site was removed from the analyses. At two sites, data 
storage was compromised, and no data could be retrieved. Analyses 
were based on 90 sites.
From the collected acoustic data, we derived four different acoustic 
indices to characterize the urban soundscapes (Fairbrass et al., 2017). 
This included the Acoustic Diversity Index (ADI), Acoustic Evenness 
Index (AEI), Bioacoustic Index (BI), and the Normalised Difference 
Soundscape Index (NDSI) (Table 2). Hourly means were calculated for 
all indices for the entire recording period (168 recording hours per site) 
using the seewave and soundecology packages in their default settings in R 
(Sueur et al., 2008; Villanueva-Rivera & Pijanowski, 2018). In addition, 
we subset the data to focus on daytime (diurnal) soundscapes where 
organisms of focus (vocal birds) are most dominant as well as when 
people are most likely to experience soundscapes (04:00 to 20:00).
We derived local songbird biodiversity using BirdNET, a deep arti­
ficial neural network that can identify vocal bird species by call in 3-s 
segments (Kahl et al., 2021). Bird vocal activity was analyzed using 
BirdNET Analyzer, with overlap 0, sensitivity 1, and i including week of 
the year and location for species filtering via BirdNET and eBird. We 
followed the settings for BirdNET suggested by Fairbairn et al. (2025a)
to produce the highest dataset resolution to validate results, keeping 
only detections with a confidence of 0.8 or above. BirdNET detections 
were validated by checking species detected ten times or fewer across all 
sites and those detected only one time per site were removed for being 
considered incidental and not resident to the site. We then calculated 
alpha diversity (vocal bird species richness), a vocal Shannon’s Diversity 
index (a standardized index that measures species diversity), and bird 
species vocal activity rate (VAR; P´erez-Granadados et al., 2019) for each 
site for each recording round. VAR was used for the calculation of vocal 
Shannon’s Diversity.

applied the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum 
tests as post hoc analyses, as not all variables were normally distributed.

Soundwalks to measure soundscape perceptions, acoustic comfort, and 
restoration
We carried out a feasibility study to test a method to conduct 
“soundwalks” to measure soundscape perceptions, acoustic comfort, 
mood and restoration. In environmental psychology and acoustic 
soundscape research, the soundwalk methodology is a research method 
for investigating environmental sounds, in which human participants 
stop along a defined route, consciously listen to the acoustic environ­
ment, and evaluate the soundscape according to different standardized 
question 
sets 
(Behrendt, 
2018). 
We 
conceptualized 
two 
German-language questionnaire variants based in the DIN ISO/TS 12, 
913–2 conceptual framework that links soundscape experience to 
human restoration for data collection.
The questionnaire had the following main components: (1) an ‘affect 
grid’ (9 × 9 square grid) to evaluate two different dimensions of mood 
states: horizontal dimension: pleasure or displeasure (from left to right; 
1–9) and vertical dimension: arousal versus sleepiness, where arousal 
refers how “activated” someone feels (from bottom to top; 1–9) (Russell 
et al., 1989); (2) standardized soundwalk questions following either 
variant (i) DIN ISO/TS std 12,913–2 (ISO/TS 12913-2, 2018) or variant 
(ii) “Perceived Restorativeness Soundscape Scale” (PRSS) (German-­
language translation) following Payne (2013); (3) a graphical query of 
ones’ relation to nature (Kleespies et al., 2021); (4) two questions on 
disturbing or perceived restorative ambient sound; and (5) sociodemo­
graphic questions. Part 2 asked how soundscapes and multisensory as­
pects are experienced including questions to be answered on a Likert 
scale, including: “There is a lot for me to explore in this sound environment”; 
“I find this sound environment fascinating”; “I like to spend time in sound 
environments like this one”; “The sounds come together to create a harmo­
nious sound environment”. The DIN ISO/TS 12,913–2 questionnaire 
measures different psychoacoustic parameters, like sharpness, tonality, 
roughness or fluctuation strength which can be correlate to the indi­
vidual perception and assessment of environmental noise sources, e.g. 
road traffic noise (ISO/TS 12913-2, 2018). Part 2 measures eight af­
fective attributes will be used to calculate a rating in the main di­
mensions of pleasantness and eventfulness. The second soundscape 
questionnaire was the German non-validated translation of the PRSS, 
which consists of 44 items, rated by a 9-point Likert Scale (0 = not at all; 
9 = completely). The scale is designed to assess perceptions of a 
soundscape’s potential to provide psychological restoration based on the 
attention restoration theory by Kaplan and Kaplan (1989).
For both questionnaires, different psychoacoustic indicators can be 
calculated that related to the restorative potential of environmental 
soundscapes. The survey was available either via a paper-based ques­
tionnaire or smartphone. We then conducted qualitative interviews after 
the soundwalk with volunteers to ask for their feedback to improve the 
quality of the soundwalks for the future. The pilot survey in the original 
German format as well English translation (note: the survey was only 
conducted in German) can be found in Appendix A, 1.1.
We tested the soundwalk design in three different green spaces in 
Munich (Fig. 2) over six soundwalks with 27 volunteer residents that 
were recruited using posters and flyers in local businesses, social 
network platforms, and networks of civil society organizations. The 
feasibility study enabled us to test tools (surveys, site selection, route 
design) and to study how residents perceive soundscapes. The three 
soundwalk study sites were selected from the same sites as 2.2. to 
represent different green space contexts of varying structural complexity 
and biodiversity (Fig. 2): Westend Park (lower structural complexity), 
Luitpoldpark (middle structural complexity), and Englisher Garten (high 
structural complexity). Along these routes, participants stopped for five 
minutes in three to four listening locations as well as a reference control 
area, such as subway stations that were the initial meeting point for the 
walks and completed the survey questions. Each of the listening loca­
tions were also respective data collection points for structural 
complexity data as well as acoustic monitoring. The routes ranged from 
1.6 to 3.3 km and were 60 to 90 min in duration.

Interactive and participatory approaches
In parallel, we piloted small-scale interactive and participatory ap­
proaches designed to engage residents with sound environments, to raise 
awareness of soundscapes, and to test the feasibility of integrating more 
interactive practices into soundscape research. Here we tested partici­
patory mapping methods, citizen science for acoustic data on bird song 
and interactive activities to qualitatively engage people in the moni­
toring, learning and shaping of their urban soundscapes. With these 
formats, we wanted to explore the contribution of biodiverse environ­
ments to urban soundscapes as experienced directly by people through 
their emotional and multisensory encounters with sounds and subse­
quent sound-based biodiversity. In addition, we sought to raise aware­
ness of people’s capacity and role in designing health-promoting 
soundscapes that support human wellbeing but also connect to the needs 
of other species. While not systematically evaluated in this phase, 
qualitative feedback indicated strong public interest, and we discuss 
these preliminary outcomes as inspiration for future, more structured 
interactive approaches below.
We piloted the methods in an “Urban Sound Lab,” a free walk-in 
workshop hosted at the BIOTOPIA Lab (Munich) on four separate days 
in 2023. Publicity was done via social media (Instagram), press releases, 
newsletters, and the museum’s event program, resulting in ~100 visi­
tors. The main aim of the workshop was to trial the suitability of the 
participatory formats from participant and scientist perspectives, using 
visitor feedback regarding the usability and exploratory observation of 
the process. The three interactive stations were offered: (1) participatory 
soundscape mapping using qualitative methods; (2) an immersive audio 
station; and (3) an introduction to citizen science digital tools to monitor 
and crowd source acoustic data on soundscapes.
With the participatory mapping exercise, we followed an analogous, 
multisensory oriented participatory cartographic approach (Fagerholm 
et al., 2021; Korpilo et al., 2023) by asking people to spatially locate, 
name and evaluate the visual and audible qualities that make up 
favorable and unfavorable acoustic environments in the city of Munich 
from their actual experiences. For this we used a physical map on which 
participants could mark places with a sticker and attach a written 
description as to how the specific place sounds to them as well as their 
emotional state when they are there. We analyzed these text responses 
using structuring qualitative content analysis (Mayring, 2014; Kuckartz, 
2014). This approach involves systematically coding responses into 
thematic categories that are either theory-driven (from prior literature) 
or emerge inductively from the material. Codes are then aggregated into 
categories, and their presence and frequency are assessed as indicators 
of subjective relevance. This method was chosen because it allows 
ecologically oriented data (place, sound type) to be meaningfully com­
bined with experiential and affective dimensions, producing both 
qualitative depth and semi-quantitative indicators that can inform 
planning and ecological research.
With the immersive audio station, we exposed people via head­
phones connected to a digital platform with a range of acoustic re­
cordings taken across Munich. Here we used two types of soundscapes 
that were presented to participants: (i) more ‘natural’ soundscapes 
(selected from our acoustic recording database for having relatively 
more biophonic sounds including bird song within green spaces in 
Munich (Fairbairn et al., 2024)); (ii) more ‘urban’ soundscapes (selected 
from our acoustic recording database for having relatively more 
anthrophonic sounds including traffic and construction within gray 
spaces in Munich Fairbairn et al., 2024). We then asked participants to 
record voice messages that qualitatively describe what they hear and 
how it makes them feel. Here the participants did not get any specifi­
cations, other than to record their feelings freely to learn about the 
descriptive words that people use to capture soundscapes and their 
sensations towards them. People were also allowed to listen to responses 
from other participants as a way of building a sense of the city as a 
shared soundscape that can be experienced very differently based on 
personal perceptions and experiences. The qualitative analysis of the 
audio recordings followed Saldana (2013). The mentioned words were 
classified into the categories “High stress”, “Moderate stress” and “No 
stress” and into “Positive”, “Neutral” and “Negative”. As a follow-up, we 
asked people to complete a survey including demographic information, 
an affect grid (as in 2.3) and some questions regarding the methodology 
of the station itself.
With the citizen science digital tools station, we offered an interac­
tive introduction to digital apps and platforms that people can use to 
collect acoustic data on soundscapes around the world. As an example, 
we used the citizen science Dawn Chorus Project (see: https://dawn-ch 
orus.org/). We introduced participants to the Dawn Chorus App and 
taught them how to document 60-s soundscape recordings, as well 
encouraged them to explore the Dawn Chorus soundmap – a virtual map 
of global citizen science recordings of early morning birdsong, with 
many examples from Munich to engage with the recordings made locally 
in Munich. Collectively, these formats provided different modes of 
listening to soundscapes with different emphasis on the aspects of 
technology and citizen science in soundscapes.

A B S T R A C T
Walking is an essential mode of mobility that enhances both physical and mental health and is a vital part of 
urban life. However, certain groups, particularly women, face obstacles in this regard. Despite the topic’s sig­
nificance, there is a scarcity of studies on women’s walking in Middle Eastern countries. This paper aims to 
address this gap by identifying the factors that affect women’s walking in Shiraz, Iran.
To collect data, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 73 women living in Shiraz, and the data were 
analyzed using reflective thematic analysis.
Six themes—health, socio-cultural, environmental, safety and security, weather, and functional—were iden­
tified. According to women, issues such as street harassment, unsafe flooring, hot weather, and unsuitable 
furniture are among the most serious barriers to walking in urban spaces. Additionally, women emphasized 
concerns like religious beliefs and family norms, which create invisible boundaries to their active presence in 
urban areas.
This study demonstrates that promoting walkability for women necessitates attention to the social, cultural, 
and symbolic aspects of urban spaces, in addition to their physical structure. By incorporating these factors into 
planning, urban planners can create strategies that improve women’s walking experiences and sense of belonging 
in cities.
1. Introduction
Walkability refers to the extent to which environmental facilities, 
such as parks and sidewalks, encourage people to walk and engage in 
other social and physical activities (Opuni et al., 2022). Walkability is 
vital for two reasons: it improves health by reducing chronic disease 
risks, dementia, stress, and sleep issues; and it promotes urban growth, 
enhances living conditions, and boosts neighborhood satisfaction 
(Herrmann-Lunecke et al., 2021; Opuni et al., 2022; Wang & Yang, 
2019). For this reason, the World Health Organization encourages 
walking, and research indicates that the risk of death decreases by 20 to 
30 % for sufficiently active individuals (Elzeni et al., 2022; Opuni et al., 
2022).
However, research indicates that the factors influencing women’s 
walking differ from those affecting men’s walking (Golan et al., 2019). 
Women often feel insecure while walking, cycling, or using public 
transportation because of their vulnerability while traveling. As a result, 
active travel for women may contribute to anxiety (Goel et al., 2023).
Research indicates that factors like homelessness, safety, street 
cleanliness, traffic, crossings, and sidewalk width can affect women’s 
walking behavior (Adeel et al., 2014; Golan et al., 2019; Herrmann- 
Lunecke et al., 2021). Women are more fearful of crime, which affects 
their walking patterns and behaviors. Nevertheless, women’s familiarity 
with urban spaces, the presence of others, and proximity to shops, 
schools, and parks can all influence their sense of security. Additionally, 
liveliness, disorder signs, control, and vegetation influence women’s 
perceived safety (Paydar et al., 2017).
This article engages in a critical discussion of gendered urban 
mobility, making a significant contribution to global knowledge by 
providing an in-depth qualitative analysis of women’s walking experi­
ences in a Middle Eastern context—a region often under-researched in 
mobility studies. While many studies on women’s walking have been 
conducted in Western countries, this study bridges a fundamental gap by 
examining how socio-cultural structures influence women’s walking.

2. Methods
Shiraz, the capital of Fars Province in southern Iran, attracts 
numerous visitors due to its diverse services, growing population, and 
numerous attractions. This leads to increased urban traffic and air 
pollution. The 2016 Shiraz Household Travel Survey shows that 50 % of 
residents use private vehicles, 12 % use public transportation, 28 % use 
taxis, and 10 % use other modes of transportation. Therefore, improving 
walkability has become a key priority for the city.
To examine the factors influencing women’s walking in Shiraz, semi- 
structured interviews were conducted from April to May 2024 with 73 
women living in the city. Written consent was obtained from all par­
ticipants before the interviews. Data collection continued until theo­
retical saturation was reached. The six-phase reflective thematic 
analysis, as outlined by Coyne et al. (2024), was used to analyze data. In 
phase one, interviews were conducted, transcribed into MaxQDA 24, 
and reviewed. Phase two involved systematic data coding, identifying 
patterns related to the research question. In phase three, codes were 
categorized, and themes emerged. Phase four involved reviewing the 
analytical structure and themes. In Phase 5, themes were identified. In 
the final phase, the report was written, incorporating participant quotes 
to deepen understanding.
3. Results
Table 1 presents the characteristics of the interviewees. Most were 
between 16 and 30 years old, held a bachelor’s degree, were single, and 
did not own a private car.
The results of thematic analysis indicate that the factors influencing 
women’s walking in urban areas include health, socio-cultural, envi­
ronmental, safety and security, weather, and functional aspects. Table 2
displays the frequency of related themes and subthemes. Environmental 
subthemes such as pavement, furniture, green space, continuity, and 
slope are frequently mentioned by the interviewees. Additionally, from a 
socio-cultural perspective, factors like religious beliefs, the lack of a 
walking culture, and family norms have influenced women’s walking 
habits. In terms of safety and security, issues such as street harassment, 
motorcycles entering sidewalks, and poor lighting in urban spaces have 
created many problems for women’s walking.

Fig. 1 illustrates the factors affecting women’s walking. The 
following addresses some of the most important points raised by women. 
Fear of street harassment is one of the most critical factors mentioned by 
women that can prevent them from walking. This is consistent with 
(Adeel et al., 2014; Paydar et al., 2017; Stark & Meschik, 2018). Women 
mentioned that street harassment includes teasing, hitting, staring, and 
physical harassment, especially on dark streets, pedestrian bridges with 
billboards, and under Grade-separated intersections. In recent years, the 
areas under intersections have become abandoned spaces used as 
parking lots during the day and sites of social unrest at night. Some 
women mentioned that they assess routes for harassment risk before 
walking alone and choose safer paths, even if it takes longer. Women’s 
fears in Shiraz stem more from urban neglect—like garbage and aban­
doned lands—than actual crime statistics. Therefore, safety encom­
passes both physical and psychological aspects.
The weather was one of the issues raised by many women, which is in 
accordance with (B¨ocker et al., 2016; Daniels & Park, 2024; Drapeau 
et al., 2024). In Shiraz, it is not possible to engage in outdoor activities 
during certain months of the year due to unfavorable weather condi­
tions, which impact the frequency of walking during those months. 
Considering existing clothing laws in society, women emphasized the 
need to create climatic comfort on sidewalks through urban design in­
terventions, especially during summer.
In recent years, car-oriented policies in Shiraz have resulted in land 
uses being dispersed throughout various parts of the city. Consequently, 
people have become highly dependent on private cars to fulfill their 
daily needs. Additionally, women mentioned that they prefer to use their 
private vehicles instead of walking or using public transportation 
because they feel unsafe in some parts of the city. One point that women 
emphasized is the necessity of developing walking infrastructure, which 
is in accordance with (Annear et al., 2024; Mirzaei et al., 2018; Nigg 
et al., 2024). Currently, there are several walkways in Shiraz, including 
Salamat (meaning “health”), Hafezieh, Chamran, and Mosala Nezhad. 
As these walkways have a family atmosphere, women are actively pre­
sent in these spaces. In recent years, some Iranian cities, such as Tehran, 
have established mother-and-child rooms that provide suitable spaces 
for mothers to rest while walking with their child or infant. In Shiraz, 
policies have been considered to create these rooms in the future.
Narrow sidewalks and construction operations on them were other 
factors mentioned by women that block their movement, especially 
when they were trying to cross the sidewalks with strollers or shopping 
carts, which is in accordance with (Boukrouh & Bouchair, 2024; Müller 
et al., 2024). Some construction projects in Shiraz take months to 
complete, making it difficult for people to use the sidewalks. What 
makes the situation worse is the motorcycles moving on sidewalks, 
especially in the city center, which endangers the safety of pedestrians. 
Women also noted that narrow sidewalks increase their chances of 
encountering street harassment.
According to women, access to public transportation stations also 
plays a role in the appeal of walking for women, which is in agreement 
with (Annear et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2020). In

Religious and cultural beliefs also influence women’s walking habits.

women walking alone might be judged.
5. Conclusion
The results showed that factors affecting women walking include 
environmental, safety and security, health, weather, functional, and 
socio-cultural factors. Our findings underscore the fundamental point 
that some urban spaces may seem accessible to everyone, but socially, 
they display signs that limit women’s presence. This shows that mobility 
is a symbolic issue shaped by sociocultural context. In other words, so­
cial, cultural, and religious norms create invisible boundaries for 
women’s presence in urban spaces. Therefore, women-friendly urban 
design must extend beyond physical infrastructure to encompass the 
intricate interplay between social, cultural, and religious factors, as well 
as women’s mobility. These findings draw our attention to dimensions of 
women’s mobility that are often overlooked in countries of the Global 
South, and especially the Middle East.
The findings of this study are not only relevant to Shiraz and Iranian 
cities, but they can also be applied to other cities worldwide, especially 
in the Middle East and the broader Global South, where social, cultural, 
and religious norms significantly influence women’s walking experi­
ences and active presence in urban spaces. To translate these findings 
into actions, Table 3 outlines strategies to improve women’s walking, 
considering physical and socio-cultural challenges identified from in­
terviewees’ experiences. These serve as guidance for cities like Shiraz.
Although this study focused on a single city using a qualitative 
approach, the findings can pave the way for future comparative research 
between different cities. Additionally, comparing the opinions of various 
age groups of women can offer insights into how generational differ­
ences influence walking behavior and show the changing nature of 
mobility during the life course.

A B S T R A C T
Currently, there is a lack of research exploring the role of green spaces in mitigating the impacts of heat and air 
pollution on running activities. Employing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) to 
compare water-friendly and non-water-friendly streets, we examined how green spaces promote running 
behavior through cooling effects and air purification, mitigating heat and air pollution. The results showed that 
running hotspots were primarily located near green spaces, bodies of water, and student-dense areas, while cold 
spots were found near government offices, commercial zones, and sparsely populated areas. Water-friendly 
streets exhibited significantly higher running intensity compared to non-water-friendly streets. The PLS-SEM 
results indicated that green spaces directly encouraged running behavior and indirectly promoted it by 
improving thermal conditions and air quality. Moreover, the cooling effects of green spaces were generally 
stronger than their air purification effects, with non-water-friendly streets showing more significant cooling 
benefits. These findings can help urban planners and policymakers understand the spatial patterns of running 
activity, offering insights into the design of more attractive, comfortable, and runner-friendly streets by 
addressing heat and air pollution.
1. Introduction
Running, because of its low cost, broad accessibility, and fitness 
benefits, has become the frequent choice of daily exercise for many 
people. Studies show that running not only significantly improves car­
diovascular function and reduces the risk of chronic diseases but also 
effectively relieves stress and enhances mental health (Huang et al., 
2023a; Schuurman et al., 2021). As sedentary lifestyles and a lack of 
physical exercise pose significant risks to public health and well-being 
(Althoff et al., 2017; Harden et al., 2022; Rosenkrantz et al., 2024; Yang 
et al., 2024b), actively promoting the health benefits of running is 
essential. Correspondingly, creating “runner-friendly” cities has become 
increasingly urgent. However, compared to research on walking and 
cycling, studies on running behavior remain relatively scarce. Given 
differences in pace and breathing patterns, environments that appeal to 
walkers and bikers might not appeal to runners (Aultman-Hall et al., 
1997; Salazar Miranda et al., 2021). Therefore, getting a clearer grasp of 
the patterns and behaviors of runners not only holds great practical 
importance for urban planners but also offers significant theoretical 
value to the fields of urban and health geography.
The rapid pace of urbanization has brought about significant issues 
(Ma et al., 2018). Among these, air pollution and heat discomfort are 
considered two of the biggest urban issues by cities across the globe 
(Akbari et al., 2001; Ngarambe et al., 2021). As one of the fastest- 
developing countries, China has experienced its share of persistent air 
pollution and widespread extreme heat events lately (Sun et al., 2024; 
Wang et al., 2024). These issues not only harm the ecosystem but also 
pose significant health risks to residents (Cao et al., 2016; Peng et al., 
2016). Particularly for runners, who typically engage in moderate- to 
high-intensity physical activity in public spaces, prolonged exposure to 
polluted air and elevated ambient temperatures can substantially in­
crease the risks of respiratory, cardiovascular, and heat-related illnesses. 
These environmental stressors therefore constitute critical constraints 
on participation in outdoor running and can markedly impair running 
performance.
High temperatures have become a key environmental factor influ­
encing running behavior and a significant health risk for running during 
the summer (Hajat et al., 2010). Excessive heat exposure can raise a

imbalance and triggering symptoms such as heatstroke and dehydration 
(Deng et al., 2018), while also significantly reducing exercise efficiency 
and the body's recovery capacity. More broadly, running in the heat 
poses substantial challenges to thermal comfort, perception of the 
running environment, and overall physical and mental well-being 
(Zhang et al., 2019). In this context, individuals often adopt behav­
ioral adaptations and avoidance strategies, such as reducing running 
frequency, shortening exercise duration, or adjusting route choices 
(Kondo et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2026). Taken together, high temperatures 
not only constitute a direct physiological health risk but also function as 
a perceptible environmental constraint that continuously shapes 
running behavior and spatial choices.
Currently, only a few studies have specifically examined the impact 
of air pollution on runners, a topic that remains underexplored 
(Hodgson and Hitchings, 2018; Huang et al., 2023a; Huang et al., 
2023b). The most direct impact of air pollution is physiological; it affects 
lung function and arterial pressure and increases the risk of heart con­
ditions, diabetes, and cancer (Beelen et al., 2014). These health risks 
may outweigh the physical benefits of outdoor exercise, reducing both 
its frequency and duration (Tainio et al., 2021). Pollutants like smog can 
also reduce outdoor visibility, discouraging participation in outdoor 
activities (Roberts et al., 2014). In addition, compared with ordinary 
walking or other recreational activities, runners exhibit greater venti­
lation volumes and higher breathing rates during exercise (Badland and 
Duncan, 2009). As a result, runners are more likely to inhale larger 
amounts of pollutants in polluted environments, thereby affecting ex­
ercise performance and post-exercise recovery (Bigazzi and Figliozzi, 
2014). Therefore, changes in air pollution levels are likely to influence 
running behavior by altering runners' environmental pollution exposure 
and their subjective perception of health risks. When combined, air 
pollution and extreme heat can accelerate physical deterioration, 
affecting both performance and mental well-being (Chen et al., 2023). 
Although current research examining the direct impact of air pollution 
and high temperatures on runners is limited, these factors may influence 
running behavior in similar ways. These conditions discourage outdoor 
running, highlighting the need for urban planners to identify cost- 
effective solutions to these environmental challenges.
Green spaces may be vital for lessening the negative effects of air 
pollution and high temperatures on runners (Feyisa et al., 2014; Yang 
et al., 2005). The cooling effect of green spaces is well-established, 
driven by three main factors. First, green spaces improve ventilation, 
lowering temperatures (Lai et al., 2023). Second, plant leaves, canopies, 
and trunks absorb or scatter visible light, reducing heat reaching the 
ground (B. Huang et al., 2022b). Third, plants lower temperatures 
through transpiration, raising humidity (Salata et al., 2017). Vegetation 
abundance influences these effects, but the morphology of green spaces, 
including size and complexity, also plays a crucial role (Yang et al., 
2020). In some cases, green space morphology may have a stronger 
cooling effect than vegetation abundance itself (Peng et al., 2016). 
Despite growing interest, research on green space morphology's cooling 
effects still requires further investigation.
The air-purification effects of green space have also been well 
documented in the literature (Ai et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022b), with 
evidence showing that vegetation can remove atmospheric pollutants 
through processes such as adsorption and filtration. However, their net 
effects are complex and may vary or be uncertain depending on vege­
tation characteristics, spatial configuration, and other contextual factors 
(Zheng et al., 2021). Specifically, green spaces can purify air by 
improving ventilation, absorbing toxins via leaf pores (Beckett et al., 
2000), and trapping particulate pollutants (Diener and Mudu, 2021). 
However, aerodynamic effects depend on environmental factors like 
vegetation density and weather conditions (Santiago and Rivas, 2021). 
Dense canopies or tall trees may hinder pollutant dispersion or cause 
localized thermal inversions, trapping pollutants (Buccolieri et al., 
2018). The relationship between sedimentation and aerodynamic effects 
is complex (Grylls and Van Reeuwijk, 2022). Additionally, the layout of 
green spaces can influence air purification, as fragmented spaces may 
reduce ventilation, and in some cases, vegetation may exacerbate 
pollution by emitting organic compounds that create secondary pollu­
tion (Duan et al., 2023). Green spaces can reduce pollutant concentra­
tions in multiple contexts through processes such as particle deposition, 
turbulence, and modulation of ventilation conditions, but their effec­
tiveness still exhibits significant uncertainty across different urban 
forms, pollutant types, and green space configurations (Pugh et al., 
2012). Therefore, in specific urban environments, quantifying the 
combined effects of different green space characteristics on multiple air 
pollutants remains critical for optimizing design to maximize health 
benefits.
For runners in particular, Green spaces positively affect residents' 
behaviors through bio-psycho-social functions and health benefits. 
Humans have an inherent tendency to connect with nature (Hanaˇcek 
et al., 2024), and green spaces offer visual appeal, tranquility, and safety 
for activities, improving perceptions of the environment (Liu et al., 
2022a). They also promote physical activity, improve health, and pro­
vide restorative environments that reduce stress and enhance mental 
well-being (Mazumdar et al., 2020). Although research on running 
behavior remains limited, urban green spaces likely influence running in 
a similar way to pedestrians, cyclists, and drivers. However, this needs 
further validation. Despite the benefits, green spaces may have negative 
effects, such as increased exposure to allergens, harmful chemicals, and 
disease vectors in certain conditions, leading to risks like allergies and 
asthma (Fan et al., 2020). Nevertheless, their positive impact on 
mobility and well-being is generally supported (Arnberger and Eder, 
2012; Persson et al., 2019).
Current research on the factors influencing running behavior pri­
marily adopts two types of models: linear and nonlinear. Linear models 
are built on the assumption of a linear relationship between variables. 
Various linear models, such as logistic regression, negative binomial 
regression, multilevel regression, and OLS regression, have been widely 
used to examine the impact of built environment factors on running 
behavior (Chen et al., 2020; D. Huang et al., 2022a; Huang et al., 2023b; 
Liu et al., 2022a). In addition, spatial regression models—a variant of 
linear models that account for spatial heterogeneity—have also been 
extensively applied. For example, models such as spatial error models, 
spatial lag models, GWR, and MGWR have been employed to explore the 
spatial heterogeneity of built environment factors on running behavior 
in cities like Chengdu, Massachusetts, Helsinki, and Guangzhou (Dong 
et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023a; Xie et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023). In 
recent years, the rise of machine learning methods has provided 
powerful tools for capturing the nonlinear effects and critical thresholds 
of environmental factors on running behavior. For instance, (Yang et al., 
2025) applied a random forest model to investigate the nonlinear impact 
of environmental factors on running behavior in Shenzhen. (Gu et al., 
2025) utilized the advanced XGBoost model to quantify the cooling ef­
fects of green spaces on running behavior from a nonlinear and inter­
active perspective. Additionally, (Yang et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2024b) 
adopted interpretable models such as GBDT and GW-RF to explore the 
influence of environmental factors on jogging behavior in Beijing. 
However, despite the progress made by both linear and nonlinear 
models in revealing the effects of environmental factors on running 
behavior, most studies have focused solely on the direct effects of in­
dividual variables. Research from the perspective of mediating effects, 
which can help uncover the underlying mechanisms through which 
environmental factors indirectly influence running behavior, remains 
limited. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) 
offers a significant advantage in addressing this gap. This approach is 
particularly suitable for our study, as it allows us to model both the 
direct and indirect pathways through which green spaces influence 
running behavior, while accounting for the mediating effects of envi­
ronmental factors such as air pollution and high temperatures (Xie et al., 
2024). Moreover, PLS-SEM enables the construction of latent variables

There are several gaps in the current body of research. First, studies 
on running behavior remain relatively limited. The existing literature 
tends to focus on the impact of urban form on walking and biking at the 
community and street scales. Nevertheless, unlike these activities, 
running is different in several key ways, and the environments that 
attract runners may not align with those that appeal to walkers or cy­
clists. Second, when modeling the factors that influence running 
behavior, most studies rely on single-equation regression models. 
However, this approach often overlooks the complex mediating effects 
between variables. By focusing on individual factors, these studies 
struggle to capture the dynamic interactions between influential ele­
ments, largely due to the inherent complexity of urban systems and the 
diverse pathways through which they exert influence. Third, the char­
acterization of green spaces and air pollution in the literature is often 
incomplete, failing to encompass the full range of their attributes. For 
example, many studies measuring green spaces use NDVI to capture 
horizontal abundance. Recent research has introduced GVI to measure 
vertical abundance (Rao et al., 2024); however, a more comprehensive 
understanding of the effect of green spaces' morphology on running, 
such as shape, size, density, and connectivity influence mobility, is still 
lacking (De Clercq et al., 2007; Wang and Tassinary, 2024; Zhang et al., 
2020). Similarly, studies on air pollution tend to focus on one or a few 
pollutants, neglecting the combined effects of multiple pollutants. 
Finally, the relationships among green spaces, high temperatures, and 
air pollution remain unclear, and their combined effects on running 
behavior have not been thoroughly explored. A few studies have inde­
pendently examined the cooling and air-purifying effects of green spaces 
on health, providing indirect evidence of these interactions. However, as 
per our understanding, this is the pioneering research to integrate these 
factors into a single framework. Specifically, we designed and tested the 
cooling and air-purifying effects of green spaces during running, 
addressing a significant gap in the existing research.
To address these gaps, we use running route data from Guangzhou, 
available from Keep, to apply spatial analysis to explore runners' envi­
ronmental preferences. Additionally, we employ PLS-SEM to quantify 
the cooling and air purification effects of urban green spaces on runners 
during their activity. The focus of this study is to characterize the spatial 
distribution patterns of urban running activities, assess the environ­
mental effects generated by green spaces along running routes during 
runners' activities, and further examine whether the cooling and air 
purification effects influence running intensity through mediating 
mechanisms, thereby revealing the intrinsic relationship between green 
space environmental effects and running behavior.
The purpose of this research is to tackle the listed issues: 
(1) What are the key characteristics of the geographic patterns of 
urban running activities?
(2) Do urban green spaces indeed provide air purification and cool­
ing effects for runners? If so, to what extent do these effects 
enhance runners' performance and running intensity?
Although running behavior is an emerging research topic within 
urban and health geography, a systematic research framework does not 
yet exist. This study makes several important contributions to that goal. 
First, by delving into the spatial patterns of running behavior, this paper 
complements and expands the existing body of theory, filling gaps in the 
current literature. Capturing the environmental preferences of outdoor 
runners sheds light on the spatial distribution and driving forces behind 
running behavior, offering new insights into the complex interactions 
between urban space and health-related behaviors. Second, the study 
quantifies the specific pathways through which green spaces influence 
running behavior. Incorporating air pollution and high temperatures as 
mediating variables highlights their critical roles in the relationship 
between green spaces and running activities. As we are aware, this 
represents the first exploration of integrating high temperatures, air 
pollution, and running behavior into a unified analytical framework, 
providing a fresh perspective on the mechanisms by which green spaces 
affect running behavior. Finally, from a bottom-up perspective, the 
study encourages urban planners to develop a more profound insight 
into the design and management of public spaces, particularly the 
environmental vulnerabilities that may be hidden along running routes. 
The study offers targeted recommendations to improve the running 
environment for urban residents by identifying these vulnerabilities, 
ultimately contributing to better public health and well-being.
2. Data and methods
2.1. Study area overview
Guangzhou, a major city in China's Pearl River Delta, is one of 
China's nine national central cities. The city has a strong running culture, 
supported by a comprehensive infrastructure and government funding. 
However, outdoor running in Guangzhou still faces two major chal­
lenges: high summer temperatures and air pollution. The city experi­
ences a marine subtropical monsoon climate, with summer average 
temperatures typically ranging from 28 ◦C to 34 ◦C, peaking in July and 
August. Meanwhile, air pollution remains a persistent issue, mainly 
originating from vehicle emissions, industrial discharges, and secondary 
pollutants formed under strong sunlight and high temperatures. The 
intense summer sunlight accelerates ozone formation, making it the 
primary pollutant affecting Guangzhou's air quality, with concentrations 
reaching 130–202 μg/m3. These factors collectively pose adverse effects 
on air quality and outdoor physical activities. Urban green spaces are a 
key component of Guangzhou's built environment and ecological sys­
tem, playing a crucial role in boosting air quality, alleviating urban heat, 
and encouraging outdoor activities like running. Known as the “City of 
Flowers”, Guangzhou has a green coverage rate of 43.8% and over 1500 
parks. The city has established a comprehensive and well-connected 
green space system, making Guangzhou an ideal case for studying 
how urban green spaces influence running behavior.
The center of Guangzhou, consisting of the Yuexiu, Tianhe, Haizhu, 
Liwan, and Baiyun districts, serves as the representative area for this 
study, as it encompasses many of the city's most iconic urban landscapes. 
This core area is delineated by the intersection of the Guangzhou Ring 
Expressway with the jurisdictional limits of the above districts, covering 
a total area of roughly 214.57 km2. The selected zone is characterized by 
a high concentration of urban features and includes many of the city's 
most popular running routes. Using the OSM road network as a foun­
dation, we created buffer zones using the ArcScan tool and conducted 
topological checks to extract the road center lines within the study area. 
The simplified road network is shown in Fig. 1. Water systems adjacent 
to the green spaces offer additional cooling effects; therefore, we clas­
sified the streets within the study area as either water-friendly streets, 
located within 200 m of the nearest body of water, or non-water-friendly 
streets, situated beyond 200 m away (Xu and Zhao, 2023). This classi­
fication fully accounts for the potential cooling effects of water systems, 
providing a clearer understanding of how green spaces impact the 
comfort levels of runners by influencing environmental temperatures.
2.2. Research approach
The study approach consisted of three main steps (Fig. 2). In the first 
step, we calculated the running intensity for each street. Specifically, a 
20-m buffer zone was created around a simplified road network based on 
the OSM. Then the Keep running route data for Guangzhou was clipped 
and preprocessed to match the research units. Applying the “Summarize

Journal of Transport Geography 133 (2026) 104653 
3 

Within” function in ArcGIS Pro 2.8, we calculated the cumulative dis­
tance of running trails within each street's 20-m buffer zone. Buffers with 
a total route length of zero were removed. The running intensity for each 
remaining street was then computed by dividing the total route length 
by the buffer area. In the second step, we calculated and collected the 
variables. Twenty-two target variables were computed across seven 
categories: urban green spaces, air pollution, building characteristics, 
traffic factors, urban blue spaces, surface temperature, and population 
density. These variables are derived from street view imagery, multi- 
source remote sensing data, and building vector data. Buffer zones of 
50, 100, 150, and 200 m were created around the road center lines, and 
the values of the 22 variables calculated for each buffer. In the third step, 
we constructed the PLS-SEM. Based on the potential cooling and air 
purification effects of urban green spaces on runners, we used the 
“plspm” package in R to build two PLS-SEMs—one for streets with water 
features and one for streets without. The models' validity and reliability 
were tested, and the optimal PLS-SEM for each scenario was identified 
and compared. Using the results from the best models, we analyzed the 
cooling and air purification roles that urban green spaces play in influ­
encing running behavior.
2.3. Indicators, data sources, and processing
2.3.1. Running intensity
Using API analysis and reverse engineering tools, we extracted 
running trajectory data for Guangzhou from Keep for August 2019, 
employing “Guangzhou” as the search key term. The dataset was then 
filtered based on the study area; duplicate records, extremely short trips, 
and invalid trajectories with incorrect sites were eliminated. Next, we 
divided the street network into segments according to road intersection 
rules and created 20-m buffer zones around each segment. For each 
buffer zone, we calculated the total length of running trails that inter­
sected or were entirely inside the buffer. The running intensity for each 
street segment was then calculated by dividing the total length of each 
running route by the corresponding buffer area. After excluding seg­
ments without any running routes, 10664 street units were included in 
the study, with running intensities between 0.000013 and 17.645459 
m/m2.
2.3.2. Urban green spaces
The morphology of urban green spaces plays a crucial role in shaping 
environmental effects and human activity patterns (Xu et al., 2025a). In 
this study, we characterized urban green spaces from two perspectives: 
abundance and morphology. Abundance is measured using NDVI, which 
represents vegetation abundance, and GVI, which represents the amount 
of greenery visible to runners along the streets. The NDVI dataset for 
Guangzhou was generated using the GEE platform based on all available 
Landsat 5/7/8 data from 2019. After applying cloud masking, shadow 
filtering, and interpolation techniques, we calculated the NDVI value for 
each valid observation. The highest NDVI value for each pixel over the 
year was determined using linear interpolation and Savitzky-Golay 
smoothing. After cells with negative NDVI values were removed, the 
average NDVI for each street buffer zone was calculated. The GVI was 
calculated using Baidu Street View images. A simplified road network 
was used to generate 11,904 street-view sampling points at 100-m in­
tervals. Python scripts extracted panoramic images from these points, 
and PSPNet semantic segmentation was applied to calculate the GVI for 
each point. The average GVI was then determined for four different 
street buffer zones.
Green space morphology was described using six indices: the Ag­
gregation Index (AI), Mean Patch Area (AREA_MN), Patch Cohesion 
Index (COHESION), Landscape Connectivity Index (CONNECT), Patch 
Density (PD), and Area-Weighted Mean Shape Index (SHAPE_AM). 
These indices reflect the aggregation, size, connectivity, patch frag­
mentation, and shape complexity of green spaces, respectively. The data 
for green space morphology were derived from Guangzhou's green-space 
raster data from 2020, sourced from the Earth System Science Data 
(ESSD). Fragstats 4.3 was used to compute these indices.
2.3.3. Air pollution
Given the data available, we selected five common air 
pollutants—O3, CO, NO2, PM2.5, and PM10—to evaluate the effect of air 
pollution on runners' behavior. The base data for these pollutants were 
sourced from the Chinese High-Resolution Air Pollutant dataset (CHAP) 
for Guangzhou in August 2019, with an original spatial resolution of 1 
km, representing the highest publicly available multi-pollutant raster 
product. We then used the “Resample” tool in ArcGIS Pro to spatially 
scale the raster data, resampling all air pollution rasters to a 100 m 
resolution. This resampling process was intended to avoid spatial 
overlay bias caused by scale inconsistency and is a standard practice for 
analyzing continuous raster variables (Cai et al., 2022; Parvar et al., 
2024). Then, using the “Zonal Statistics” tool in ArcGIS Pro, we calcu­
lated the average concentration of each pollutant within the four types 
of street buffer zones.

encountered during outdoor activities. Under direct sunlight, high-

continuously emit longwave radiation to the human body, and LST can

2.3.5. Other indicators

2.4. PLS-SEM
PLS-SEM is an approach used to analyze data from multiple observed 
variables by integrating them into latent variables and examining the 
linear associations between these latent variables (Xie et al., 2024). The 
model consists of two main parts: the measurement model and the 
structural model. The model is constructed (Yen et al., 2017) by 
assessing latent composite predictors in the outer model and evaluating 
path coefficients between latent variables in the inner model. Compared 
to covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM), PLS-SEM offers several advantages 
(Westerholt et al., 2022): (1) it does not require data to follow a normal 
distribution, enhancing estimation accuracy and applicability; (2) its 
predictive capability allows for causal interpretations even in the 
absence of strong theoretical guidance; and (3) it can be applied even 
when fewer than three observed indicators exist for a latent variable. 
Given that this study primarily focuses on exploring the relationships 
between latent variables, rather than emphasizing the role of individual 
observed indicators in the model, PLS-SEM is particularly suitable. This 
approach allows us to effectively capture the complex mechanisms of 
green space on running behavior while accounting for the mediating 
effects of air pollution and temperature. Furthermore, the loading 
weights of observed indicators, such as NDVI GVI, and the green 
morphology indexes, are included in the measurement model to ensure 
the reliability and validity of the constructed latent variables, which 
indirectly reflects their contribution to the overall model.
In this study, we collected five latent variables and three observed 
variables to build the PLS-SEM. For the model representing water- 
friendly streets, we used five latent variables—urban green spaces, 
blue spaces, air pollution, traffic factors, and building factors—along 
with three observed variables—temperature, running intensity, and 
population density. For non-water-friendly streets, the same variables 
were used except for blue spaces. Fig. 3 presents the hypothesized PLS- 
SEM models for both scenarios.
2.5. Assessment of PLS-SEM
The assessment of reliability and validity of PLS-SEM is crucial to 
ensure the robustness of the measurement and structural models. This 
evaluation typically includes three key aspects: reliability, convergent 
validity, and discriminant validity. Additionally, the predictive power of 
the structural model is examined using Q2 and R2 values. To ensure the 
reliability of our measurements, we employed factor loadings, Cron­
bach's α, and composite reliability (CR) (Xie et al., 2024). Factor load­
ings indicate the correlation between observed variables and their 
underlying constructs, while Cronbach's α measures the internal con­
sistency of the constructs. Composite reliability further evaluates the 
overall reliability by considering the factor loadings of each indicator. 
For convergent validity, we used the average variance extracted (AVE) 
(Xu et al., 2017). It assesses the extent to which a construct explains the 
variance of its associated indicators, with a threshold of 0.5 typically 
indicating adequate convergent validity. To test discriminant validity, 
we adopted the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio method (Henseler 
et al., 2015). The HTMT ratio compares the correlations between con­
structs, with a value below 0.90 indicating sufficient discriminant val­
idity. Finally, for structural model evaluation, we examined Q2 and R2 
values to assess the predictive power of endogenous variables (Al-Jundi 
et al., 2020). The R2 value indicates the proportion of variance explained 
by the model, while Q2, obtained through blindfolding procedures, 
measures the predictive relevance of the model.

A B S T R A C T
People perceive waterfront walking spaces through continuously changing visual landscapes and soundscapes. 
However, existing audio-visual assessments largely rely on fixed static scenes or isolated acoustic indicators. This 
study proposes a scalable framework for explaining dynamic landscape preference in waterfront walking envi­
ronments by integrating distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape characteristics. Taking Dalian as a 
case study, 59 participants provided dynamic perceptual evaluations of 36 one-minute walking videos. Deep 
learning models were applied to extract distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape features, while a 
structural causal model (SCM) and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis were combined to examine 
dose–response patterns and interaction effects. The comparison between SHAP-based predictive attribution and 
SCM-based effect-strength ranking suggests that the intervention-oriented relevance of the equivalent continuous 
sound pressure level (LAeq) may be underestimated when preference is interpreted only through general pre­
dictive importance. Different variables exhibited distinct nonlinear patterns. The contribution direction of 
midground disturbance to preference was moderated by LAeq and showed opposite patterns. The interaction 
between background openness and foreground enclosure further revealed spatial configuration mechanisms in 
dynamic walking environments. Notably, features such as human sounds, background openness, and natural 
sounds showed clear contextual heterogeneity across functional areas and park types. These findings provide 
measurable empirical evidence for the synergistic audio-visual design of waterfront walking environments.
1. Introduction
Waterfront parks are important urban blue–green spaces for daily 
recreation, leisure walking, and the promotion of physical and mental 
well-being [1,2]. Their capacity to attract visitors and support 
well-being depends not only on the presence of water and greenery, but 
also on how individuals perceive these environments during movement 
[3]. Landscape preference is a key perceptual indicator linking land­
scape quality and restorative experience with the design and manage­
ment of urban natural environments [4,5]. Waterfront walking 
experience involves continuous changes in visual, auditory, and spatial 
exposure that are not fully represented by fixed-scene evaluations [6]. 
Therefore, re-examining landscape preference in waterfront parks from 
a dynamic, multisensory perspective has clear theoretical and practical 
significance.
Landscape preference is shaped by multiple environmental cues 
operating across visual, auditory, and spatial domains. In the visual 
domain, it is significantly associated with both compositional attributes, 
such as visible greenery, water visibility, and color diversity, and 
organizational attributes, including openness, scene depth, and enclo­
sure [7–9]. In the auditory domain, soundscape quality also significantly 
influences restorative experiences and aesthetic preference [4,5]. 
Furthermore, audio-visual interactions can alter overall scene evalua­
tion [10–13]. Taken together, these studies indicate that landscape 
preference in waterfront parks should be understood as a holistic 
response shaped during dynamic exposure, rather than as an isolated 
judgment of a single visual element.
Despite these insights, existing research still has three main limita­
tions. First, many studies still rely on static photographs, street-view 
images, or fixed scenes. These materials allow controlled comparison 
of visual attributes, but they provide limited information about the 
temporal progression of waterfront walking experience. Along a route, 
pedestrians may encounter changing openness, enclosure, water visi­
bility, activity intensity, sound-source composition, and sound pressure 
level. Such sequential exposure is difficult to represent through isolated 
scenes [6,14]. Second, existing visual quantification mostly focuses on

For pedestrians, foreground elements shape immediate enclosure,

coupled. Therefore, it is necessary to combine a structural causal model 
based on a prespecified causal graph with interpretable machine 
learning to identify dose–response patterns, interaction effects, and key 
environmental cues with design-intervention relevance.
To address these research gaps, this study develops an integrated 
multisensory framework for explaining dynamic audio-visual preference 
in waterfront walking environments. The framework links route-based 
first-person audio-visual stimuli with continuous preference ratings, 
distance-layered spatial structure, soundscape indicators, and an 
explanatory modeling strategy. It is designed to clarify how foreground, 
midground, and background spatial configurations, together with 
acoustic exposure and sound-source characteristics, contribute to land­
scape preference under dynamic walking conditions. Specifically, the 
research objectives are as follows. 
(1) To develop a scalable workflow for evaluating dynamic prefer­
ence responses under synchronized visual and acoustic exposure.
(2) To quantify distance-layered spatial structure features and 
multidimensional soundscape indicators, and to estimate their 
design-relevant associations with landscape preference under a 
prespecified causal graph and observed-covariate adjustment.
(3) To identify nonlinear dose–response patterns, cross-modal in­
teractions, and contextual differences that can inform fine-scale 
renewal strategies for waterfront walking environments.
2. Literature review
2.1. Dynamic landscape preference and audio-visual interactions
Landscape preference is not a simple response to static visual infor­
mation, but an integrated perceptual outcome shaped by dynamic 
exposure and multisensory integration. Because environmental experi­
ence is inherently multisensory, auditory stimuli can modify visual 
landscape evaluation; natural and congruent sounds generally enhance 
aesthetic preference and restorative experience, whereas incongruent 
audio-visual stimuli are more likely to elicit negative evaluations [4,5,
11,13,16]. Dynamic perception may be relevant to landscape preference 
because route-based exposure presents sequential changes in spatial 
layout, visibility, and environmental cues during movement. Such 
moving visual sequences may help capture spatial continuity, explora­
tion, and changing audio-visual context that are less explicit in isolated 
static scenes [14,17].
Taken together, landscape preference is shaped by visual informa­
tion, dynamic experience, and audio-visual interaction; however, most 
studies still examine visual content and soundscape characteristics 
separately, leaving unclear how spatial organization modifies sound­
scape effects during dynamic exposure.
2.2. Spatial-structure metrics for landscape preference
Visual assessment of landscape preference provides an important 
approach to understanding human responses to the environment [18,
19]. From a design perspective, the visual attributes shaping landscape 
preference can be divided into compositional attributes, which define 
landscape content, and organizational attributes, which describe how 
that content is spatially configured [8,15]. Previous studies have shown 
that compositional attributes, such as green view index, plant richness 
[20], artificial facilities [21], perceived color diversity [22], and 
element combinations [23], have been associated with landscape pref­
erence and visual behavior.
Although organizational attributes such as scene depth, sense of 
order, and scene type can moderate the effects of compositional attri­
butes on preference, their measurement remains insufficient because 
existing visual metrics are still largely limited to two-dimensional pixel 
proportions and provide limited representation of openness, scene 
depth, and enclosure [9,15].
Prospect–refuge theory suggests that people tend to prefer environ­
ments that balance visual access and a sense of protection [24]. 
Empirical studies based on viewshed analysis, photographs, virtual re­
ality (VR), and field experiments have linked preference to visible area 
[25,26], scene type [27], openness [23], scene depth [9,28], and spatial 
form [29]. Recent image-based studies have attempted to quantify 
prospect and refuge using low-level visual features, but evidence re­
mains mixed regarding whether openness and enclosure have stable and 
independent effects on preference [9,30,31].
Moreover, compositional and organizational attributes have often 
been examined separately, while limited attention has been paid to how 
landscape elements are spatially organized. Because the acquisition of 
distance-layered spatial structure information often relies on controlled 
experiments, subjective assessments, or costly scanning devices, low- 
cost and scalable quantification of compositional and organizational 
attributes in real-world settings remains a key challenge [32,33].
2.3. Machine learning and causal explanation in landscape preference 
research
In recent years, machine learning and deep learning techniques have 
provided new methodological pathways for landscape preference 
research. Compared with traditional linear regression models, methods 
such as XGBoost and random forest can better capture complex 
nonlinear relationships and often achieve higher predictive accuracy. 
Interpretation tools such as SHAP can also help reveal the predictive 
logic within models, thereby supporting design decision-making and 
management. Scalable feature-extraction methods, including low-level 
visual analysis, semantic segmentation, monocular depth estimation, 
and sound classification, make it possible to integrate visual [34], spatial 
[35], and auditory indicators [36] for landscape-preference research.
Although these models have clear advantages in feature extraction 
and predictive accuracy, the black-box nature of machine learning 
models makes it difficult for them to provide directly actionable evi­
dence for planning and design. This problem is especially evident when 
environmental elements are highly coupled, because multicollinearity 
and confounding may prevent correlation analysis or conventional 
machine learning from identifying the independent role of a single 
element. Causal inference has therefore been introduced into urban and 
environmental studies to estimate intervention-oriented effects under 
explicit assumptions, and some studies have further combined SCM with 
machine learning models [37–39]. However, in landscape perception 
research, the integration of causal inference with high-dimensional au­
dio-visual features and interpretable machine learning remains limited.

Based on the above gaps, this study addresses the following research 
questions: 
(1) How can dynamic audio-visual preference in waterfront walking 
environments be measured and represented through route-based 
stimuli, continuous ratings, spatial-structure metrics, and 
soundscape indicators?
(2) Which distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape vari­

(3) How do these variables vary across exposure levels, interact

soundscape interact in dynamic settings. Under explicit assumptions, it 
also seeks to advance an intervention-oriented explanation of observed 
relationships, thereby providing evidence to support fine-scale renewal 
and design decision-making in waterfront spaces.
3. Methods
The analytical workflow comprised four main steps. First, route- 
based first-person audio-visual stimuli were collected in five water­
front parks in Dalian and used in a real-time landscape-preference 
assessment experiment conducted in a Cave Automatic Virtual Envi­
ronment (CAVE). Second, computer-vision, audio-processing, and 
acoustic models were applied to extract spatial structure, low-level vi­
sual, and soundscape features from the audio-visual stimuli. Third, 
dose–response relationships were estimated conditional on a pre­
specified directed acyclic graph (DAG) and measured covariates, and 
their robustness was assessed using multiple refutation tests and a video- 
level block bootstrap based on Unique_Scene_ID. Finally, variables that 
passed robustness screening, together with the selected confounding 
controls, were used to build predictive models. SHAP analysis was then 
employed to interpret main effects, interaction patterns, and contextual 
heterogeneity in feature attribution.
3.1. Data collection
3.1.1. Study area
To identify key environmental cues associated with landscape pref­
erence under dynamic audio-visual conditions, the experiment used 
route-based first-person videos as stimuli. This design allowed partici­
pants to experience continuous visual–acoustic changes while reducing 
potential confounding effects from ambient thermal and meteorological 
conditions. Five waterfront parks in Dalian, Liaoning Province, China, 
were selected as study sites (Fig. 1): Dalian Botanical Garden (Park_1), 
Xishanhu Park (Park_2), Baiyun Yanshui Fitness Park (Park_3), Qiang­
guan Central Urban Wetland Park (Park_4), and Zelonghu Park (Park_5). 
These sites represent botanical, suburban waterfront, sports and fitness, 
urban wetland, and community waterfront park settings. They also vary 
in sound-source composition, spatial openness, activity intensity, and 
waterfront interface morphology, thereby increasing the environmental 
heterogeneity of the stimulus sample.
3.1.2. Audio-visual stimuli
Field data were collected under clear daytime conditions from late 
May to early June 2025. The data acquisition system was mounted at an 
adult eye-level height of 1.6 m to approximate first-person audio-visual 
perception during movement. Continuous visual footage was recorded 
using a DJI Osmo Action 4 action camera at 4K resolution and 60 fps. An 
Aihua ISV 1102 sound level meter was used to measure the A-weighted 
equivalent continuous sound pressure level (LAeq) during video 
recording. Sound-source information was captured using a TASCAM DR- 
05X recorder at 96 kHz/24-bit sampling resolution. Spoken start and 
end commands were recorded to mark each recording session, and the 
audio-visual information was precisely aligned during post-processing 
based on timestamps.

viewed only 18 videos. The presentation order of videos within each 
subset was randomized. Because group assignment and video subset 
were not fully separable by design, Group was treated only as a 
contextual control and was not interpreted as an independently identi­
fiable experimental effect.
Previous research has reported that CAVE-based environments can 
provide good ecological validity in eliciting psychological responses 
[40]. Accordingly, the audio-visual stimuli in the landscape preference 
experiment were presented using a CAVE system. Preference ratings 
were recorded using a mobile-based continuous rating tool developed 
for this study. Before the formal experiment, participants completed a 
5-min practice session using a similar video that was not included in the 
experimental stimuli. Preference was rated on a continuous 1–7 scale, 
ranging from “dislike” to “like”. A 5-s neutral gray screen was presented 
after each video, and a 2-min break was provided after every six videos 
to minimize cumulative fatigue. The study-site examples and segmen­
tation of the dynamic audio-visual stimuli are shown in Fig. 2.
During the experiment, preference ratings were recorded at 1-s in­
tervals, and video-frame and acoustic features were extracted at the 
same interval. For analysis, subjective ratings and environmental fea­
tures were temporally matched and aggregated into adjacent 3-s time 
windows to reduce second-by-second rating noise and partially account 
for short-term response lag. Each 1-min video therefore generated 20 
consecutive video-window observations, yielding 720 video-window 
observations from the 36 videos. These video-window observations 
were used as the statistical units for subsequent causal inference and 
machine-learning analyses. These 720 records represented video- 
window analytical units derived from 36 videos × 20 time windows. 
They should not be interpreted as independent participant-level obser­
vations. The main SCM analysis therefore estimated group-level average 
response patterns at the video-window level. In the dose–response 
analysis and related robustness checks, Unique_Scene_ID was used as the 
video-level block resampling unit. Thus, the main analysis identified 
group-level average preference patterns at the video-window level 
rather than individual-level responses.
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the authors' 
affiliated institution (Approval No.: YSSJ-20250415), and all partici­
pants provided written informed consent before the experiment. All data 
were anonymized before analysis, and the study was conducted in 
accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
3.2. Feature engineering
To identify the key drivers of dynamic landscape preference, this 
study constructed a multidimensional indicator system covering spatial 
structure, low-level visual features, and acoustic attributes. The ratio­
nale for feature selection and the variable definitions and calculation 
formulas are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S2.
For spatial-structure and low-level visual-feature extraction, video 
frames were first extracted at 1-s intervals using FFmpeg. Low-level 
visual features, including brightness, saturation, contrast, number of 
colors, and edge complexity, were then calculated from the extracted 
frames using OpenCV. Next, Mask2Former, pretrained on the ADE20K 
dataset [41], was applied to estimate the semantic-class proportions of 
sky, vegetation, water, roads, buildings, people, and other scene ele­
ments (for detailed mapping rules, see Supplementary Table S11). These 
semantic outputs were further aggregated into composite indicators, 
including green view index, blue view index, enclosure, openness, visual 
imageability, and disturbance.
Subsequently, the metric-depth version of Depth Anything V2 was 
used for outdoor scene depth estimation [42]. This version was 
fine-tuned on the Virtual KITTI 2 dataset and can generate metric depth 
maps in meters. Infrared distance measurements were also conducted in 
the field to compare actual distances with model-estimated distances 
and to assess the accuracy of the depth estimates. Based on the estimated 
depth values, the scene was divided into three distance layers: fore­
ground (0–10 m), midground (10–30 m), and background (>30 m).

The three distance layers were used as a pedestrian-scale oper­
ationalization of distance-dependent spatial perception rather than as 
universal psychophysical cutoffs. From an ecological perspective, visual 
perception during walking is shaped by the relationship between the 
moving body and the surrounding environment; therefore, spatial ele­
ments at different distances may provide different perceptual and 
behavioral affordances [43]. In depth-perception theory, the distinction 
between action space and vista space suggests that the range within 
approximately 30 m is more closely related to locomotion, avoidance, 
and action planning, whereas more distant space is perceived primarily 
through global layout, openness, and background continuity [44]. 
Accordingly, the >30 m layer was interpreted as the background or vista 
field. Within the action-related range, the 0–10 m and 10–30 m layers 
were further distinguished to reflect pedestrian-scale spatial experience. 
The 0–10 m layer represents the immediate near field where enclosure, 
disturbance, vegetation, facilities, path-edge conditions, and social 
presence directly shape bodily proximity and walking experience. This 
near-field interpretation is informed by proxemics, which emphasizes 
culturally and socially structured interpersonal distance, and by 
human-scale public-space theory, which highlights how pedestrian 
experience is shaped by eye-level spatial cues and social activities in 
public space [45–48]. The 10–30 m layer represents the middle-distance 
field in which activity patterns, water edges, vegetation groups, and 
spatial boundaries remain legible during movement [45,47]. This 
operationalization is also consistent with landscape visual-structure 
studies that distinguish foreground, middleground, and background 
views, while adapting this logic to the finer spatial scale of waterfront 
walking environments [49,50].
To examine whether the main findings depended on the exact dis­
tance thresholds, we conducted an additional distance-threshold sensi­
tivity analysis. The baseline scheme was S0 = 0–10 m / 10–30 m / 
>30 m. Three alternative schemes were tested by slightly shifting the 
foreground–midground and midground–background boundaries: S1 
(0–5 m / 5–20 m / >20 m), S2 (0–8 m / 8–25 m / >25 m), and S3 
(0–15 m / 15–35 m / >35 m). For each scheme, the distance-layered 
spatial variables were recalculated, the SCM-based dose–response 
curves were re-estimated, and the results were compared with the 
baseline scheme using effect strength, effect direction, Spearman cor­
relation, and root mean square error (RMSE). Detailed results are pro­
vided in Supplementary Section S8 and Table S12, with supplementary 
curves for variables ranked 5–14 shown in Fig. S4.
Outputs from Mask2Former and Depth Anything V2 were then 
combined to extract spatial-structure features across different distance 
layers. Because depth prediction is less stable in distant sky regions, such 
pixels were assigned to the open background through a rule-based 
procedure. Therefore, the resulting indicators should be interpreted as 
proxies for spatial structure rather than as an absolute 3D 
reconstruction.
For auditory-feature extraction, YAMNet was used to derive the 
mean confidence scores of Sound_Nature, Sound_Traffic, Sound_Human, 
and Sound_Quiet, representing natural sounds, traffic/mechanical 
sounds, human sounds, and quietness, respectively [51]. These outputs 
reflect the relative confidence of class labels and were used as proxy 
indicators for sound source types and soundscape perception. YAMNet is 
a pretrained acoustic recognition model based on the MobileNetV1 ar­
chitecture. It performs automatic multi-label classification of 521 sound 
classes in the AudioSet dataset by analyzing log-mel spectrogram fea­
tures. In addition, sharpness, roughness, and fluctuation strength were 
calculated using the MoSQITo Python library, following the psycho­
acoustic approach described in previous work [52].
All feature variables were preprocessed. First, variables with vari­
ance inflation factor (VIF) values greater than 10 were removed through 
a multicollinearity test to reduce parameter instability and prevent 
collinearity-induced inflation of estimation variance. Second, although 
experimental recording and feature extraction were conducted at 1-s

3.3. Causal inference and SHAP interaction analysis
As illustrated in the overall research workflow (Fig. 3), this study 
adopted an analytical framework integrating an SCM with machine 
learning to estimate intervention-oriented associations between spatial- 
structure and soundscape features and landscape preference under the 
predefined DAG and observed covariates. The SCM was used for DAG- 
based adjustment and dose–response estimation, while SHAP was used 
to interpret predictive interactions and contextual heterogeneity. All 
core causal inference and statistical analyses were conducted in Python 
using DoWhy and related libraries.
3.3.1. Causal graph construction and dose–response curves
First, a predefined DAG was constructed to represent the hypothe­
sized relationships among variables, based on environmental perception 
theory and prior knowledge. The complete DAG, key pathways, and 
theoretical rationale are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and 
Table S3.
To clarify the assumptions underlying the SCM, the directed edges in 
the prespecified DAG were used as theory- and evidence-informed 
structural assumptions for DAG-based adjustment and intervention- 
oriented dose–response estimation. The main pathways were classified 
into three assumption types: theory-supported perceptual pathways, 
physical-process pathways, and empirical-assumption pathways. The 
perceptual pathways assumed that spatial structure, visual features, and 
soundscape exposure influence landscape preference through openness, 
enclosure, visual disturbance, landscape legibility, acoustic comfort, and 
audio-visual integration [3,11]. The physical-process pathways assumed 
that vegetation, ground surfaces, water surfaces, hard pavements, and 
built interfaces may influence acoustic and psychoacoustic indicators 
through sound absorption, reflection, scattering, shielding, and attenu­
ation [53,54]. The empirical soundscape pathways assumed that spatial 
form, functional area, activity intensity, and ecological background are 
associated with sound-source composition, including natural, human, 
traffic/mechanical, and quietness-related sounds [55,56]. In particular, 
the pathway from spatial structure to LAeq was specified through two 
linked assumptions: a physical-propagation assumption, in which spatial 
materials and interfaces may modify sound transmission, and a 
sound-source-distribution assumption, in which spatial form and func­
tional area may shape activity patterns, crowd aggregation, traffic 
proximity, and sound-source composition. Detailed explanations of the 
pathway assumptions, supporting references, and interpretation 
boundaries are provided in Supplementary Table S3.
Individual characteristics were not included as conventional con­
founders in the main SCM because the exposures in the main analysis 
were video-window-level environmental variables, whereas the 
outcome was the group-level mean preference score for the same video 
window. Variables such as noise sensitivity, professional background, 
aesthetic preference, or individual response lag may influence partici­
pants’ rating baselines and sensitivity to visual or acoustic cues, but they 
do not determine the LAeq, openness, water visibility, or visual distur­
bance level of a given video-window observation. These factors were 
therefore treated as potential sources of individual-level heterogeneity

confounders. Professional background was measured and examined in

In the DAG, directed edges between nodes represent the assumed 
causal relationships among variables. The variables were organized into 
three layers: spatial structure, low-level visual features, and soundscape 
indicators. Based on prior knowledge, edge directions across these layers 
were constrained. Park_ID and Functional_Area were included to ac­

included as design and temporal controls [57]. According to the back­
door criterion, variables satisfying the criterion were identified using the 
DoWhy library and included in the adjustment set. Specifically, Park_ID 
and Functional_Area were considered related to the potential con­

To estimate intervention-oriented effects, each candidate environ­
mental variable was treated as an exposure T, and landscape preference 
was treated as the outcome Y. Under the prespecified DAG and the 
corresponding adjustment set Z, the target quantity was the expected 
preference score under intervention: 
μ(t) = E[Y|do(T = t)]
(1) 
where do(T = t) denotes an intervention that sets the exposure to a given 
dose level. For each exposure, the observed range was divided into 100 
dose levels. Dose–response curves were estimated using SCM-based 
nonlinear prediction with 300 video-level block bootstrap resamples 
based on Unique_Scene_ID. Random forest models were used to fit 
nonlinear relationships among non-root nodes. The response amplitude 
within the central 95% exposure range was used as the effect-strength 
index. Detailed estimation procedures are provided in Supplementary 
Section S4.
3.3.2. Robustness checks
Four refutation tests were used to assess the robustness of the esti­
mated dose–response curves: placebo treatment, data subset refutation, 
random common cause (RCC), and mechanism-change tests. Placebo 
curves were expected to show near-null effects, whereas the other 
refutation curves were compared with the baseline curves using 
Spearman correlation and TOST equivalence testing. All refutation 
procedures were implemented with video-level block resampling. 
Detailed criteria are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

4. Results
4.1. Data preprocessing and methodological checks
In the multicollinearity test, three variables—Green_Fore, Enclose_­
Mid, and Vis_Entropy—had variance inflation factor (VIF) values greater 
than 10 and were therefore removed before machine-learning and 
causal-inference analyses (Table S6). After their removal, the VIF values 
of all remaining variables were below 10. Based on the predefined data- 
quality criteria, three participants were excluded: two due to abnormal 
recordings from the rating device and one due to an invalid response 
pattern in the rating sequence. The final valid sample included 59 par­
ticipants, with 32 in Group_1 and 27 in Group_2 (Table 1). Because the 
primary statistical unit in this study was the aggregated group-level 
average score, the average-measures intraclass correlation coefficient 
(ICC) was used to assess the reliability of group-level average ratings. 
The results showed that the ICC was 0.870 for Group_1 and 0.919 for 
Group_2, both statistically significant at p < 0.001, indicating good 
reliability of the group-level average ratings (Table S7). In the subse­
quent main analyses, all results were based on 720 video-window ob­
servations. These records were used only as units of analysis, and video- 
level block resampling based on Unique_Scene_ID was applied to pre­
serve the within-video temporal correlation structure.
The depth-layer validation results are reported in Supplementary 
Fig. S2 and Table S8. Based on 191 annotated validation points, the 
overall classification accuracy of foreground, midground, and back­
ground layers was 87.43%. Within the 0–80 m analysis range, model- 
predicted depth was strongly and significantly correlated with field- 
measured distance (r = 0.896, p < 0.001). Stratified validation by 
scene type further showed classification accuracies of 83.33% for 
wooded / shaded vegetation areas, 92.50% for open green / path- 
corridor areas, 87.88% for paved and facility-dominated areas, and 
86.05% for open water / waterfront-edge areas. These results indicate 
acceptable and reasonably stable distance-layer classification perfor­
mance across the tested scene types.
To further examine whether the main findings depended on the 
temporal aggregation window or the distance-layer thresholds, two 
sensitivity analyses were conducted. The temporal-window sensitivity 
analysis compared 1-s, 2-s, 3-s, and 5-s aggregation windows, whereas 
the distance-threshold sensitivity analysis compared the baseline 
0–10 m / 10–30 m / >30 m scheme with three alternative distance-layer 
schemes. The sensitivity analyses showed that the main dose–response 
patterns of the top-ranked variables remained generally stable under 
alternative temporal-window and distance-threshold specifications 
(Fig. 6). In particular, Aud_LAeq, Disturb_Mid, Disturb_Fore, Open_Back, 
Sound_Human, and Water_Mid retained consistent effect directions 
across the tested specifications, although moderate vertical shifts and 
local curve deviations were observed. The complete numerical results of 
the temporal-window sensitivity analysis are reported in Supplementary 
Table S13, with supplementary curves for variables ranked 5–14 shown 
in Fig. S5 and the integrated summary shown in Fig. S6. The complete 
numerical results of the distance-threshold sensitivity analysis are re­
ported in Supplementary Table S12, with supplementary curves for 
variables ranked 5–14 shown in Fig. S4 and the integrated summary 
shown in Fig. S6.
