A B S T R A C T
Cycling mitigates urban development-related traffic and environmental issues and benefits human health. 
However, exploring the nonlinear associations between urban environmental factors and cycling remains chal­
lenging. Moreover, the potential of crowdsourced data like Strava Heatmap for cycling research has rarely been 
validated. Using Melbourne as a case study, we assessed the association between urban environmental attributes 
and cycling amount through street view images and artificial intelligence techniques. The results indicate that 
proximity to blue spaces is the most significant factor in promoting cycling amount. Additionally, road network 
density, sky openness, and distance to green spaces each have an optimal threshold. Lastly, built environment 
features, landscape features, and perceived environment are all associated with cycling amount, validating the 
inclusion of both subjective and objective environmental measures in cycling research. These findings provide 
insights and empirical evidence for policymakers in designing bicycle-friendly urban environments.
1. Introduction
Urban bicycle transportation planning has become a core component 
of global urban and regional planning initiatives, aiming to improve the 
well-being of residents (Hoffmann, 2016). However, as urbanization 
accelerates and vehicle numbers rapidly increase, problems like traffic 
congestion, environmental pollution, and energy consumption have 
become more prominent (Erhardt et al., 2019; Yin et al., 2021). Cycling, 
as a sustainable mode of transportation, has garnered extensive atten­
tion and research (Ant´on-Gonz´alez et al., 2023). Compared to mecha­
nized travel methods like private and public transportation, cycling 
provides several advantages, including flexibility, cost-efficiency, health 
benefits, and environmental sustainability (Frank et al., 2022; Iroz- 
Elardo et al., 2020). Consequently, planners and policymakers world­
wide are interested in maximizing cycling amount using limited re­
sources, whether through bike-sharing schemes or bicycle lane design 
(Ahmed et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024; Zhao and Guo, 2024). To promote 
efficient urban sustainable transportation, planners must understand the 
key environmental attributes that influence public cycling amount.
This study employs street view image analysis and machine learning 
to analyze data collected from the central area of Melbourne, Australia. 
Specifically, in this work, we address three research questions: 
• Research question 1 (RQ1): Which urban environmental attributes 
may contribute to increasing public cycling amount?
• Research question 2 (RQ2): Which attributes are more important 
for public cycling amount than others?
• Research question 3 (RQ3): Is there a nonlinear relationship be­
tween urban environmental attributes and cycling amount?
This study makes a threefold contribution to the literature on 
bicycle-friendly urban planning and the practice of slow traffic system 
planning. Firstly, it employs machine learning methods, which are rare 
in this field, to examine public cycling behavior (Shao et al., 2022). More 
importantly, by applying reinforcement learning methods and utilizing 
Strava Heatmap data, it investigates the spatial and environmental im­
pacts on cycling activities on urban streets. This enhances planners’ 
understanding of potential contributors and aids current and future

2. Literature review
2.1. Impacts of street environments on cycling behavior
Given that planning resources are always limited (Dong et al., 
2023b), planners are keen to identify the key correlates of cycling 
amount. Some studies depend on the statistical significance of urban 
built environment features (Hong et al., 2024). While they have found 
strong associations with cycling behavior, this is often insufficient 
because trivial effects can achieve statistical significance if the sample 
size is large enough. Scholars have compared the correlation coefficients 
between urban built environment attributes and cycling behavior 
(Gilliland et al., 2018; Porter et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2019). However, 
bivariate correlations ignore the interactions between various built 
environment attributes, potentially misleading planners regarding their 
relative importance. Hence, multivariate analysis is necessary.
Volunteered Geographic Information (VGI) and GPS devices have 
facilitated the collection of extensive data on trajectories and user be­
haviors (McArthur and Hong, 2019; Zhai et al., 2021), providing op­
portunities to investigate how urban environments influence cycling 
patterns with finer granularities and on a broader scale(Guo and He, 
2021). Existing studies have predominantly utilized macro-level built 
environment metrics, such as land use and Points of Interest (POI)(Qiu 
and Chang, 2021). Yu et al. (2022) examined the association between 
macro- and micro-scale urban built environment attributes and cycling 
behavior. By comparing variable coefficients in linear regressions, their 
results showed that public service facilities had the greatest impact on 
cycling behavior, followed by building area and road density. Moreover, 
in terms of streetscape characteristics, eye-level greening has been found 
to significantly correlate with cycling behavior (Lu et al., 2019; Wang 
et al., 2020), although it is not as influential as other attributes. Given 
the complexity of environmental factors, it is necessary to assess these 
influences on cycling activity comprehensively, from the macro aspects 
of urban built environments to the micro aspects of streetscape 
environments.
It is noteworthy that the significance of cyclists’ perceptions in 
micro-level street environments is often underestimated (Guo and He, 
2021; Gao and Fang, 2025), overlooking their role in moderating the 
impacts of the physical environment on cycling behavior (Wei and Zhu, 
2023). Factors such as perceived land use diversity, proximity to shop­
ping and entertainment venues, and street aesthetics are linked to 
cycling activity (Blitz, 2021). Moreover, streets that exhibit higher vi­
sual complexity and perceived safety attract dockless bikeshare users 
(Song et al., 2024). To enhance cyclists’ safety, various engineering 
measures have been implemented in streetscape designs (Broach et al., 
2012), such as colorful bicycle lane markings and warning signs (Abadi 
and Hurwitz, 2018), which improve their perceived comfort. Impor­
tantly, insights into the effects of perceived environments provide urban 
planners and policymakers with a foundation to implement 
cost-effective streetscape design interventions that improve the cycling 
experience, thus creating more ideal urban streets (Shen et al., 2018). 
However, empirical evidence on how street landscape environments and 
perceived settings specifically influence cycling volumes still requires 
further exploration (Koh and Wong, 2013; Wei and Zhu, 2023; Gao and 
Fang, 2025).
2.2. Non-linear associations with cycling amount
Generally, assessing the impact of urban built environment attributes 
on cycling behavior typically depends on either unstandardized or 
standardized forms of coefficients. This choice depends on whether the 
variables share the same measurement units (Ewing and Cervero, 2010). 
Usually, these coefficients are estimated in models assuming linear re­
lationships between variables (Zhou et al., 2023; Gao and Fang, 2025). 
However, when this assumption is relaxed, the models may reveal 
varying degrees of influence of variables like urban built environment 
attributes on cycling behavior (Yin et al., 2024), potentially altering 
their relative importance.
In early research, most scholars assumed a linear (or generalized 
linear) relationship between urban environmental attributes and cycling 
behavior. For instance, (Ma and Dill, 2015) utilized linear regression 
models to analyze cycling behavior. However, literature on cycling 
studies (Chen and Wei, 2024; Naseri et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), 
including some in the field of urban planning (Wang et al., 2021; Zhou 
et al., 2023), has shown that this assumption does not always hold true 
(Yang et al., 2022). If the relationship between urban built environment 
attributes and cycling volume is nonlinear, assuming a linear relation­
ship can result in biased estimates, thereby impeding planners’ under­
standing of the true associations. Moreover, this erroneous assumption 
could misestimate the relative importance of various urban environ­
mental attributes on cycling frequency or volume (Chen and Wei, 2024; 
Yin et al., 2024), potentially causing planners to misallocate limited 
planning resources.
Recent studies have relaxed the linear assumption, finding wide­
spread nonlinear associations between the urban environment and 
cycling amount probability (Yang et al., 2024). For example, (Yin et al., 
2024) explored the use of SHapley Additive exPlanations based on 
Shapley values from game theory to investigate the impact of multiple 
environmental features on cycling. They demonstrated that attributes 
such as direct distance, property prices, density of points of interest, 
proximity to subways, building area/road density, and urban greenery 
exhibit significant nonlinear effects. Particularly, some studies have 
emphasized that nonlinear methods are better suited for predicting 
cycling frequency (Wang et al., 2021), thus promoting land use and 
transportation planning strategies (Yin et al., 2021).
2.3. Potential of machine learning and crowdsourced data
With the development of GPS technology, big data mining, and 
machine learning algorithms, the methodologies in urban studies have 
undergone a transformation. Traditional approaches, which relied on 
small sample surveys and basic statistical analyses, have gradually 
shifted towards leveraging urban big data and machine learning models 
(Lu, 2019; Yang et al., 2023). This transformation has stimulated more 
granular and large-scale urban research (Biljecki and Ito, 2021; Liu 
et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b), such as the impact of the urban built 
environment on customized bus services (Wang et al., 2023). This shift 
not only improves research efficiency but also enhances the under­
standing of complex urban dynamics. In particular, integrating machine 
learning methods has significantly reduced the time costs associated 
with traditional desktop-based environmental audit tools (Biljecki and 
Ito, 2021). The spectrum of machine learning algorithms has advanced 
from traditional methods such as support vector machines, Bayesian 
algorithms, and decision trees to ensemble learning algorithms known 
for their high predictive accuracy (Zhang et al., 2024b). As a data-driven 
black-box model, machine learning excels in modeling complex 
nonlinear patterns and has gained prominence across various fields

machine learning to the transportation field, such as in travel behavior

can accurately predict travel mode choices (Rasouli and Timmermans, 
2014; Yang et al., 2023). Nevertheless, despite their excellent predictive 
performance, these black-box models frequently lack interpretability, 
which can generally be improved through post-hoc explanations.
Furthermore, cycling routes and check-in information from mobile 
fitness applications form an innovative open crowdsourced dataset, 
voluntarily gathered and shared by users (Hong et al., 2020). These 
datasets offer extensive data with more precise geographic location 
tracking, free from recall bias (Lee and Kwan, 2018). Consequently, 
researchers can utilize crowdsourced data to map the geographic dis­
tribution and intensity of activities on streets. Semi-open data sources 
like Strava Heatmap provide a potential solution for utilizing large 
sample sizes of individual GPS routes in activity studies (Dong et al., 
2023a).
3. Methods
3.1. Study area
In this study, the inner city of Melbourne, Australia (Ma et al., 2023), 
was selected as the case study location based on official boundary data, 
as illustrated in Fig. 1. Melbourne’s inner city is among the regions with 
the highest cycling frequency in Australia (Pistoll and Goodman, 2014). 
This area features a high-density, mixed-use core, surrounded by sig­
nificant urban expansion (Pistoll and Goodman, 2014). Additionally, the 
flat terrain and mild climate of the inner city provide ideal conditions for 
year-round cycling (Pucher et al., 2011). Consequently, Melbourne 
presents an attractive case for examining the relationship between the 
urban environment and public cycling behavior.
3.2. Data collection
3.2.1. Cycling amount
We utilized crowdsourced GPS-based Strava data to depict the 
cycling trajectories of Strava users. As a highly reliable source of phys­
ical activity data, Strava has been extensively used in cycling research 
(Hong et al., 2020). Although it is acknowledged that Strava data may 
include records of active commuting and competitive cycling, it pre­
dominantly encompasses travel and sport cycling. Consequently, our 
quantification of cycling data is focused on the latter.
These data reflect the cumulative amount of cycling activity through 
varying brightness levels, where brighter colors indicate higher cycling 
frequencies in that area (Dong et al., 2023a). This correspondence be­
tween color brightness and activity intensity makes Strava heatmaps an 
effective tool for analyzing cycling hotspots and spatial preferences. 
Consequently, we extracted cycling raster values from the Strava heat­
map and created a 20-m buffer, which served as a proxy indicator for 
quantifying the frequency of cycling activity. The 20-m buffer was 
chosen for its superior explanatory power in our study model (Dong

3.2.2. Feature extraction
Based on existing research (Song et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023), 
environmental features are considered potential predictors that may 
influence cycling amount. These features are detailed in Table 1 and are 
classified into two main categories based on their nature: objective 
features and subjective features. Objective features primarily include 
built environment features and landscape features, whereas subjective 
features encompass personal perceptions of the urban street 
environment.
Objective built environment features were selected based on 
(Renalds et al., 2010) four-dimension (4D) framework, encompassing 
the indicators of “Density,” “Design,” “Diversity,” and “Destination.” We 
innovatively created a 200-m buffer around urban street views to 
comprehensively capture the built environment, addressing boundary 
effect issues and ensuring accurate spatial relationships. This approach 
takes into account the Uncertain Geographic Context Problem, which 
posits that the impact of area-based attributes may be influenced by the 
geographic delineation of environmental units (Guo et al., 2021).
Landscape features were obtained using the Google Street View 
Image (SVI) application programming interface. Sample points were 
taken at 50-m intervals along street segments from OpenStreetMap. For 
each sample point, SVIs were captured in four directions (bearings of 0◦, 
90◦, 180◦, and 270◦) to cover the cyclist’s entire field of view, as shown 
in Fig. 3 (a). After excluding missing points or indoor images, we secured 
35,416 valid SVIs from a total of 8854 sample points. Objective land­
scape features are typically represented as the pixel ratio of visual ele­
ments in SVIs, a common method to highlight the importance of eye- 
level perspectives in activity studies (Ito and Biljecki, 2021). We 
utilized DeepLabV3+, a computer vision model well-known for its ac­
curacy in extracting and measuring objective visual indicators, to clas­
sify and analyze the SVI (Qiu et al., 2022). Using the ADE20K dataset to 
predict object categories in urban landscapes as shown in Fig. 3 (b), the 
semantic segmentation achieved a pixel accuracy exceeding 93.4 % 
(Dong et al., 2023a; Ki and Lee, 2021).
Perceived environmental features were assessed using a Convolu­
tional Neural Network (CNN) model applied to street view images, 
suitable for urban settings with high spatial heterogeneity. We utilized 
the Place Pulse 2.0 dataset from MIT Media Lab to predict six perceptual 
scores at each sample point within Melbourne’s inner city, as shown in 
Fig. 3 (c). This dataset is renowned as the largest training dataset for 
urban perception based on scene understanding algorithms (Ito and 
Biljecki, 2021; Zhang et al., 2018), encompassing six perceptual attri­
butes: wealthy, safe, lively, beautiful, boring, and depressing. This 
provides a comprehensive framework for assessing perceptual charac­
teristics. The predictive model’s street view index provided six percep­
tual scores, and we calculated the average score for each attribute at 
each sample location. These six perceptual indicators, encompassing 
both positive and negative perceptions, have been employed in previous 
studies to gauge general human perceptions (Zhang et al., 2018). 
Particularly, cyclists’ perceptions of risk and safety are crucially 
important (Zhang et al., 2024a).
3.3. Model development
3.3.1. Data pre-processing
During feature selection, variables with a p-value greater than 0.05 
were removed following Pearson correlation analysis, as illustrated in 
Fig. 4. Subsequently, Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) models 
were used for preliminary prediction and selection. The predictive 
performance of variables was then evaluated based on multivariate 
regression models and machine learning regression algorithms. Addi­
tional analysis involved inspecting the learning curves and cross- 
validation outcomes of different models. This process was imple­
mented using scikit-learn version 1.0.2, a highly popular machine 
learning library (Pedregosa et al., 2011).

For choosing predictors, the tree-based feature selection method

In constructing the Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model, we first 
excluded all independent variables with p-values greater than 0.05, 
ensuring the model only contained statistically significant predictors. 
Additionally, to prevent multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor 
(VIF) for any independent variable was required to be less than 3.
3.3.2. Algorithm selection and model evaluation
For the predictive analysis of structured data, this study employed 
five ensemble learning algorithms, which are particularly suited for 
handling such data. These algorithms all use Decision Tree as the base 
model and are divided into two categories: Bagging-based methods, 
including Random Forest and Extra-Trees; and Boosting-based methods, 
encompassing LightGBM, AdaBoost, and XGBoost. To ensure the effec­
tiveness and generalizability of the model training, the dataset was split 
into a training set and a test set in an 80 % to 20 % ratio, thus ensuring 
the predictive accuracy and robustness of the algorithms.
To evaluate the performance of the models, we used R-squared and 
RMSE as evaluation metrics. R-squared measures the proportion of 
variance explained by the model, while RMSE assesses the error between 
the predicted values and the actual values. We compared the model 
performance using the R-squared and RMSE on the test set. The “Shuffle 
Split Cross Validation” method was employed for data splitting (with a 
split ratio of 8:2) to analyze the generalization ability of the five models 
using R-square as the evaluation metric. The randomness of this method 
ensures the stability of the cross-validation scores and mitigates the 
accidental errors caused by uneven data splits, thus providing an unbi­
ased error estimate. Through learning curves, we further observed the 
changes in RMSE for both the training and test sets as the sample size 
increased, to assess the models’ adaptability to new data.

4.2. Nonlinear effects on cycling amount
Sensitivity analysis is crucial for evaluating the influence of predictor 
variables on the dependent variable and identifying key features. This 
evaluation involves analyzing feature importance and Partial Depen­
dence Plot (PDP). In the RFR model, the distance to blue spaces has the 
greatest impact, followed by built environment features such as building 
density, intersection density, and road network density. Urban greenery 
features, including SVI_grass, street greening, and distance to green 
spaces, along with sky openness, collectively account for over 80 % of 
the influence on cycling amount, as shown in Table 2.
PDP are employed to demonstrate the relationship between depen­
dent and independent variables as interpreted by the machine learning 
model, showing the average response for each value of the independent 
variable (Friedman, 2001). These PDPs are augmented by Individual 
Conditional Expectation plots, which illustrate the dependency of the 
target function on the input features of interest, visualizing the de­
pendency of each sample’s prediction on the feature independently. 
Fig. 6 presents the PDP based on the RFR model, depicting the nonlinear 
relationships between features and cycling amount. Due to the relatively 
minor influence of perceived environmental features such as aesthetics, 
liveliness, and bicycle facilities on cycling amount, these were excluded 
from the PDP analysis.
Based on the univariate PDP in Fig. 6(b), when the distance to blue 
spaces exceeds 500 m, the cycling amount tends to stabilize. This reveals 
that the influence of proximity to blue spaces on cycling amount is most 
significant within 500 m, with a stronger preference for closer distances. 
Beyond 500 m, the cycling amount remains stable.
The cycling amount decreases with increasing road network density 
and intersection density. Specifically, when the building density is 
around 450,000 m2/km2, the cycling amount reaches its lowest point 
and remains stable. Conversely, the cycling amount is highest when the 
road network density is at 10 km/km2. For street greening, the cycling 
amount initially increases with an increase in street greening but then 
decreases, with the optimal level being around 160 trees within a 200-m 
buffer. This indicates the presence of an optimal amount of street trees 
for cycling. Additionally, the impact of the distance to green spaces on 
cycling amount is significant at both very close and very distant ranges.

5. Discussion
5.1. Contributions of objective and perceived environmental attributes to 
cycling
Firstly, objective built environment attributes demonstrated a strong 
overall advantage in explaining cycling amount, consistent with previ­
ous findings on running volume using crowdsourced data from Strava 
and Keep (Dong et al., 2023a; Zhang et al., 2024b). However, the col­
lective effect indicates that subjective perceptions can complement 
objective environmental features in influencing Melbourne’s cycling 
amount. This confirms the importance for transportation planners to 
consider both aspects when planning for cycling.
Secondly, according to the feature importance rankings derived from 
the Random Forest model, built environment characteristics have a 
stronger explanatory power than landscape features. Specifically, blue 
space distance, road network density, and intersection density are more 
critical in contributing to public cycling amount than other attributes. 
Notably, the distance to blue spaces has the most significant impact on 
cycling amount, likely due to the sense of safety and mental restoration 
offered by proximity to water bodies (e.g., coasts, rivers) (Pasanen et al., 
2019). Additionally, landscape features also significantly explain 
cycling amount, indicating that the landscape of urban cycling routes 
should not be underestimated.
Thirdly, SVIsky openness, street greening, and road network density 
exhibit peaks in the PDP, suggesting that these environmental charac­
teristics have optimal values. For instance, the optimal sky ratio when 
cycling amount is at its maximum is 0.3, implying an ideal height-to- 
width ratio in the street environment cross-section (Zhang et al., 2024b).
Lastly, subjective perception attributes, though less contributive 
than objective features, maintain significance in multiple models, with 
‘beautiful’ and ‘lively’ consistently important. However, other perceived 
environmental features do not enhance model performance, suggesting 
a potential conflict between subjective perceptions and objective envi­
ronmental characteristics in explaining cycling amount. Despite this, 
subjective perceptions still play a complementary role in deepening our 
understanding of cycling behaviors.
5.2. Implications for urban transportation planning and policy
Our research provides valuable insights into the use of artificial in­
telligence technologies and crowdsourced data to evaluate the impact of 
urban cycling environments, incorporating both subjective and objec­
tive measurements, to guide the study of cycling behavior. The findings 
offer practical references for planners and policymakers to enhance 
cycling amount by designing more cycling-friendly urban environments. 
These policy initiatives encompass a comprehensive consideration of 
both objective factors and subjective experiences.
Firstly, our study demonstrates that both objective and subjective 
urban environments significantly influence cycling amount. Planners 
and designers should focus not only on the macro-scale built environ­
ment but also on landscape and perceptual environments. For example, 
lower building and intersection densities, improved accessibility to blue 
spaces, relatively wider streets, and broader views of grasslands can 
increase the likelihood and frequency of cycling. Our results support the 
notion that planning feasible cycling routes and improving urban 
streetscapes can be an effective and economical method for promoting 
cycling activity and enhancing public health. This approach is poten­
tially less expensive and easier to implement than constructing large 
public infrastructure such as indoor sports arenas, and it may also reduce 
public healthcare costs (Thompson, 2011). Additionally, it can foster a 
more cyclist-friendly urban transportation environment.
Secondly, from the perspective of transportation design guidelines, 
the planning of cycling routes should consider areas with high inter­
section and road densities to ensure continuity and a pleasant cycling

Finally, by leveraging crowdsourced data and machine learning, our

Considering the characteristics of crowdsourced data and street-view

First, although Strava data have been validated for cycling research,

As a result, they may overlook everyday cycling behaviors such as

deeper insights into cyclists’ experiences on city streets and informing 
policymakers as they regulate the urban cycling environment. Future 
research should further investigate how urban cycling infrastructure can 
be designed and configured to optimally promote cycling. In particular, 
examining how cycling behaviors evolve over time, especially under 
varying social and personal pressures, can inform the development of 
more adaptive and supportive transportation environments for cycling 
activities.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we utilized cycling data from the Strava Heatmap to 
thoroughly analyze the spatial patterns of cycling amount in relation to 
urban environmental attributes. This research not only focused on the 
impacts of objective environmental factors but also considered visible 
landscape features and subjective perceptions, thus enriching the 
cycling literature and providing policy recommendations to promote 
active transportation.
Firstly, we identified key urban environmental features that influ­
ence cycling amount, revealing the nonlinear and threshold effects of 
these environmental characteristics on cycling. Secondly, for coastal 
cities, blue spaces emerged as the most significant factor in promoting 
cycling behavior, providing crucial evidence for enhancing the cycling 
experience through transportation environment transformation. Lastly, 
we utilized partial dependence plots to elucidate the nonlinear re­
lationships between environmental features and cycling amount, offer­
ing design guidelines for urban slow traffic system planning.
Outdoor cycling is not merely a lifestyle choice; it reflects public 
demands for environmental health and quality. As one of the most 
widespread and active types of behavior trajectories, cycling holds sig­
nificant research relevance and optimization value. Studies on cycling- 
friendly streets can aid planners and policymakers in improving the 
environment, distribution, and quality of cycling routes. This, in turn, 
can stimulate public cycling activities and advance the development and 
implementation of healthy urban transportation systems.

A B S T R A C T
Urban green spaces (UGSs) are major settings of human–nature interaction and important to public well-being. 
Visitation and satisfaction are indicators that reflect the utilization degree of and feedback regarding UGSs. The 
focus of this study is to analyze the visitation and satisfaction characteristics of different types of UGSs, analyze 
the dominant factors affecting these indicators, and rank their importance to provide strategies for improving 
management. Here, 50 typical UGSs in Shanghai are selected. All-subset regression, hierarchical partitioning 
analysis and other methods are comprehensively used to explore the influencing factors. The results are as 
follows: 1) Comprehensive parks have the highest visitation rates, whereas community parks have the lowest 
visitation rates but the highest satisfaction rates. 2) UGS size has the greatest influence on visitation, followed by 
connectivity, building shape index, and edge density, with contributions of 69.35 %, 16.40 %, 8.50 %, and 5.75 
%, respectively. 3) Transportation facility density and edge density have the greatest influences on satisfaction, 
with contributions of 51.49 % and 48.51 %, respectively. In this study, the applicability of using multisource data 
to analyze UGS attributes and their factors affecting visitation and satisfaction are demonstrated. Targeted 
strategies for constructing UGSs will help authorities plan and manage UGSs effectively.
1. Introduction
Urbanization is a global trend that is changing the relationship be­
tween humans and the natural environment. It is estimated that by 
2050, more than 68 % of the world's population will live in cities (United 
Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Divi­
sion, 2018). In cities, human contact with nature occurs through outdoor 
activities, mostly in small and heterogeneous urban green spaces (UGSs) 
(Belaire et al., 2022; Fang et al., 2023). The development of UGSs fo­
cuses on the role of complex ecological networks in biodiversity, culture, 
urban safety, and meeting the recreational needs of citizens (Palliwoda 
& Priess, 2021). Since 2020, rapid urbanization and the COVID-19 
pandemic have led to travel restrictions and an increasing demand for 
UGSs, which have become major locations for human–nature in­
teractions (Lopez et al., 2021; Noszczyk et al., 2022). UGSs provide 
places where people can engage in outdoor activities, physical exercise, 
nature education, and other activities that are beneficial to physical and 
mental health; thus, these spaces play a role in the self-realization of 
UGS users and contribute to achieving the United Nation's sustainable 
development goals (Addas & Maghrabi, 2022; Tate et al., 2024; Yang 
et al., 2022). To improve urban livability and social well-being (Gozalo 
et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024; Taczanowska et al., 2024), we need to un­
derstand how people use and perceive UGSs.
Visitation and satisfaction are indicators that reflect the degree of 
utilization and feedback on UGSs and are currently research hotspots 
(Ghahramani et al., 2021; Huai et al., 2023). UGS visitation reflects UGS 
popularity; it is usually collected through on-site surveys, telephone or 
online questionnaires or by integrating a large number of data points 
from multiple sources (such as tickets, ferry tickets, etc.) (Kim et al., 
2023; Liu et al., 2017). However, obtaining these data takes substantial

an overall psychological state described by the fulfillment of one's ex­
pectations or needs (Sun & Shao, 2020). UGS satisfaction refers to the 
feedback, attitudes and positive or negative feelings of tourists and can 
reflect the quality of the UGS's facilities and services, the degree to 
which tourists' needs are met, and the attraction of UGSs. The evaluation 
of satisfaction focuses on participatory methods, including question­
naires, interviews, and participatory geographic information system 
(PGIS) (Korpilo et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021; Rall et al., 2019); however, 
the sample size of such methods is relatively small. To better reflect the 
perceptual experience of the public and expand the scope of data 
collection, web-based big data provide high-quality data sources 
(Heikinheimo et al., 2020). People use online social tools to record their 
feelings and opinions in texts, photos, and other forms of expression, 
resulting in a large amount of public opinion and perception data 
(Foltˆete et al., 2020). Texts are helpful in exploring the public's positive 
and negative emotions and well-being through the use of various sce­
narios (Tenerelli et al., 2016), and photo data convey figurative 
emotional expressions (Oteros-Rozas et al., 2018; Vaz et al., 2020). So­
cial media supports the sharing of UGS name information and provides 
users with accurate location services, and these data can be analyzed to 
provide geocoded points for visitation and expression of spatial expe­
riences (Chang & Olafsson, 2022). Many platforms support options for 
users to check in or comment, and the number of these user interactions 
can be used to study user behavior, geospatial distribution, and usage 
information (Toivonen et al., 2019). Therefore, the number of reviews in 
social media data with geotagged or spatial information may represent 
visitation, while sentiment analysis of review texts may represent 
satisfaction. In addition, UGSs can be divided into different types ac­
cording to their area and positioning, such as comprehensive parks, 
community parks, and theme parks. A classified analysis of visitation 
and satisfaction characteristics of various UGS will help management 
departments understand the gap between management and citizens' 
needs, thereby improving management levels.
Linking multisource data to UGS attributes helps elucidate the fac­
tors influencing visitation and satisfaction and provides information for 
management and planning design (Baumeister et al., 2020). With the 
rise of big data, points of interest, OpenStreetMap, remote sensing im­
ages, street view images and other types of data are increasingly used in 
research for analyzing influencing factors (Grzyb et al., 2021; Huai & 
Van de Voorde, 2022). Generally, UGS attributes can be roughly divided 
into the following three categories: (1) Landscape patterns. The area 
typically determines the level and function of a UGS (Ha et al., 2022), 
and the types of landscape elements are quite different (Wang et al., 
2022), particularly for natural landscapes, including plants and water 
bodies (Li et al., 2022; Samus et al., 2022), which have a considerable 
impact on public perception. Landscape patterns, such as landscape 
diversity (Feng & Astell-Burt, 2022), heterogeneity (Xie et al., 2023), 
connectivity (Dong et al., 2020), and shape (Liebelt et al., 2018), may 
affect the landscape hierarchy and visual effects of UGSs, thereby 
affecting landscape perception. (2) Ecological effects. The normalized 
difference vegetation index (NDVI) is positively correlated with 
emotional recovery and self-rated health (Zhang et al., 2022), and the 
cold island effect can improve pedestrian comfort (Liu et al., 2024). (3) 
Socioeconomic effects. Public perception is spatially dependent, with 
residents considering travel convenience and cost, distance from the city 
center, and accessibility among the factors influencing perception (Dong 
et al., 2024; Phillips et al., 2022). Some UGSs are adjacent to industries, 
such as catering, commerce, and entertainment businesses, and the 
accessibility of UGSs and supporting facilities affects the public's eval­
uation of the social equity of these spaces (Boone et al., 2021). Popu­
lation density and crowding risk within the areas covered by UGSs affect 
public satisfaction (Annerstedt van den Bosch et al., 2016). It is known 
that UGS visitation and satisfaction are affected by many driving factors; 
however, few studies have focused on the dominant factors affecting 
these two indicators and their importance rankings.
The aim of this study is to understand the connections among attri­
butes, visitation, and satisfaction and to address the following questions: 
(1) Based on social media data, what are the differences in visitation and 
satisfaction among different types of UGSs? (2) How can we analyze the 
dominant factors influencing UGS attributes on visitation and satisfac­
tion based on multisource data and rank their importance? Here, an 
attempt is made to provide managers and landscape architects with 
optimal UGS information from the perspective of public feedback, 
thereby contributing to improvements in UGS usage and public 
satisfaction.
2. Methods
2.1. Study area
Shanghai is the most populous city in China, with a resident popu­
lation of approximately 24.89 million as of 2021 and a UGS coverage 
area of 1712.15 km2, which is a relatively high level of green space 
coverage among cities in China. Urban development and population 
growth have put pressure on UGS construction. Therefore, we conduct a 
comprehensive analysis of UGSs in Shanghai to obtain representative 
information.
According to the “Urban Green Space Classification Standard (CJJ/ 
T85-2017)” (Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the 
People's Republic of China, 2017), we selected 50 UGSs with high visi­
bility and a high number of comments from social media as our research 
objects (Fig. 1). The UGSs can be divided into the following four types: 
comprehensive parks, community parks, theme parks, and regional 
green spaces, the numbers of which are 15, 22, 11 and 2, respectively. 
Comprehensive parks are green spaces with areas of >10 ha and com­
plete recreational and supporting facilities. Community parks are green 
spaces with areas of 1–10 ha that mainly provide daily leisure services 
for residents within the community. Theme parks are green spaces with 
specific contents or appearances, including historical gardens, children's 
parks, and sports parks. Regional green spaces are located outside urban 
construction land and have functions, such as recreation and protection. 
These spaces are open to the public of all ages and are widely distributed 
across 10 administrative districts of Shanghai, with sizes ranging from 
1.15 to 283.41 ha. Details of each UGS are provided in Table A.1.
2.2. Research design
Fig. 2 shows the workflow developed for the case study. First, visi­
tation and satisfaction, as reflected on social media, are quantified for 
each UGS using a model based on comment volume and deep learning. 
Second, the UGS attributes of landscape patterns, ecological effects, and 
socioeconomic effects are summarized using 34 indicators (Annerstedt 
van den Bosch et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020). Finally, factors influencing 
UGS visitation and satisfaction are identified using statistical analysis 
methods, such as all-subset regression and hierarchical partitioning 
analysis. For a list of the data used in this study, please see Table A.2.
2.3. Quantification of visitation and satisfaction
Dianping (https://www.dianping.com/) is one of the most popular 
social media platforms in China, enabling users to publicly share text or 
photos of their comments based on the type of venue they have visited. 
Others can view these records on the platform, which has become an 
important source of data for urban infrastructure analyses in recent 
years. The comment data were obtained through an application pro­
gramming interface (API). We established a team of experts to randomly 
check and verify the results through the Delphi method to delete 
duplicate and irrelevant records and improve accuracy. A total of 
111,814 valid comments from 2016 to 2020 were collected for the 50 
UGSs investigated in this study. The number of comments on each UGS 
was counted and represents visitation.

2.4. Analysis of UGS attributes
We explored the landscape element ratio, facility layout, and site 
selection scheme used in UGS planning and management to construct 34 
indicators of landscape patterns, ecological effects, and socioeconomic 
effects describing UGS attributes (Tables 1, A.3).
In terms of landscape patterns, land use was classified into six types, 
including trees and shrubs, lawns, water bodies, roads and squares, 
buildings, and sports fields, based on the green space land type data 
provided by the Shanghai Landscaping and City Appearance Adminis­
trative Bureau and combined with the visual interpretation of Gaofen-6 
images with a 2-m resolution. The field survey data were processed in 
ArcGIS 10.6 to obtain a digital map of the distribution of landscape el­
ements in the surveyed UGSs, and the landscape pattern index was 
calculated using Fragstats 4.2.1. Openness was calculated using Baidu 
Street View data to identify UGS boundaries and entrances/exits.
In terms of ecological effects, the NDVI was calculated using clear 
and cloudless Sentinel-2 images typical of four seasons with a 10-m 
resolution. The surface temperature was inverted, and the UGS tem­
perature regulation was further analyzed using Landsat-8 remote 
sensing images with a 30-m resolution.
In terms of socioeconomic effects, point-of-interest data were ob­
tained using the API of the AutoNavi Open Platform, and transportation 
facilities, catering, sports, and shopping facilities in the database were 
extracted as the analysis objects. A 500-m buffer of walking roads was 
applied to the OpenStreetMap road data, and the density of each indi­
cator was calculated using ArcGIS. WorldPop 2020 data were used for 
population coverage. The distance from the city center was measured 
using the Shanghai Park Hotel as the origin, and the straight-line dis­
tance to each UGS center-of-mass point was calculated.
2.5. Analysis of influencing factors
First, the factors of UGS features influencing the number of com­
ments and level of satisfaction were identified separately. Pearson cor­
relation analysis was carried out between the UGS attributes and two 
indicators (comment volume and satisfaction). Due to the excessive 
number of factors considered, some factors contain explanatory infor­
mation similar to the dependent variable, resulting in a strong 
correlation between factors. According to Fox and Weisberg (2018) and 
this study, a Pearson correlation coefficient >0.7 indicates a strong 
correlation between two groups of variables, which are selected ac­
cording to the actual meanings of the indicators or the degrees to which 
they contribute to the interpretation of the results. A correlation matrix 
was constructed to determine collinearity, and factors with variance 
inflation factors (VIFs) <10 were selected to avoid the problem of factor 
collinearity (Murtagh & Heck, 2012).
Second, optimal regression models are constructed. The most com­
mon method for multivariate analysis is multiple linear regression; 
however, this method uses only a single influencing factor to construct a 
regression model and cannot consider all possible combinations of fac­
tors. This study used an all-subset regression to overcome this limitation. 
The top-ranked models were selected according to the adjusted R2, and 
the model with the smallest Akaike information criterion (AIC) value 
was selected to explain the greatest variance with the fewest indepen­
dent variables (Akaike, 1974).
Finally, the contribution of the dominant factor was identified for the 
optimal equation. It is difficult to clarify the relative importance of all 
factors using stepwise regression, while hierarchical partitioning anal­
ysis is more suitable for multidimensional landscape data analysis to 
obtain independent importance values of factors. Hierarchical parti­
tioning was used to identify the independent effects of the explanatory 
variables in the optimal equation (Walsh & MacNally, 2020) and to rank 
the importance of the influencing factors and determine their contri­
butions. The above analysis was performed using R4.2.1.
3. Results
3.1. Descriptive analysis of UGS visitation and satisfaction
There was an average of 2236 comments for the 50 UGSs. Compre­
hensive parks have the highest average number of comments (3227), 
followed by theme parks (3077), regional green spaces (2607), and 
community parks (1100). Among the four UGSs with the highest number 
of comments, Gucun Park (14,700), Century Park (10,500), and Daning 
Park (7470) are comprehensive parks, and Gongqing Forest Park 
(13,000) is a theme park. The remaining 46 UGSs each had fewer than 
5000 comments, of which 12 had 2000–4999 comments, 10 had 
1000–1999 comments, 16 had 500–999 comments, and eight had fewer 
than 500 comments.
The satisfaction scores were between 0.52 and 0.94, with a mean of 
0.716 and a variance of 0.007, indicating that the overall satisfaction

4. Discussion
4.1. UGS visitation and satisfaction characteristics based on social media 
data
This study analyzes the characteristics of UGS visitation and satis­
faction based on social media data. From the perspective of data sources, 
previous research has found strong correlations between social media 
data and official visitation statistics (Tenkanen et al., 2017); however, 
there has been insufficient research on satisfaction. Huai et al. (2023)
studied UGSs in Shanghai and found that comprehensive parks had the 
highest level of satisfaction, which is different from the results of this 
study. For texts expressing low satisfaction with comprehensive parks, 
we found that by searching for high-frequency words, the main reason 
was insufficient management of public recreation-related needs. For 
example, outdoor camping has gradually emerged in these areas in 
recent years; however, UGS managers prohibit tourists from entering the 
lawn, which reduces the opportunities for tourists to engage in recrea­
tional activities. Therefore, social media data are still worthy of dis­
cussion in terms of their representativeness of satisfaction.
The period changes displayed by social media data reveal the phe­
nomenon of seasonal fluctuations and the dynamic changes in tourist 
behavior patterns behind these fluctuations, which can also provide a 
basis for UGS flow detection and management (Zhao et al., 2020). The 
outdoor thermal comfort standard recommended by the World Health 
Organization (WHO) (2007) is approximately 18 ◦C, which corresponds 
to the most comfortable month with the average temperature in 
Shanghai occurring in April–May and October–November (Fig. C.1). 
This period is consistent with the highest number of reviews in this 
study. Simultaneously, climate differences create basic conditions for 
seasonal fluctuations in UGS landscapes. The seasonal landscapes are the 
most distinctive in spring and autumn. UGSs often have plant viewing or 
festival activities in these two seasons. In the study area, Gucun Park has 
the Cherry Blossom Festival from March to April; Century Park has the 
Spring Flower Tour from March to April, mainly creating landscapes 
with cherry blossoms, viburnums, and rapeseed flowers; the Golden 
Autumn Garden Season is held in October to create ginkgo landscapes; 
and Guilin Park has the Osmanthus Festival from September to October. 
Climate and cultural activities have important impacts on visits to and 
satisfaction with UGSs, and other related studies have reached similar 
conclusions (Guan et al., 2021; Liang & Zhang, 2021).
4.2. Factors influencing UGS visitation and satisfaction
This study revealed that the main factors influencing visitation are 
UGS size, connectivity, the building shape index, and edge density. The 
size of the UGS and the visibility are significantly positively correlated, 
with large UGSs typically having a richer native landscape, some aspects 
of wild nature, abundant vegetation, walking trails, water features, and 
wildlife, such as birds. The strong appeal of this natural landscape may 
attract nature-oriented users to make a special trip to such a UGS 
(Phillips et al., 2022). Larger UGSs have lower internal temperatures, 
with aggregated and compact patches of the same landscape type 
(Zhang & Zhou, 2018), thereby attracting more people. Users of larger 
UGSs can stay longer and engage in more types of outdoor activities, 
contributing to greater social cohesion (Kingsbury et al., 2020; Peters 
et al., 2010) and better mental health and well-being (Ayala-Azc´arraga 
et al., 2019). Well-connected natural spaces can provide people with 
greater access to biodiverse environments (Ha et al., 2022). In addition, 
good connectivity encourages occasional users, such as cyclists and 
runners, to spend more time in UGSs, and green corridors can reduce 
stress and negative emotional states (Marselle et al., 2015). Buildings 
and structures are important vehicles for displaying a city's history and 
culture, and historical relics with unique elements of the city 
(Baumeister et al., 2020) in the green landscape significantly influence 
public perception.
This study revealed that the main factors influencing satisfaction are 
transportation facility density and edge density. In terms of trans­
portation facilities, relevant studies have shown that the number of bus 
stops, subway stations, and parking lots around UGSs is positively 
correlated with utilization (Fan et al., 2021; Hamstead et al., 2018). 
Therefore, UGS accessibility has a positive impact on satisfaction 
(Ugolini et al., 2022). Edge density generally reflects the heterogeneity 
of space and is positively correlated with satisfaction (Li et al., 2023). 
Some studies have shown that compared to a single, large UGS, several 
scattered, small UGSs can more effectively alleviate the psychological 
distress experienced by the public (Ha et al., 2022). In addition, in the 
correlation analysis, this study finds that satisfaction and service facility 
density are positively correlated. UGSs have become urban green com­
plexes due to their diverse functions at the city's strategic level, 
including their ecology, landscape, recreation, and culture. People can

4.3. Suggestions for UGS management
By analyzing the factors affecting UGS visitation and satisfaction, we 
can understand the gap between urban management and resident ex­
pectations and then guide and refine the management of UGSs. While 
large UGSs are less common in densely populated urban centers, small 
UGSs, such as community parks, can promote the daily experience of 
being outdoors and have better accessibility (Peschardt et al., 2012), 
thus enhancing social interaction and cohesion among community res­
idents (Ekkel & de Vries, 2017). Therefore, small, well-managed UGSs 
with convenient transportation are very important for the recreational 
activities of nearby residents. UGSs, from the theme planning of large 
forest parks and country parks to the site selection of small street and 
waterfront UGSs, should be built and managed in a targeted manner 
according to the usage features, frequency, and perceived characteristics 
of various groups of people (Wang et al., 2023). The existing UGSs can 
increase the frequency of public visits by hosting festivals and plant- 
viewing activities (Li et al., 2023). Moreover, the edges of plants, 
water bodies, and roads can be constructed with increased curvature, 
and buildings in UGSs, such as museums and art galleries, can be 
designed with complex shapes to increase the public perception of UGS 
recreation services.
In future planning, the accessibility of UGSs should receive increased 
attention from decision makers. The regional road network, especially 
bicycle infrastructure, can be strengthened, bus and subway lines and 
stations can be rationally arranged (Yang et al., 2022), and the parking 
lot layout can be modified to improve transportation convenience and 
public satisfaction (Gorzelany et al., 2023). Moreover, more attention 
should be given to the surrounding commercial, service, and other 
supporting facilities (Zhong et al., 2020) for new or renovated UGSs.
5. Conclusions
The research questions posed in this study have been answered. For 
question one, comprehensive parks have the highest number of reviews 
and lower-than-average satisfaction, while community parks have the 
lowest number of visits but the highest satisfaction. Regarding the sec­
ond question, the factor most influencing visitation factor is UGS size, 
with an independent contribution of 69.35 %, followed by connectivity, 
the building shape index, and edge density, with independent contri­
butions of 16.40 %, 8.50 %, and 5.75 %, respectively. The factors 
influencing satisfaction are transportation facility density and edge 
density, with independent contributions of 51.49 % and 48.51 %, 
respectively.
Based on the results, we provide several recommendations for UGS 
planners and decision-makers: (1) In the UGS system, the roles played by 
small, easily accessible UGSs and large, well-functioning UGSs overlap 
and complement each other. (2) UGSs should be constructed to improve 
their openness, accessibility, and proximity from the perspective of 
serving urban residents.
Our study area covers only formal UGSs, while informal green spaces 
(e.g., wastelands and brownfields) are not included, although these 
spaces are also recognized for their recreational potential. In the future, 
additional crowdsourced data can be considered, and the research scope 
can be expanded to include different types of public recreational areas; 
visitation, satisfaction or other dimensions can be analyzed; and the 
influencing factors can be explored to provide more effective informa­
tion for urban public space design practices.

A B S T R A C T 
COVID-19 restrictions imposed significant changes on human mobility patterns, with some studies finding sig­
nificant increases or decreases in cycling. However, to date there is little understanding on how the 
neighbourhood-level built environment influenced cycling behaviour during the COVID-19 restrictions. As 
different neighbourhood have different built environment characteristics, it is possible that cycling trends varied 
across different built environment settings. We aimed to answer this question by examining recreational cycling 
during different stages of lockdown in Melbourne, Australia. We compared self-reported recreational cycling 
frequency (weekly) data from 1344 respondents between pre-COVID and two different stages in lockdown. We 
tested whether the built environment of their residential neighbourhood and different sociodemographic char­
acteristics influenced leisure cycling rates and whether the effect of these factors varied between different stages 
of COVID-19 restriction. We found that cycling declined significantly during the two stages of COVID-19 lock­
down. Cycling infrastructure density and connectivity are two built environment factors that had a significant 
effect on limiting the decline in leisure cycling during the pandemic. Furthermore, men and younger people had 
higher cycling rates in comparison to other groups, suggesting that restrictions on indoor activities and travel 
limits were not enough to encourage women or older people to cycle more during the pandemic. 
1. Introduction 
There is a close relationship between the built environment and 
active transport (Sallis et al., 2018). Exposure to a supportive built 
environment such as green space can affect people’s attitudes toward 
active travel and consequently their travel behaviour (Barajas, 2019; 
Cheng et al., 2019). The built environment and socio-demographics are 
the two important factors determining cycling behaviour. Research has 
shown that these two factors often have interdependencies. For 
example, low-income people living in high-density areas are less likely 
to cycle compared to higher-income people for commute purpose 
(Tortosa et al., 2021; Hino et al., 2014). 
During COVID-19, many cities implemented significant restrictions 
on travel distances or reasons to leave the home. These restrictions 
limited people’s exposure to the built environment, but they also offered 
opportunities for many people to explore their local surroundings. In 
addition, the closure of gyms and indoor recreation in many cities meant 
that, for many people, cycling was one of the few remaining sources of 
physical activity. In the first year of the pandemic, many cities reported 
significant increases in cycling rates (Schweizer et al., 2021; Fuller et al., 
2021) and bicycle purchases(Buehler and Pucher, 2021). As a result, it is 
possible that the established relationships between the built environ­
ment and cycling behaviour did not hold during the pandemic. It is even 
possible that the role of demographics changed during the height of the 
pandemic; for example, perhaps women were more willing to take up 
cycling during the pandemic because car traffic levels were much lower. 
In Melbourne, Australia, residents experienced some of the longest 
and toughest lockdowns in the world (263 days in total). Two significant 
‘lock-down’ periods in 2020 restricted travel to within 5 km of people’s 
homes. Although some cities, including Melbourne, implemented tem­
porary or permanent cycling infrastructure as an immediate response to 
COVID-19 (Kraus and Koch, 2021; Dunning and Nurse, 2020), residents 
in most cases had to cycle within their immediate neighbourhood due to 
travel restrictions. The regulatory changes in Melbourne highlighted the 
role of the local built environment on cycling activity. In ‘normal’ times 
this relationship may be somewhat clouded as people can travel to 
distant locations to enjoy a walk or bicycle ride. Given that a recrea­
tional cycling trip length is usually longer than cycling for transport

2) To determine whether built environment effects varied between

2. Literature review 
2.1. COVID-19 and cycling 
On January 2020 the World Health Organization (WHO) declared an 
emergency in regards to COVID-19. Consequent responses such as social 
distancing, movement restrictions, work from home and travel bans 
were introduced in different countries and communities. People’s 
movement and public activities were greatly restricted, causing mobility 
disruption and modal shift (Barbieri et al., 2020). As a consequence of 
these restrictions, the amount and pattern of travel changed. This 
change in mobility pattern is not identical for all transport modes 
(Bucsky, 2020) and for all neighbourhoods. Road networks, which are 
dominated by cars and play a major role in urban mobility, were empty 
due to travel restrictions, working from home, e-learning and reduced 
number of public activities (De Vos, 2020). 
Among other modes of transport, cycling received significant atten­
tion both as a mode for transport and leisure activity. Cycling was 
popular alternative transport modes during the peak of COVID-19 
because it provided both exercise and mobility without close exposure 
to other people who might spread the virus (Budi et al., 2021). In 
response, many cities reallocated urban spaces for cycling and improved 
their active transport infrastructure in the hope that increased active 
travel would be maintained as COVID-19 receded (Combs and Pardo, 
2021; Shamshiripour et al., 2020). 
It should be noted that there is a disagreement over whether cycling 
increased or decreased during the pandemic. Some studies found a in­
creases in cycling rates (Buehler and Pucher, 2021; Venter et al., 2020) 
and Strava data in Germany showed cycling in urban public green spaces 
increased (Schweizer et al., 2021). However other studies found a 
decrease in cycling rates (Patterson et al., 2021). Clearly there is still 
much unknown about the interaction between COVID-19 and cycling, 
and the built environment is likely one key to this relationship. 
2.2. Built environment and cycling 
The built environment is the human-made surroundings that support 
human needs. A long history of research explores how built environment 
characteristics affect travel behaviour in general and cycling in partic­
ular (Ewing and Cervero, 2010). This history suggests characteristics of 
the built environment such as density (Cervero and Duncan, 2003), 
green space percentage (Pasha et al., 2016), land-use diversity (Cervero 
et al., 2019), residential area percentage (Sun et al., 2017) and street 
connectivity (Cerin et al., 2017) may impact people’s cycling behaviour 
by providing more or fewer opportunities to safely reach local destina­
tions such as shops or parks. 
Higher residential density is associated with increasing rates of 
cycling, at least for transportation (as opposed to recreation) 
(Beenackers et al., 2012; Saelens et al., 2003). Parks and green space in 
urban environments provide contact with nature and may increase 
recreational cycling (M¨aki-Opas et al., 2016; Beenackers et al., 2012). 
Land-use mix (LUM) quantifies the heterogeneity of the built environ­
ment and has been shown to be associated with physical activity. More 
diverse land-use may provide a broader range of destinations within 
close proximity, which in turn is associated with more active travel trips 
in a neighbourhood (Saelens et al., 2003). Connectivity, defined as the 
density of routes (sidewalk/ bike lanes) and connections between them, 
improves the directness of travel between two points (Jia et al., 2019). 
Previous studies have demonstrated positive association between street 
connectivity with physical activity and active transport (Yang et al., 
2019). Finally, a higher density of cycling infrastructure facilitates op­
portunities for cycling (M¨olenberg et al., 2019; Hull and O’holleran, 
2014). 
However, as yet very little is known about whether differences in the 
local built environment played a role in differences in people’s cycling 
response to COVID-19. Since there are different built environment 
characteristics in each neighbourhood, it is possible that variations in 
the response to COVID-19 restrictions may be associated with some 
differences across different neighbourhoods. One study of active travel 
in Shiraz, Iran found people’s self-reported perception of the quality of 
urban vegetation, safety of intersections, and cycling infrastructure 
played an important role in active travel during COVID-19 (Shaer et al., 
2021). A study in the UK found that, although leisure cycling increased 
on ‘standard’ roads during lockdowns, the increases were much greater 
increases in safe cycling lanes and off-road paths (Hong et al., 2020). 
This leads us to explore the effects of the built environment on leisure 
cycling behaviour before and during the pandemic. The effect of the 
built environment on cycling is not necessarily constant over time. It 
could be that the effect of some characteristics may be stronger or 
weaker during the pandemic compared to before. For example, diverse 
land-use could encourage more leisure cycling if interesting destinations 
are closer to home. But during the pandemic, when non-essential busi­
nesses were closed, diverse land-use may no longer entice people to ride 
in their local neighbourhoods. 
2.3. Socio-demographics and cycling 
Socio-demographic characteristics may have an effect on leisure 
cycling, since people with different characteristics are likely to have 
different preferences or concerns. Moreover, it is possible that people 
with different sociodemographic characteristics have different access to 
cycling infrastructure or other encouraging built environment elements. 
Income, education, immigration status and cultural norms can all 
influence preferences and attitudes toward cycling(Barajas, 2020; Bar­
ajas, 2019). But the most noticeable examples of demographic in­
fluences are the role of gender and age. In most countries, men are more 
likely to ride a bicycle than women (Wittmann et al., 2015; Prati et al., 
2019; Heesch et al., 2012). This is primarily because on average, women 
are more likely to prefer separated cycling infrastructure because it in­
creases their sense of safety (Aldred et al., 2017). Therefore, it is possible 
men and women living in the same neighbourhood have equal access to 
cycling infrastructure, but in practice women’s participation in cycling 
is less than men because of the difference in sense of safety. 
Age is another significant factor in studies of cycling. In many 
countries cycling is more likely to occur among young rather than older 
adults (Bourke et al., 2019; Zander et al., 2013; Grudgings et al., 2021). 
Aldred et al. (2016) examined places where cycling rates were 
increasing to determine whether older adults and women were also 
increasing their cycling rates. Their results found no improvement in 
representation among older people or women. A possible reason for this 
lack of diversification is lack of change in the environment which did not 
reflect the needs of female or older cyclists. 
In addition, people with different sociodemographic characteristics 
may be exposed to different types of built environments and cycling

closely related sociodemographic characteristics related to uneven dis­

forced to live far from the city centre and they experience long com­

cycling rates as a commute mode. Moreover, many analyses have found 
that low-income people have poor access to cycling infrastructure 
whereas more affluent areas have better access to cycling infrastructure 
(Winters et al., 2018; Pistoll and Goodman, 2014; Flanagan et al., 2016; 
Braun et al., 2019). 
It may be that COVID-19 created a situation that overcame some of 
these barriers to cycling for low-income people, women or older adults. 
For example, because car traffic was extremely low during COVID 
lockdowns, perhaps women or older adults felt safer riding a bicycle 
than they might have before lockdowns and increase their cycling rate 
(Fischer et al., 2022). However, at least one study suggests that COVID- 
19 has not changed these relationships; a study in the United States 
found that for most older adults, their cycling habits did not change 
during COVID and that older adults with a higher income are more likely 
to report an increase in cycling during COVID-19 (Gladwin and Duncan, 
2022). 
3. Data sources 
3.1. Study area 
Melbourne is the capital and most-populous city of the Australian 
state of Victoria. We chose Melbourne as a case study location because it 
experienced among the world’s longest and most restrictive lockdowns 
(263 days of lockdown in total). The first lockdown restrictions were 
progressively implemented by Australian government in mid-late March 
2020 to restrict citizens’ movements and reduce their opportunities to 
gather with other people outside their household. It lasted until mid- 
May 2020. The second wave of infections emerged in Victoria during 
May and June, which led to a second lockdown in late June which 
eventually lasted almost four months. In these lockdown periods, a 5-km 
travel restriction forced people to stay in their neighbourhood and use 
their local infrastructure for exercise and leisure. Schools were closed for 
much of these periods and any non-essential workers were forced to 
work from home. 
These two lockdowns in Melbourne substantially disrupted normal 
urban life. However, they also provided an opportunity to conduct a case 
study to evaluate changes in active travel behaviour across three stages 
(pre-COVID, first-, and second-lockdowns) and how these changes relate 
to the built environment in which people lived. 
3.2. Survey data 
We used two different datasets to compare recreational cycling 
behaviour, which is done for purpose of pleasure not reaching to a 
specific destination, in different built environments. First, we used a self- 
reported questionnaire survey data that measured travel behaviour in 
different stages of lockdown. The survey was conducted as part of the ‘C- 
19 Long Term Transport Impact Study’ in Melbourne (Currie et al., 
2021). This questionnaire gathered a wide range of information about 
respondent travel behaviour, commute mode, changes to working from 
home, attitudes and socio-demographics. Although the survey gathered 
a range of travel behaviour information from the respondents, we only 
explored their self-reported recreational cycling data in this paper. 
The questionnaire survey was conducted through an online panel 
survey company. The survey was run in two different waves; the first lag 
of data covered the first lockdown in Melbourne and the second one 
gathered after the second lockdown and latest travel restrictions were 
announced. As we are interested in whether recreational cycling 
behaviour changed during the two different lockdown periods, we 
analysed data from the second wave of this dataset (sample size 1341 
responses), which ran from 16 July to 8 August 2020. The characteristics 
of the sample are presented in Table 1. It shows that the survey sample 
was over-representative of females but the income and age character­
istics were broadly similar to the Melbourne average. 
Respondents were asked to indicate how often they cycled for rec­
reational purposes in three different stages of the lockdown: Stage 1- 
pre-COVID, Stage 2- during first lockdown, and Stage 3- during second 
lockdown. Note that the two lockdown periods were during autumn and 
winter of 2020 when cycling rates are historically lower (Pazdan, 2020). 
Responses were recorded on a seven-point scale:

3.3. Built environment data 
The second set of data set that we used in this research is open spatial 
data to quantify built environment characteristics. We linked the cycling 
questions and socio-demographics of respondents to the built environ­
ment of the residential location reported by respondents. As we only 
knew the suburb of the respondents, we had to use the spatial resolution 
of SA2 statistical area for this analysis, which matches closely to the 
suburb boundary. 
Based on the literature, we calculated built environment indicators 
including residential density (Forsyth et al., 2007), green space area 
(Watts et al., 2013), and land-use mix (Cervero and Kockelman, 1997), 
street connectivity (Bentley et al., 2018), and cycling infrastructure 
coverage (Buehler and Dill, 2016). 
To quantify the built environment characteristics, baseline data were 
downloaded from Victorian Government open data and GIS methods are 
utilised to form indicators. For dwelling numbers in each neighbour­
hood, the 2016 census data were used. Green space percentage was 
calculated based on the total area of parklands within each neighbour­
hood. Residential area percentage was determined based on the total 
area of residential blocks in each neighbourhood, and residential density 
was based on the number of dwellings per Km2 in each neighbourhood.

4. Analytical methods 
In this study we used two different analyses to understand the cycling 
trend during different stages of lockdown. First, we used descriptive 
statistics to analyse the distribution of leisure cycling across the 3 
different stages of COVID-19. To determine if there was a statistically 
significant change in cycling frequency between the three time periods 
(before-COVID, during-COVID and between-waves), we used Cochran’s 
Q test, which is an extension of McNemar test based on Chi square 
distribution for testing differences between repeated data. Then, we 
compared the mean cycling rates across different built environment and 
socio-demographic characteristics. 
In the second stage of the results, we used multivariate models to 
isolate the association between the built environment, demographics 
and time period on cycling rates. Because we asked respondents to 
report their cycling frequency for three different time periods, we 
needed to use repeated-measures modelling. After checking the cycling 
data distribution, we found that our data set has a negative-binomial 
distribution. Therefore, we used multilevel modelling to analyse 
repeated measures data with a negative-binomial distribution. As our 
explanatory variables (built environment and socio-demographics) do 
not change during the study time, we consider them as fixed effects in all 
models. This method gives us the flexibility to investigate potential in­
teractions between different variables. 
In order to test the main effects of COVID stage, built environment 
and demographics, as well as their potential interactions, we formulated 
a series of six models. Models 1 and 2 only considered the main effects, 
testing whether demographics improve the model fit. Model 3 explores 
the research aim of whether or not the effect of built environment varied 
depending on COVID stage, by adding a series of interactions between 
the two variables. Model 4 checks whether the influence of de­
mographics varies based on COVID stage through interaction effects. 
And finally, Models 5 and 6 test whether the influence of the built 
environment interacts with participants’ gender. 
In order to interpret the effects of the models, we graphed out the 
estimated marginal means from the model results, using a contrast test 
to determine whether there were statistically significant differences 
between groups. 
5. Descriptive results 
5.1. Leisure cycling dynamics and the built environment 
In this study, leisure cycling rates declined during COVID-19. Table 3 
shows that overall, leisure cycling declined during the first and second 
lockdown stages. The changes in mean cycling level between different 
stages were statistically significant (F(2,4020) = 7.467, p < 0.01) based 
on the multilevel modelling results by considering time in the model. 
Similarly, the percentage of people who choose to ride a bike as their 
leisure physical activity dropped from 24.5% before COVID-19 to 21.0% 
in the first lockdown and 16.8% in the second lockdown. Based on the 
Cochran’s Q test results for cycled / did not cycle, the changes in cycling 
level during different stages of lockdown is statistically significant (χ2 
(2) = 72.518, p < 0.01). 
We now examine whether the built environment influenced leisure 
cycling rates, perhaps ‘protecting’ against the impact of COVID-19. 
Fig. 2 shows the changes in leisure cycling during different lockdown 
stages by built environment characteristics. As we would expect, people 
living in neighbourhoods with higher cycling infrastructure density 
were more likely to cycle pre-COVID and also during the first lockdown. 
However, by the second lockdown these rates had dropped to be similar 
in all areas. 
Unexpectedly, people who live in the lowest quartile of green space 
had the highest cycling level before COVID-19 but their cycling level 
dropped significantly in lockdown. This may be because places with 
lower green space also tended to have higher cycling infrastructure 
density (see Fig. 1). As with cycling infrastructure, by the second lock­
down cycling rates are similar in all areas. 
Land-use diversity does not appear to interact with cycling rates 
either before or during COVID. In contrast, street connectivity was 
clearly associated with higher cycling rates pre-COVID. Although 
cycling rates reduced, they remained higher among highly connected 
neighbourhoods. 
The effect of residential land use varies depending on whether you 
looked at residential density or residential area percentage. In higher 
residential density areas, we saw higher cycling levels pre-pandemic. 
Although they dropped during the COVID-19 lockdowns, the rate 
remained higher high-density areas compared to low-density areas. In 
contrast, people who lived in neighbourhoods with the lowest residen­
tial percentage had the highest level of cycling but this dropped off to the 
lowest levels during COVID. An area with lowest residential percentage 
means that these are non-residential in nature (e.g. predominantly 
commercial, industrial), which remained closed during the lockdowns 
providing little opportunities for cycle.

6. Multivariate results 
In the next step we assess the interaction of built environment and 
socio-demographic characteristics on leisure cycling during pandemic 
stages. 
From the descriptive analysis, it appears that some demographic and 
built environment characteristics such as gender and residential area 
percentage may be influencing cycling rates before and during COVID. 
In order to explore the combined influence of built environment and 
socio-demographic characteristics, we conducted a series of multilevel 
models for repeated-measure data to examine the association between 
built environment characteristics, socio-demographics and cycling 
levels over time.

7. Discussion 
This paper had two research objectives. In response to the first 
objective, we found a declining trend for leisure cycling in our survey 
sample. This was somewhat unexpected as recent studies found in­
creases in non-commute cycling during COVID-19 (Hong et al., 2020), 
although other studies showed a decrease in cycling rates (Patterson 
et al., 2021). This study could be reflecting a real reduction in leisure 
cycling, or it could be due to recall bias among survey participants. It 
may be, for example, the commuter cyclists are over-estimating how 
much they cycled before COVID even though we asked them to only 
report on cycling for leisure. 
Moreover, we found that leisure cycling rates were only sensitive to 
cycling infrastructure density and, to some extent, connectivity. People 
who lived in neighbourhoods with greater connectivity cycled more, 
suggesting that greater intersection density encourages leisure cycling. 
However, the effect of cycling infrastructure density was non-intuitive. 
In the descriptive comparisons, higher infrastructure density was asso­
ciated with more leisure cycling, especially pre-COVID. But in the 
models that control for demographics and other variables, higher 
infrastructure density was associated with lower cycling. This could be 
because our built environment measures were not spatially sensitive 
enough as we could only link survey participants to an SA2 census 
boundary (a neighbourhood of around 10,000 people). This could also 
explain why we did not see more significant associations between the 
built environment and leisure cycling. Another possible explanation 
could be concentration of bike lanes and infrastructure in Melbourne’s 
inner-city area, where there is less green space and other characteristics 
that encourage people to cycle for recreation rather than commute. 
Interestingly, we did not find any interaction over time between 
cycling and built environment characteristics. The effects of infrastruc­
ture were the same whether we were predicting cycling before COVID- 
19 or during the first or second lock-down. This is indirect contrast to 
a paper in the UK that found a greater influence of cycling infrastructure 
during COVID-19 (Hong et al., 2020). 
Finally, we wanted to examine the relationship between leisure 
cycling and socio-demographic characteristics. As expected from the 
literature, younger adults and men were more likely to cycle than older 
adults and women. This did not vary over time; for example, women and 
older people did not suddenly take up cycling more during COVID-19. 
We did, however, find that women were more likely to cycle if they 
lived in more residential neighbourhoods, whereas men were not 
influenced by the residential area of their home location. 
These findings suggest that although planners have tried to make

8. Conclusion 
Cycling is potential means of physical activity which can contribute 
to people’s health. It can be considered to be a potential low-cost 
strategy to improve communities’ health and wellbeing. The link be­
tween physical activity and health, physical and mental, has long been 
known (Warburton and Bredin, 2016; Biddle et al., 2019). Supportive 
infrastructure plays an important role for people with inadequate access 
to private recreational facilities or limited mobility such as low-income 
people, youth, and women. These vulnerable groups are already at 
greater risk of physical inactivity; therefore, it is important to consider 
them in neighbourhoods planning. This need was recognized by the 
United Nations who have codified the aim of providing “universal access 
to safe, inclusive and accessible, green and public spaces, particularly for 
women and children, older persons and persons with disabilities” in 
their Sustainable Development Goals (United Nations General Assembly,

A B S T R A C T
Studies dealing with services and benefits of urban green space are overrepresented compared to those 
addressing disservices, perceived constraints and other negative perceptions. Even when focusing on disservices 
and perceived constraints, the studies often take into account only a limited number of reasons for the negative 
perception. In this study we conducted a series of focus group interviews with the members of the public in the 
city of Zagreb (Croatia) with the goal of grasping the variety of reasons for negative perception, providing 
classification of those perceptions and exploring whether and how the perception is related to sociodemographic 
characteristics. Participants mostly discussed negative perceptions due to the behaviour of other users and 
perceived inadequate management of urban green space, while the least about the perception of safety. Tree 
disservices accounted only for a small portion of overall reasons for the negative perception. Age, gender, 
household income, employment status, number of preschool children and the length of living in Zagreb proved 
statistically significant for the perception, while education was not. Focus groups as a method enabled cata­
loguing the variety of reasons for negative perception. Understanding the range of reasons leading to the negative 
public perception of urban green space contributes to better planning, management and meeting public needs 
and expectations.
1. Introduction
The scientific literature has widely discussed the contribution of 
ecosystem services and the benefits of urban green space (UGS) to 
human well-being (Escobedo et al., 2019). However, there is a growing 
number of publications focusing on ecosystem disservices (D¨ohren and 
Haase, 2015). Indeed, the scientific literature recognises the need to 
focus on both services and disservices of UGS to provide a balanced view 
of the plurality of human-nature interactions (Baumeister et al., 2022; 
Roman et al., 2020; D¨ohren and Haase, 2015; Lyytim¨aki et al., 2008). 
This more balanced view is expected to improve the management and 
planning of UGS (Baumeister et al., 2022; Roman et al., 2020). 
Ecosystem disservices are defined as ecosystem „functions that are 
perceived as negative for human well-being“ (Lyytim¨aki and Sipil¨a, 
2009). The negative impacts on human well-being may be ecological (e. 
g. emission of volatile organic compounds by trees), economic (e.g. 
damage to infrastructure or property due to plant growth or tree failure), 
health impacts (e.g. allergies caused by trees), psychological impacts (e. 
g. negative feelings such as fear due to dense vegetation or discomfort 
caused by animal excrements or plant litter) and other (D¨ohren and 
Haase, 2015).
Given the benefits, the use of UGS among residents should be pro­
moted. However, people may perceive various reasons that may prevent 
them from visiting UGS and engaging in outdoor recreation (Dawson 
et al., 2023; Cohen et al., 2010). Hence, knowledge of how people 
perceive UGS and how it affects their behaviour is pertinent. In the 
scientific literature those perceived reasons are embodied in the concept 
of perceived constraints (Ghimire et al., 2014). They may be structural 
(e.g. no time, money, poor maintenance of recreational areas), intra­
personal (e.g. personal safety problems, personal health issues) and 
interpersonal (e.g. no companion) (Crawford et al., 1991).
Typologies of ecosystem disservices focus mainly on negativities 
emanating from the ecosystem itself, while many other nuances, spe­
cifically those caused by social factors, such as evidence of vandalism or 
littering, are excluded (Baumeister et al., 2022). However, social factors 
may be even more pertinent (Dawson et al., 2023). In general, disser­
vices and costs associated with UGS has received less attention in 
research papers than the services and benefits (Shackleton et al., 2016;

Roy et al., 2012). Many scientific studies addressing negative percep­
tions of UGS consider limited number of reasons for negative perception 
and usually use quantitative approach, while to the best of our knowl­
edge, a comprehensive study of a wide variety of reasons why the public 
perceive UGS negatively is still missing.
Research proves that perception in general (including negative 
perception) depends on individual, social and environmental factors 
(Tveit et al., 2019). Urban planning should secure urban residents’ ac­
cess to UGS, as well as UGS quantity and quality because these attributes 
have the most influence on residents’ health benefits (Dipeolu et al., 
2021). The absence of these requirements for UGS could elicit negative 
perceptions.
Even though trees are often considered attractive and a source of 
various cultural ecosystem services (Kloster et al., 2021; Krajter Ostoi´c 
et al., 2020), they can also be perceived negatively as a source of dis­
services due to tree species, size or location (Shackleton et al., 2016; Roy 
et al., 2012). Trees are also seen as nature-based solutions, especially in 
urban areas (Kong et al., 2021). Hence, knowing how people perceive 
trees benefits our understanding of human-nature interactions.
The management and maintenance of UGS may also be a source of 
positive and negative perceptions and is often seen through prism of 
perceived safety (Nassauer et al., 2021; Judice et al., 2021). People find 
the perception of safety in UGS one of its most important attributes 
(Stessens et al., 2020).
The behaviour of other UGS users may be a source of frustration and 
negative perception. The studies show that evidence of vandalism, lit­
tering and other incivilities, especially those related to dog fouling, are 
often perceived negatively by the public and other greenspace users 
(Dawson et al., 2023; Palliwoda and Priess, 2021; Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 
2017; Richardson and Shackleton, 2014).
Numerous studies have shown the relationship between perception 
of and attitude towards UGS and sociodemographic factors (Moffat 
et al., 2024; Dade et al., 2020; Lo and Jim, 2012). However, some studies 
find only a limited influence of sociodemographic variables on percep­
tion (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017). Among those variables, gender, age, 
and education prove to be pertinent.
Given the lack of comprehensive studies focusing on various sources 
of negative perception of UGS and the effect of individual factors on 
human perception, the goal of the paper is to a) explore negative per­
ceptions of tree-based UGS based on a city-wide participatory approach, 
b) provide classification of these negative perceptions, and to c) explore 
relations between negative perception and sociodemographic variables.
2. Methodology
2.1. A study area
The study was conducted in Zagreb, the capital of the Republic of 
Croatia and geographically located in Southeast Europe. According to 
the latest census data from 2021, the population is close to 800,000. It is 
a very green city and was recently pronounced as the second greenest 
European capital based on the amount of green infrastructure and urban 
tree cover (EEA, 2022). However, the distribution and quality of UGS 
varies (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017; Kalasek and Schneider, 2021). There 
is a scarcity of previous studies addressing public perception of UGS in 
Zagreb, including the negative perception. There is only one study on 
the city level that showed the public is mostly concerned with 
vandalism, behaviour of dog owners and other users, or lack of equip­
ment and mobility infrastructure, while being less concerned with UGS 
size, connectivity, noise, tree species or access (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 
2017). The same study showed that while the public perceives Zagreb as 
a green city, at the same time, they perceive the amount of the green­
space is decreasing. The results show that the public supports education 
and raising awareness about UGS benefits and proper behaviour of users 
from an early age, as well as better rule enforcement and fining those 
who continue with incivilities. Another study on urban forest and green 
space governance in Zagreb showed that public participation is one of

2013). In Zagreb, collaborative UGS design involving citizens is not a 
norm. In that sense, parks can be designed completely without the input 
of local community. However, requirements of EU funded projects have 
been a major driver of the implementation of co-designing approaches 
(Roitsch et al., 2024).
2.2. Focus group method
The focus group interviews were part of the larger research project 
on participatory mapping of cultural ecosystem services and disservices 
of UGS (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2020). They were the method of choice 
because they help collect in-depth information on how people talk about 
the phenomenon of interest, what they find important, and most 
importantly, why (Skoko and Benkovi´c, 2009). Hence, this qualitative 
approach has an advantage over a quantitative survey. However, focus 
groups provide a sound basis for the application of such surveys in 
future, especially in the context of the scarcity of previous research on 
the perception of UGS in the study area (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2020). The 
purpose of the focus groups was to collect a diversity of responses that 
would serve as an input in the quantitative survey at a later stage.
The focus group protocol covered questions related to several cul­
tural ecosystem services of tree-based UGS, such as forests, parks, forest 
parks, street trees etc. Additionally, one of the questions was about tree- 
based UGS they perceive negatively and why. However, in this paper we 
will focus only on the reasons for the negative perception of UGS. Par­
ticipants were invited not only to name UGS they perceive negatively 
but also to explain why and point to those on the map of their city 
district. One of the assistants helped participants find those UGS on the 
map and mark the locations with self-adhesive dots. If participants were 
unaware of the name of UGS or the exact location, researchers invited 
them to provide as detailed a description of the location as possible. The 
research team later added those locations to the map.
At the end of each focus group discussion, each participant answered 
a short sociodemographic questionnaire. We collected information on 
age, gender, employment, household income, educational attainment, 
type of housing (living in a house or an apartment building), growing up 
(urban or rural), number of preschool children in their household, and 
the length of living in Zagreb and their respective city districts.
The principal researcher and two doctoral students moderated focus 
groups. They took turns as lead moderators (the ones who asked ques­
tions and moderated discussion), while others acted as assistants, 
helping respondents with the mapping exercises and ensuring voice 
recorders were working correctly. Interviews took place in the evenings 
(usually at 18:00 h local time) either at the premises of the local self- 
government or public libraries that were easily accessible to 
participants.
The main requirements for participants were that they were adults 
and living in the city district where the focus group took place. The goal 
was to conduct focus groups in each city district because we wanted to 
tap into local knowledge on locally relevant UGS and potential issues 
related to these. We assumed that people would be more knowledgeable 
of UGS in their city district. Additionally, we wanted to avoid them 
talking only about the well-known and most visited UGS in the city of 
Zagreb.
We used various communication channels to invite focus group 
participants, such as flyers in public places, invitations on the project 
website, Facebook page, local TV and radio, or personal contacts (asking 
family, friends and acquaintances to spread the word). Local community 
board members were kindly asked to spread the word to their constit­
uencies. Initially, the idea was for potential participants to contact re­
searchers and announce their presence at the focus group discussion so 
that we do not exceed the manageable number of participants. However, 
it did not work well because people appeared at their own will or

Hence, researchers could not control who would appear for the discus­

At the beginning of each focus group, a moderator introduced the 
research team, explained the purpose of the project, topics that would be 
discussed, and informed participants about their rights and how data 
would be analysed. Also, it was necessary to lay some ground rules for 
discussion: each opinion is welcomed and valuable, participants should 
respect the opinions of others, even those that are different from their

asked for informed consent and consent to use voice recorders. Partici­

locations they mapped and the sociodemographic questionnaire. We 
used these numbers in focus groups transcripts. Participants were 
informed their responses will be reported only in anonymised form. 
Only principal researcher and two PhD students had the access to 
recorded data and transcripts.
We conducted 20 focus groups with 94 participants covering all city 
districts, meaning the average number of participants per focus group 
was 4–5 (Table 1). We wanted to conduct focus groups in each city 
district to give residents in all city districts an equal opportunity to have 
their say, so we kept conducting them even when new focus groups did 
not bring new information. The length of focus groups was between an 
hour and two hours, with the vast majority lasting an hour or so. In 
comparison to census data, women, highly educated, older persons and 
retirees were overrepresented in the sample.
2.3. Qualitative and quantitative data analyses
All focus group interviews were conducted in Croatian, recorded, 
and manually transcribed into Croatian to prevent the loss of subtle 
meanings. Only excerpts from transcripts were translated into English 
(Appendix B). We used MAXQDA 18.5.2 (VERBI Software, 2018), a 
computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software. Sociodemographic 
variables were also imported for each participant to explore their 
connection with perceptions.
We used inductive thematic analysis, meaning a bottom-up approach 
to coding (Kuckartz and R¨adiker, 2019). In the first round of coding, we 
were led by the discussion topics. For instance, participants were asked 
about UGS they perceived negatively and why. We looked up and coded 
in the transcripts all the instances when participants discussed the 
negative perceptions of UGS. In the second round of coding, individual 
codes were grouped under themes. For instance, all codes that addressed 
weak or absent management of UGS were grouped under the theme 
‘weak or absent management of UGS’. Similar themes were grouped 
under umbrella categories in the third round. The ‘weak or absent 
management of UGS’ theme was attributed to the ‘management of UGS’ 
category.
For the analyses of the connection between sociodemographic vari­
ables and what people said about the negative perception of UGS, we 
used the Crosstab for Focus Groups tool that allows various analyses. For 
our purposes, we calculated distributions of coded segments according 
to the categories of sociodemographic variables, namely the number of 
coded segments, column percentages based on the sum of coded seg­
ments and column percentages based on the number of participants.
Later, we did statistical analyses to explore the relationship between 
the participant’s sociodemographic characteristics and the negative 
perception of UGS. For this purpose, we used contingency tables that 
were calculated for each sociodemographic variable in MAXQDA based 
on the number of coded segments for each category of the negative 
perception. Pearson’s chi-squared test was performed for each contin­
gency table with a level of significance alpha of 0.05. When the chi- 
squared test indicated statistically significant differences in the distri­
bution of the number of codes among categories, a chi-squared posthoc 
analysis of standardised residuals with the Bonferroni correction was 
used to explore and characterise the difference in more detail with the 
level of significance alpha 0.05. We built new categories for some var­
iables when the chi-square test indicated that the results might not be 
correct due to some cells having too small a frequency. For instance, 
education as a variable initially had three categories – elementary 
school, high school and faculty. However, due to a small number of 
respondents who had elementary school or less, we created a new 
category that included both those with elementary school or less and 
those with high school education. Similarly, for the variable employ­
ment status, we added six unemployed respondents to those employed 
due to their age and having in mind that age proved important for 
perception in many previous studies. We used R software (v 4.1.2. “Bird 
Hippie”) with RStudio for the statistical analyses. More specifically, we 
used R stats and chisq.posthoc.test (Ebbert, 2019).
3. Results
3.1. The negative perceptions of UGS classified
Based on the inductive thematic analysis, we created 634 coded 
segments and identified five groups of negative perceptions of UGS. 
These groups were negative perceptions due to the behaviour of other 
people, negative perceptions due to urban planning, negative percep­
tions due to UGS design, negative perceptions due to UGS management, 
and negative perceptions due to feelings of unease or lack of safety 
(Table 2). The typical excerpts from focus group transcripts depicting 
the groups of negative perceptions are available in the Appendix B. Half 
of the coded segments addressed negative perceptions that are result of 
either how other behave or the management of UGS.

people referred to misbehaviour of people who use and/or own the UGS 
or crowding. The most pronounced themes were littering (the presence of 
litter on UGS) and the behaviour of dog owners. The latter mainly

3.1.2. The negative perception due to green space management
The negative perceptions due to the management of UGS were 
mainly related to the perceived Absence of or insufficient maintenance, the 
interpretation of which varied. UGS was deemed insufficiently main­
tained or not maintained at all when UGS was perceived as impassable 
(due to the absence of trails, shrub coverage or wood debris on forest 
trails after tree felling), when removed or old trees were not replaced, 
young trees were not watered (enough), tree branches were scraping on 
window panes, fallen trees were present in the forest, forest meadows 
were overgrown by forest vegetation due to natural succession, the 
lawns were not mowed often enough, tree pests, mosquitos, crows or 
even rats were present, there was evidence of litter, and finally, when 
wear and tear were present on the equipment.
Besides this, too extensive management activities were also negatively 
perceived, such as when trees were perceived as “pruned too much” or a 
forest too thin because of the extensive felling.
In a few instances, focus group participants discussed the uneven 
management of UGS as displeasing for residents, making them feel like 
“second-grade citizens”, as one participant put it. Uneven management 
referred to the perception that UGS of the central neighbourhoods 
received better care than those in more distant neighbourhoods.
3.1.3. The negative perception due to green space design
The primary source of negative perceptions due to UGS design was 
related to what we labelled as Content. They either perceived a lack of it 
or the content was undesirable. Focus group participants often perceived 
a lack of urban equipment (benches, trash bins, illumination, or outdoor 
gyms), shade, walking trails, trees or hedges for noise abatement or 
content for certain groups of users (UGS not serving various age or user 
groups). The undesirable content of UGS was mainly related to having 
trees in the wrong place or having the wrong tree species. The former 
was about the trees planted too close to buildings, which were perceived 
either as suffocating for people or dangerous for buildings, while the 
latter referred to trees potentially causing allergies (such as birch), those 
leaving a sticky residue (e.g. linden trees on parking lots) or when their 
fruits filled sewer drains or vents (such as maple tree).
A less pronounced source of negative perception was related to the 
Selection of material and implementation quality. The topic referred to the 
presence of materials considered unsafe, low-quality, unsustainable or 
unfit for the context in aesthetical sense, such as having concrete frames 
or asphalt flooring in children’s playgrounds, the flooring of children’s 
playgrounds with plastic-based materials (too hot in summer and leav­
ing microplastics in the environment), the low quality of outdoor gym 
equipment that showed evidence of wear and tear in only a few months 
of being in use or when materials used for a footbridge were deemed 
unfit for the historical context of the UGS.
Furthermore, participants mentioned some problems with the 
implementation of the UGS design, mostly due to lack of user’s input. 
For instance, when benches were placed without access to foot trails, 
perceived as facing the wrong direction or when designers went with 
straight lines for foot trails instead of following the logic of actual users.
Lastly, the new design was perceived negatively after the recon­
struction of a UGS surrounding the famous architectural building. The 
reconstruction reduced previously rich design with different vegetation 
types to grass-only design.
3.1.4. The negative perception due to urban planning
This group of negative perceptions was related to UGS in the broader 
context of the neighbourhood or city districts due to what was perceived 
as inadequate urban planning that led to a lack of access or difficulties in 
accessing UGS.
The most pronounced was the perceived lack of public UGS in their 
city districts, followed by the perception that UGS had been replaced by 
development. The difficulties with access to existing UGS due to a lack of 
infrastructure or equipment fostering mobility were somewhat less 
pronounced. These referred to the lack of bicycle trails, pedestrian 
promenades, sidewalks or sidewalks perceived as being too narrow, 
buildings blocking the access to UGS, lack of benches for older people on 
their way to UGS, or temporary road construction work making access to 
UGS difficult.
Additionally, some focus group respondents voiced a lack of strategic 
planning of their city districts, uneven distribution of UGS, lack of 
particular services for residents, such as dog parks, or too much traffic in 
the vicinity of UGS.
3.1.5. The negative perception due to feeling of unease or perceived lack of 
safety in green space
In this group, negative perceptions are all the instances when people 
explicitly said they did not feel comfortable or were even afraid of 
visiting UGS due to fear for their safety. The group consists of four 
subgroups based on the sources of unease or fear. These were feelings of 
unease/perceived lack of safety due to UGS design, other people’s behaviour, 
UGS management and other reasons.
The most pronounced reason was UGS design. It referred to UGS that 
were perceived as "too dark", with illumination missing, tall vegetation 
(trees) present, when respondents felt suffocated by the vegetation, or 
when UGS had been perceived as unsafe for senior citizens due to the 
terrain or lack of walking trails.
The behaviour of other people was the reason for discomfort or even 
fear when there were dogs present without their owners or were 
unleashed, specific user groups were present (groups of youngsters, 
football fans on game days), or even when there were not enough people 
present, especially in the evenings.

4. Discussion
This bottom-up participatory study showed that the public might 
perceive tree-based UGS negatively for a wide variety of reasons. The 
reasons were categorised into five groups based on the in-depth thematic 
analysis. Based on the number of coded segments, focus group partici­
pants addressed the most negative perceptions due to the behaviour of 
other users and perceived inadequate management of UGS (Table 2). 
The UGS design and urban planning were less in focus, and the 
perception of safety was the least.
4.1. Negative perception due to behaviour of other people
When looking into reasons for negative perceptions due to other 
people’s behaviour, most of these were related to incivilities, weak rule 
compliance and rule enforcement (see Section 3.1.1). Indeed, other 
studies prove the behaviour of other UGS users is an important source of 
frustration and negative perception, especially evidence of vandalism, 
littering and other incivilities, such as dog fouling (Dawson et al., 2023; 
Palliwoda and Priess, 2021; Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017; Richardson and 
Shackleton, 2014). In our study, negatively perceived were also private 
forest owners and homeowners if they were perceived as contributing to 
the decrease of UGS by careless management or soil sealing of private 
yards.
4.2. Negative perception due to UGS management
The negative perception due to UGS management was mainly related 
to the perceived absence or insufficient maintenance, the interpretation 
of which varied among participants (see Section 3.1.2., Appendix B). 
The perception of maintenance (perceived care) is strongly connected to 
perceived safety (Nassauer et al., 2021; Judice et al., 2021). Given the 
importance of recreational opportunities, especially in Western soci­
eties, urban residents expect UGS to be managed and maintained with 
the provision of these services in mind, with cleanliness and mainte­
nance being among the most important attributes (Stessens et al., 2020). 
In our study, negatively perceived were obstacles to recreational 
enjoyment in forests related to access and unobstructed movement (e.g. 
wood debris or fallen trees left on forest trails), unkempt character 
(overgrown forest lawns, understory) and the presence of deadwood. 
Other studies as well show that people prefer managed urban forests 
without deadwood or traces of forest operations (Gundersen and Friv­
old, 2008; Manyani et al., 2021; Tyrv¨ainen et al., 2003). However, a 
segment of the public also prefers more ‘wild’ UGS (Hwang et al., 2019), 
but our participants did not fall into that category.
Inadequate maintenance was perceived as presence of litter for UGS 
other than forests and equipment that is damaged, unfunctional, or looks 
shabby. Indeed, according to the Broken Window Theory (Kelling and 
Wilson, 1982), evidence of disorder is not welcome and invites more 
disorder and crime. Translated into the UGS context, it could lead to 
vandalism or a perceived lack of safety.
Participants were also concerned with potential disservices due to a 
perceived lack of care for trees, and interpreted as the presence of un­
healthy trees, “too old” trees or when newly planted trees were not being 
watered. Additionally, disservices in the form of unpleasant animals, 
such as pests, mosquitos, rats and crows, were mentioned by several 
participants. Indeed, studies show that trees can be perceived as a source 
of disservices and associated costs, such as posing a risk to power lines or 
people due to tree failure, when trees are dead or diseased (Kloster et al., 
2021; Roman et al., 2020; Roy et al., 2012). Public concern over tree 
maintenance and potential disservices may lead to a lack of public 
acceptance of trees in their neighbourhoods (Carmichael and McDo­
nough, 2019; Moffat et al., 2024).
Too extensive tree management was defined by respondents as either 
trees being pruned too much or as too intensive forest thinning. This 
could be related to the lack of understanding of forest management 
strategies by the public when the results of regeneration cuts are 
perceived as detrimental to forests (Bradley and Kearney, 2007).
4.3. Negative perception due to UGS design
Participants mostly discussed the lack of various recreational 
equipment and infrastructure (see Section 3.1.3). The result is corrob­
orated by many other studies showing that people negatively perceive 
the lack of walking and cycling paths, sitting areas, rubbish bins, illu­
mination, children’s playgrounds or dog parks (Gorzelany et al., 2022; 
Manyani et al., 2021; Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017). However, compared to 
the previous study with the public in Zagreb and other issues, illumi­
nation was considered less important (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017). In this 
study, shade was discussed positively, while evidence from the literature 
shows ambiguous results (positive - Kloster et al., 2021; negative - 
Davies et al., 2017). Participants perceived trees as both desirable and a 
source of disservices (due to allergenicity – e.g. birch, tree being in the 
wrong place, tree sap on cars, nuisance due to blocked sewer drains or 
ventilation shafts).
Some participants voiced the importance of participatory (collabo­
rative) design to meet the needs of various user groups by creating in­
clusive spaces for all age groups and in a way that users consider 
functional. Co-creation of UGS has many benefits, as perceived by public 
administration, despite being challenging (Roitsch et al., 2024). The 
study also included in the analysis some EU-funded projects conducted 
in Zagreb.
It is worth noting that the size of UGS was not the topic of discussion, 
despite studies demonstrating its importance to visitors (Schipperijn 
et al., 2010). Importantly, there is a positive correlation between the size 
of UGS and the frequency of walking, exercising and relaxing (Gozalo 
et al., 2019). This correlation highlights the potential benefits of larger 
UGS. Smaller UGS in dense urban areas are sometimes perceived as 
noisy and less suitable for certain recreational activities, while quietness 
is among the most appreciated attributes of UGS (Stessens et al., 2020). 
However, a previous study on public perception in Zagreb also showed 
that the public gives more importance to other attributes (Krajter Ostoi´c 
et al., 2017).
4.4. Negative perception of UGS due to urban planning
The crucial issue was the lack of access to UGS in some city districts 
due to a lack of infrastructure that supports mobility, such as sidewalks 
and bicycle trails, or a perceived lack of UGS in general because of the 
perceived absence of strategic planning (see Section 3.1.4). A recent 
study of the distribution of urban green infrastructure in Zagreb, 
commissioned by Greenpeace, shows that the access to UGS varies, even 
though 60 % of the population lives within 300 m (5 min) of nearby 
greenspace (Kalasek and Schneider, 2021). However, our study showed 
that the public, especially in less green city districts, still experienced 
problems with accessibility.
4.5. Negative perception due to perceived lack of safety
Perceived lack of safety was less important in the context of other 
groups of negative perceptions (Table 2). However, expectedly, it was 
present among senior citizens (due to their perceived frailty and 
mobility issues), who also voiced a need for walking trails and safe 
pavements, women, participants with small children in their house­
holds, and higher-income households.
The feeling of unease or even fear, including fear of crime, is often 
attributed to individual, social and physical factors, such as gender, 
vegetation and the presence of other people (Honey-Ros´es and Zapata, 
2023; Lis et al., 2022; Sreetheran and Konijnendijk van den Bosch, 2014; 
Skår, 2010). The reasons reported by our participants are present in 
these studies as well. However, what was interesting was that UGS near 
a mental hospital elicited unease and was perceived as being avoided.

Our respondents rarely expressed that negative perceptions prevented 
them from visiting UGS. Recreation is an underlying goal of 
human-nature interaction, and UGS services and disservices usually 
coincide in the same place (Baumeister et al., 2022; Riechers et al., 
2016).
4.6. Effect of sociodemographic variables on negative perception
Our study confirmed the importance of variables such as gender, age, 
household income, having small kids or the length of living in Zagreb 
(Table 3). Somewhat surprisingly, men talked significantly more than 
women about the negative perceptions due to the behaviour of other 
people. We expected that both men and women would be equally con­
cerned with this issue since the issues of littering or dog fouling were the 
most pronounced, and these are pretty ubiquitous. Older age proved 
important for the negative perception due to UGS management and 
design, which is understandable given the unique needs of this age 
group (Zhai et al., 2023) Younger people were more concerned with 
urban planning than other age groups (Table A.1). Household income 
proved important only for the perception of UGS design (Table 3). Ed­
ucation had no significant effect on negative perception, in contrast to 
some other studies (Moffat et al., 2024; Jim and Shan, 2013; Tyrv¨ainen 
et al., 2003). We also tested sociodemographic variables not present in 
other studies, such as length of residence, whether they lived in a house 
or apartment building and whether they grew up in urban or rural areas. 
However, all those variables were not statistically significant (Table 3). 
It could be explained by the ubiquity of many negative perceptions of 
tree-based UGS, such as negative perceptions due to incivilities or 
perceived lack of care.
4.7. Recommendations for urban planning, UGS management/ 
maintenance and design
The study findings provide an opportunity to give recommendations 
to urban planners, city administration, and green space managers. Better 
rule enforcement in terms of monitoring and sanctioning people per­
sisting on incivilities may be helpful regarding the negative perception 
due to the behaviour of other people. More city officers will be needed to 
monitor and fine those who continue with such behaviour, as well as 
educational campaigns to increase rule compliance. Zagreb residents 
support these measures (Krajter Ostoi´c et al., 2017). Those city officers 
dealing with rule enforcement in public areas in Zagreb are short in 
numbers and designated with rather large areas. In addition, their job 
description is relatively broad and related to all public spaces, including 
parking lots or state of buildings’ fronts. Public involvement in moni­
toring the state of public green space and visitor behaviour may over­
come the lack of capacity on the side of public administration.
Regarding management and maintenance of UGS, better communi­
cation by forest and green space managers with the public, having 
certified tree care professionals and implementing tree care standards 
may counter the lack of knowledge of tree care or forest management 
operations. Indeed, quality communication with the public is helpful in 
promoting better understanding of tree care practice and forest man­
agement strategies (Janse and Konijnendijk, 2007). Also, more focus 
should be on tree disservices perceived by the public, which was 
strongly connected to UGS maintenance in this study. Furthermore, our

Soundscape quality of communal open space (COS) in residential housing estates has been less thoroughly
investigated. This paper aims to reveal the intricate relationships among physical sound, visual and thermal
environments and their respective human perceptions, and soundscape quality, as well as their relative in-
fluences. To this end, a path model was formulated with aid of 1749 face-to-face field interview responses
collected via questionnaire surveys together with physical measurements at the COS of nine residential housing
estates in Hong Kong. The model findings suggested that pleasantness and landscape visual quality, as well as
thermal acceptability were the primary drivers for soundscape quality of COS. The size of effects of thermal
perceptions on soundscape quality was found comparable to those of aural and visual perceptions. In turn,
pleasantness was more influenced by perception of dominant vehicle sound, human sound and birdsong than
physical sound properties and psychoacoustics factors. Visual quality of landscape also played an important role
on soundscape assessments via visibility of greenery and sky. Besides, thermal acceptability was found to directly
influence soundscape assessments instead of thermal comfort or sensation. Above all, our findings provide
valuable insights for residential housing estate designers in formulating strategies that can effectively improve
soundscape quality in COS in compact city environments.
1. Introduction
1.1. Literature review
Global urbanization trend has caused a rapid increase in the number
of populations dwelling in dense city areas. Within dense living envi-
ronments, the demands for public open spaces have been escalating as
they can offer precious opportunities for people of different ages to
undertake different types of leisure and recreational activities and
escape from hectic city life [1]. Various forms of open space, ranging
from sports fields to city parks and linear corridors, have often been
provided by city government. Given cramped dwelling space for ma-
jority of people in dense city areas, communal open space (COS) has
often been provided within residential housing estates to facilitate social
connections and foster a feeling of community among housing residents
by offering valuable extra space [2].
Recognizing the benefits of public open space offered to urban living,
there are intense interests in formulating urban design and planning
strategies that can effectively improve the quality of open space, with an
ultimate objective of increasing the number of visitors and frequency of
their visits, and alluring them to stay longer [3]. Apart from macro-scale
factors like accessibility [4], and landscape connectivity [5], a number
of micro-scale factors including function [6], aesthetic appearance [7],
spatial configuration of landscape [8], physical facilities [7], thermal
environment [9] and soundscape quality [10] have also been identified
as determinants for vitality of open space.
Compared with all the micro-scale factors, soundscape, being
defined as the sound environment as perceived or experienced and/or
understood by people in context (ISO12913:1), has been received rela-
tively less attention. Soundscape quality of open space is influenced by
type of sound sources present, as well as the basic physical properties of
its sound sources like sound pressure level [11,12] and frequency [13],
and dynamic patterns like temporal profile and intermittency [14].
Given that open spaces often entail cocktails of different types of sounds,
their composition and variations are also determinants for soundscape
quality [15]. In addition, type of open space e.g., parks [15] and squares

In evaluating the soundscape of open spaces, it is crucial to recognize
that beyond the objective physical sound properties, human perception
significantly influences soundscape assessment as soundscape is a
perceptual construct of a sound environment [18–20]. Therefore, we
must clearly distinguish this perceptual construct from the tangible
physical surroundings.
Conceivably, human perceptions of sounds play an important role in
soundscape assessment. Preference and perception of specific types of
sounds by open space users have been shown to affect their soundscape
assessment of an open space. Positive sound assessments will be evoked
for perception of favorable sounds while negative assessments will be
evoked for perception of unfavorable sounds. People generally prefer to
hear natural sounds, e.g., water sounds [21,22] or birdsong [23,24], but
dislike mechanical sounds like vehicle noise [14,25] and human sounds
[15]. However, even for same types of sound sources, different fre-
quency spectra or temporal variation will evoke different soundscape
assessments. For instance, high pitch birdsong was more preferred to
low frequency one [26] and high sharpness water sound with less
temporal variation was less preferred [27]. Besides, the dominancy of
sound sources perceived by users was also found as a determinant of
soundscape quality in open space [28]. A higher soundscape quality
rating would be received if the most dominant sound type shifted from
artificial to natural [26,29]. Thus, further exploration is required to
determine whether specific types of sounds must be perceived as
dominant by open space users or if their mere presence is sufficient to
influence soundscape quality assessment.
As human perception is multi-sensory in nature, the cross-modal
interactions between aural and other types of sensory perceptions,
such as visual-aural interactions, have been determined to affect
soundscape assessment in open space. Visual landscape of open space
has been determined to affect its soundscape assessment through visi-
bility of specific natural and built landscape features. Generally, views of
natural features, e.g., greenery [21,24] and water [21], will help to
produce positive soundscape assessments. On the contrary, views of
some artificial features will produce negative soundscape assessments.
Visibility of roads produced negative effects on soundscape assessment
[14]. However, the effect of visibility of densely packed buildings on
soundscape assessment is less ascertained as they were found likely to
produce negative soundscape assessment in a street environment in
Seoul [30], but not in a high-rise residential area in Singapore [31].
In comparison, the interaction effects between thermal and aural
perceptions have been less comprehensively investigated in outdoor
open space, despite majority of earlier studies having only focused on
exploring their bivariate correlation relationships. People were found
more sensitive to the perception of noise in hot environments [32].
Hotter thermal condition tended to produce lower sound quality [33].
Conversely, thermal perceptions can also be modified by sound char-
acteristics. Different sound types would influence thermal comfort
differently [34,35]. Perception of music and natural sounds was found
positively correlated with thermal comfort in a campus open space in a
cold climate region [34,35]. The extent of improvement was found to
vary with type of sounds [35] and season [34]. Conversely, perception of
unwanted sounds like people crowd noise and machine noise would
decrease thermal comfort [34]. Besides, high sound pressure level
significantly deteriorated thermal comfort in summer [16]. Loud/noisy
sound sensation increased the thermal sensation in parks and squares in
a tropical climate city [36], and high traffic noise level slightly increased
the thermal sensation in squares during summer in a cold climate
country [16]. On the contrary, low sound level would produce a more
comfortable acoustic environment, leading to enhanced thermal com-
fort [35].
• Above all, there is still a number of research gaps that have not been
fully addressed. First, majority of findings have been focused on
exploring the effects of single aspects of multisensory factors on
soundscape quality. In addition, most of them reported only on the
bivariate relationships between soundscape quality and influencing
factors in terms of correlations instead of causation, which makes it
impossible to conclude which factor causes change in the other.
Second, there is a lack of understandings on the relative influences
and direction of effects of various types of sound sources on thermal
perception and vice versa despite majority of earlier efforts being
mainly focused on revealing the bivariate relationships between
thermal environment and perceptions, and acoustic comfort, and/or
their interactions. Third, majority of studies have been reported in a
piecemeal manner, or focused only on one perceptual dimension of
soundscape quality whose effects may not be fully applicable to the
overall soundscape quality. Above all, there is a lack of studies that
can holistically reveal whether and how the multisensory perceptual
and physical micro-environment attributes influence soundscape
quality in COS in residential housing estates in a high-dense or
compact city, which are affected by rich arrays of road traffic and
human sounds and built environment features. Accordingly, the
main objectives of the study are:
• to assess how the physical sound, visual and thermal environments
along with their perceptions by COS visitors, impact soundscape
quality in COS; to reveal their relative influences, and to explore
their intricate interrelationships; and
• to identify the sub-factors that influence aural, visual thermal de-
terminants of soundscape quality in COS, and to assess their relative
impacts.
These objectives were addressed by first constructing a path model
that can portray the intricate inter-relationships among soundscape
quality, multisensory perceptual and physical micro-environmental
characteristics, with particular emphases on simultaneously exploring
between their associations and interactions with soundscape quality as
relevant studies remain scarce. The determination of the major factors,
their interrelationships and their relative impacts on soundscape quality
can shed lights on formulation of effective strategies to enhance the
soundscape quality of COS in residential housing estates.
1.2. Hypotheses and conceptual framework
Table 1 lists all the main assumptions employed for developing the
conceptual model based on the findings identified from relevant refer-
ences. The years of review ranged from 1999 to 2024, using keywords
"soundscape, landscape, thermal comfort, soundscape descriptors,
soundscape indicators, psychoacoustic, outdoor urban spaces and
soundscape perceptions". For revealing all the potential relationships for
quality of public open space, we have included both the relationships
that have been previously confirmed and those have not fully confirmed
(i.e., H10, H11, H13, H14, H15 and H18) as our major hypotheses in the
model development. In addition, the studied factors have been further
categorized according to different types of sensory variables - auditory,
visual, and thermal as shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1 shown below [33,34,
36]. The visual factors include visibility of greenery, road and sky; the
auditory factors include perceived dominance of sound sources (e.g.,
vehicle noise, human sound and birdsong), LAeq, and Psychoacoustic
factors (e.g., fluctuation strength and sharpness); the thermal factors like
PET, thermal sensation, thermal comfort and thermal acceptability.
Based upon the above hypotheses, a conceptual model has been
proposed in Fig. 1 to illustrate the interrelationships among soundscape
quality, sound environment, visual landscape, and thermal environ-
ment, and people’s perceptions.
Firstly, soundscape quality refers to the overall perception and
evaluation of the acoustic environment in open spaces. It is influenced
not only by the perceived affective quality of the soundscape (i.e.,

Pleasantness and Eventfulness) but also by the visual quality of the

Secondly, it is hypothesized that the perceived affective quality of

Thirdly, the model proposes interactions between different sensory-
related factors, where the visibility of visual elements influences the
perceived dominance of sound sources, which in turn affects thermal
perceptual factors.
2. Methodology
In order to construct the proposed conceptual model and explore the
inter-relationships among various factors, a series of questionnaire
surveys were conducted concurrently with field measurements.
2.1. Site selection
Hong Kong as a coastal city has a humid subtropical climate with hot,
long and humid summers, as well as mild and short winters. COS is
commonly found in PHEs in Hong Kong, which are predominantly used
by residents and shared with other visitors as well as residents of
neighboring developments. As some public housing estates are located
in high-dense urban areas, densely packed high-rise towers further
aggravate the thermal comfort problems in COS induced by heat island
effects during hot seasons. In addition, high-rise towers frequently block
the scenery views from COS. For COS located on ground, they are
constantly exposed to noise and air pollution and the high-density as
well as high-rise of building morphology can also magnify traffic noise
problems. Accordingly, COS in PHEs is regarded as suitable survey sites
for investigation of acoustic and non-acoustic factors influencing the
soundscape quality in public open space. A total of 23 COSs were
selected from nine public housing estates (PHEs) in Hong Kong (Site A-
I), which included 10 play areas and 13 sitting-out areas (see Fig. 2). The
survey sites were chosen such that they covered a wide range of envi-
ronmental conditions, including varying levels of exposure to traffic
noise, as well as different visual and thermal conditions. In addition,
they covered functional diversity of COS, which included play areas,
known for higher activity and noise levels, and sitting-out areas,
intended for relaxation and quieter use. This approach enabled us to
examine how different types of functional space influence soundscape
quality under similar external environmental conditions.
2.2. Questionnaire surveys
The form of questionnaire surveys followed the guidelines laid down
by ISO/TS 12,913–2:2018 Standard for data collection and reporting
requirements of soundscape but with additional questions on thermal
sensation and acceptability. Respondents were randomly chosen within
the predefined boundaries of each site during each survey session. A
structured questionnaire was employed with details provided in Table 2,
which outlines the key questions and their corresponding rating scales.

individual respondents. The recording staff took pictures by following
the respondent’s orientation and viewing direction (See Fig. 3). The

2.4. Data collection and analysis
A series of field measurements was conducted in conjunction with
questionnaire surveys in three-time sessions from 09:00 to 18:30 during
weekdays and weekends between August 2021 and March 2023. The
collected subjective human responses and physical field measurement
data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 22.0, while a path
model was formulated using AMOS version 22.0.
3. Results
3.1. Descriptive statistics
In total, 1749 valid responses were successfully administered via
face-to-face interviews at the COS of nine PHEs. Each interview lasted
for about five to ten minutes on average. Table 3 shows the personal
details of all the respondents surveyed in the nine individual sites. About
46 percent of the respondents were men, and about half were between
ages of 20–59. Their average self-assessed auditory capacity was higher
than ’Good‘, and their average self-reported health condition was better
than ’Fair’.
3.2. Site characteristics and sound environment
Table 4 shows a summary of the major site characteristics and sound
environment of nine survey sites. As described before, the 23 COSs of
nine sites can be categorized into play area and sitting-out areas. As most
of our survey COSs were located in urban centers, they were closely
surrounded by high-rise buildings and busy traffic roads, resulting in
high and constant levels of background noise throughout the day. In
addition, they were occupied by large crowds of people for socializing,
leisure and rest. Many COSs were provided with greenery, which
attracted birds and provided shade (See Fig. 5).
3.3. Path analysis
Given the mild winter weather conditions in Hong Kong between
2021 and 2023, only one path model has been developed based on the
1749 questionnaire responses. These responses were recorded within a
narrow-recorded temperature range, with 69 % of the survey days
exceeding 27 ◦C. The sample size of 1749 responses is more than
adequate for constructing the model, surpassing the minimum recom-
mended sample size of 200 for structural equation modeling and
adhering the rule of thumb that requires 10 samples per variable (i.e. 21
variables in this study).
Table 5 shows our model results satisfied both the criteria for
acceptance values of the goodness-of-fit (GFI) tests and the root mean
square error of approximation (RMSEA) frequently used for assessing
the GFI of structural equation models, the constructed model is
considered a reasonably good representation of the interrelationships.
In addition, the reliability and validity of all the observed variables
have been evaluated. Specifically, the Cronbach’s alpha values for all
observed variables exceeded 0.7, indicating good internal consistency.
Furthermore, only the statistically significant path coefficients (p <
0.05) have been retained for our model construction, highlighting the
robustness and validity of the proposed model. Fig. 6 shows the con-
structed path model together with the estimated correlation values for
each of individual factors. All coefficient values have been standardized
to facilitate comparison. A low coefficient indicates a weak association,
while a high coefficient indicates a stronger causal relationship between
the independent and dependent variables. A positive coefficient suggests
a direct relationship, meaning that as the value of the independent
variable increases, so does the value of the dependent variable.
Conversely, a negative coefficient indicates an inverse relationship,
where an increase in the independent variable leads to a decrease in the
dependent variable. A dummy variable (i.e., ‘Child_Play areas’) has been
created for facilitating comparison of the differences among play areas
dominated by children’s play activities and others.

4. Discussions
With aid of the collected 1749 responses from COS visitors and
physical field measurement data, a path model has been successfully
formulated to provide a holistic view on how sound environment, visual
landscape and thermal environment-based factors, and their associated
human perceptual factors link to the assessments of soundscape quality
of a COS in PHEs in Hong Kong. To our knowledge, this is one of few
large-scale studies that has formulated a path model based on more than
1700 human perceptual responses and physical microenvironmental
data collected within a single study. With such a large number of
collected data, the model findings have successfully not only revealed
that soundscape quality had positive effects on open space quality, but
also revealed the intricate cross-modal interrelationships among the
related perceptual and objectively measured aural, visual and thermal
based factors in COS. In addition, they also provide a string of valuable
insights for residential housing estate designers and planners in formu-
lating strategies that can effectively improve soundscape quality in COS
in compact city environments to enhance well-being of their residents.
They are discussed in detail as follows.
First, the findings confirmed our hypothesis that the physical sound,
visual and thermal environments and their respective perceptions by
COS visitors significantly affect the soundscape quality in COS. Pleas-
antness plays a crucial role in auditory perception, significantly
enhancing soundscape quality with the highest relative influence
(relative influence =0.327) [28,43]. This is because pleasantness mir-
rors an individual’s emotional response to sound, which depends not
only on the sound’s physical properties, but also on how individuals
subjectively interpret it within a specific environment context. Conse-
quently, pleasantness emerges as the primary factor driving soundscape
quality, surpassing the physical characteristics of sound and under-
scoring its essential role in soundscape evaluation.

A B S T R A C T 
The increasing emphasis on vulnerable pedestrians and on the social aspects of active mobility, such as walking, 
has raised the profile of mobile methods for investigating mobilities in densely built environments. This paper 
examines the everyday mobility practices and perceptions of less mobile pedestrians, i.e., older adults and people 
with disabilities (PWDs), through mobile ethnographic interviewing. In total, 26 older adults and 7 PWDs were 
recruited for go-along interviews in localities familiar to them in Singapore. These semi-structured go-alongs 
generated a wealth of qualitative data covering topics ranging from their travel patterns to their perceptions of 
the built environment and their attitudes towards space sharing. Interview transcripts, field notes and video 
footage enabled an in-depth analysis of both the verbal and nonverbal aspects of the active mobility behaviour. 
The analysis revealed emerging sociospatial themes, including movement within an extended pedestrian 
network, such as detouring and taking shortcuts, and ambivalence towards space sharing caused in part by 
unclear rights-of-way among active transport modes and preconceptions of social norms. These themes have 
implications for how to shape travel behaviour based on a more inclusive approach to urban planning and 
neighbourhood design that instils a deeper humanistic understanding. 
1. Introduction 
Urban and transport planning has shown a resurgent interest in 
revitalising walking as a basic form of physical activity. By extension, 
there is a need for the built environment to better accommodate geri­
atric and aided pedestrian mobilities. Together, these two developments 
correspond to the two salient principles of inclusive street design: 
inclusiveness of all transport modes and all mobility capacities. With 
active transport and physical activity as key objectives of inclusive street 
design, extensive research has been conducted on the effects of the built 
environment across macro-, meso- and microscales. This has generated a 
vast body of knowledge ranging from urban metrics (e.g., the land use 
mix) to pedestrian facilities that are useful for public health in­
terventions (Bauman et al., 2012; Heath et al., 2006; Saelens and Handy, 
2008; Sallis et al., 2006). In line with the parity of macroscale and 
microscale urban settings and elements, neighbourhood walkability 
studies have contributed to refining measurement instruments and 
facilitated comparisons between cities and regions (Wang and Yang, 
2019). However, aggregating built environmental attributes and corre­
lating them with physical activity at a neighbourhood level and above 
obscures the lived and affective experiences of individual pedestrians 
(Lee and Dean, 2018; van Cauwenberg et al., 2012), particularly those 
with limited mobility, as well as the interaction of the physical and so­
cial realms as experienced and negotiated by pedestrians (Castrodale, 
2017; Heath et al., 2006). 
In Singapore, the vision of inclusive streets is gaining ground in 
public discourse and in policymaking concerning urban development. In 
addition to enhancing the connectivity and serviceability of the road 
network and public transport system, the Land Transport Master Plan 
lists a “liveable and inclusive community” as one of three key focuses 
(LTA, 2013) that was subsequently recaptured under the “Walk Cycle 
Ride SG” vision (LTA, 2016a). This focus is congruent with the growing

2. Theoretical motivation 
2.1. Mobility needs of vulnerable pedestrians 
The development of the conceptualisation of accessibility and, by 
extension, walkability can also be seen as a perennial call for built 
environmental and societal inclusiveness, in which the field of envi­
ronmental gerontology is showing increasing interest (Cerin et al., 2017; 
S´anchez Gonz´alez and Roguez-Roguez, 2016). The heterogeneity of 
health conditions or impairments causes multifaceted accessibility is­
sues (Luiu et al., 2017). Although the association between walkability 
and walking for transport holds true throughout adulthood, the greater 
variability of mobility capability among less mobile people necessitates 
more tailored accessibility measurements (e.g., minutes versus metres as 
a distance metric) and corresponding planning interventions (Cerin 
et al., 2017). However, less mobile people, such as those who are 
partially sighted, may not be as recognisable in everyday encounters. 
Thus, the built environment (i.e., whether a street is friendly) may have 
a greater impact on these sensitive user groups. Broader street design 
should be able to cater to their special needs and accessibility barriers, 
such as slippery surfaces, narrow doorways, and dropped curbs with no 
adjacent tactile warning; these can be identified and removed to facili­
tate a smooth journey (Lavery et al., 1996). Similarly, in a pedestrian 
level-of-service scoring scheme, ten physical elements, including tactile 
paving and wheelchair-accessible drinking fountains, were singled out 
to estimate street accessibility for PWDs (Asadi-Shekari et al., 2013). In 
this vein, the myriad of built environmental factors associated with 
pedestrian transportation, such as the provision of pedestrian facilities, 
traffic management and safety, and the proximity of destination land 
uses (Ferrer et al., 2015), differ in terms of their sensitivity to individual 
factors rather than in the range between able-bodied and less mobile 
pedestrians. 
Reflecting critical thinking on accessibility in transport and urban 
planning is Martens’s (2015) recommendation to develop a “potential 
accessibility and mobility framework”. Such a framework would, for 
instance, stress the importance of people’s needs and their ability to 
pursue out-of-residence activities in transport-land use systems. 
Mobility needs go beyond the utilitarian dimension of accessibility and 
extend into more psychologically advanced levels, as Musselwhite and 
Haddad (2010) theorised, including people’s affective needs for inde­
pendence and control and aesthetic needs when the purpose of travel 
itself is hedonic experience. Meeting these needs is vital for the mental 
and social dimensions of quality of life that are less adequately 
addressed in transport planning (Lee and Sener, 2016), especially for 
older adults and PWDs. 
2.2. Sociality of walking 
The rich variety of walking types (K¨arrholm et al., 2017) and the 
complex social implications manifested through walking can be better 
investigated in mobilities studies (mainly qualitative) than in transport 
geography (mainly quantitative), which is mainly a derivative of posi­
tivist spatial science (Cresswell, 2011), or through the collaboration of 
the two disciplines (Hanson, 2015). Lager et al. (2015) employed the 
concept of social capital to study older adults’ everyday mobilities and 
explored their intra- and intergenerational social interactions afforded 
by walking and gatherings. Analogous to the analysis by Vanoutrive 
et al. (2018) of motorway network growth, the potential convergence of 
positivistic and hermeneutical stances in transport and mobilities 
studies is also relevant to the case of pedestrian networks in which so­
cietal factors such as politics in decision-making can significantly 
strengthen the explanatory power with respect to infrastructural 
development. Out-of-residence physical activity, or retirement homes in 
Middleton’s (2018) interview study, was found to be mediated by the 
affective and emotional aspects of walking experiences, which are 
pertinent to better person-environment fits. In Middleton’s (2018) ex­
amination of the microgeographies of everyday urban walking, the 
theme of what makes a “good pedestrian” emerges as a spatiotemporal 
practice that also involves social norms and identities. These studies, 
except Lee and Dean’s (2018), share an avoidance of committing to a 
priori theories and attempts to retain the contexts of mobility practices. 
This study aims to offer some grounded vignettes of how the bodily 
movement of less mobile pedestrians is reassembled into an intricate 
web of behaviour, perception, attitudes and preferences within the 
spatiotemporal constraints of a pedestrian trip. These vignettes may 
serve as inputs for the formulation of future confirmatory studies on one 
or more active mobility aspects discussed in this paper. Bearing this aim 
in mind, in the next subsections, we describe the study area and the 
methods of data collection and analysis. 
3. Material and methods 
3.1. Conducting go-along interviews 
Go-along interviews were conducted in Singapore, which had a 
tropical climate with a mean annual temperature of approximately 27 ◦C 
(MSS, 2018), a population of 5.61 million and a land area of 719.9 km2 
at the time of data collection (DOS, 2017). The total population con­
sisted of 3.97 million residents (3.44 million Singaporean citizens and 
0.53 million permanent residents) and 1.65 million non-residents who 
held long-term permits to stay or work in the country (DOS, 2017). 
Given the reliance on rich qualitative data to reveal both the physi­
cality and sociality of everyday walking experiences and mobility 
practices, on-the-move methods are a compelling choice. Often situated 
in the new mobilities paradigm (Cresswell, 2011; Sheller and Urry, 
2006) or the mobility turn (Faist, 2013), the need for cross-disciplinary 
thinking for mobility practices has thus spawned diverse applications of 
mobile methods that enable researchers to be in the same physical 
setting as and a more collaborative environment with mobile research 
subjects. As a hybrid of unobtrusive, detached observation (Tomi´c et al., 
2015) and reflective sit-down interviews, go-along interviewing is an 
effective tool that facilitates a researcher’s task through the participa­
tion in movement to unearth inner layers of the research subjects’ lived 
experiences in the dimensions of perception, spatial practices, bi­
ography, social architecture, and the social realm (Hein et al., 2008; 
Y. Wang et al. 

Journal of Transport Geography 115 (2024) 103811
3
Kusenbach, 2003). At the conceptual level, our approach responds to 
van Wee’s (2016) accessibility research agenda, particularly with 
respect to slow modes and accessibility perceptions, and Cerin et al.’ 
(2017) synthesis that built environment perceptions are likelier to pre­
dict active travel than objective measurements. At the operational level 
(Amaya et al., 2022), all go-along interviews were arranged in urban 
areas but the routes were chosen by the participants rather than desig­
nated by the researchers (Sun and Lau, 2021) as an attempt to strike a 
balance between control for inter-individual variability and accommo­
dation for participants’ self-efficacy. 
Drawing from a previous similar methodology (Ferrer et al., 2015; 
Simons et al., 2014), greetings and questions about general travel pat­
terns were not employed as paper-and-pencil questionnaires but rather 
served to warm the participants up for the go-along interviewing format 
and determine the pedestrian routes that were familiar to the partici­
pants. This introductory part usually took place at the beginning of an 
interview in a seated setting, after which the interviewer accompanied 
the participant on a pedestrian excursion. The transition question on the 
notion of inclusive streets was intended to be a stimulus, and the in­
terviews dwelled mainly on pedestrian experiences, attitudes towards 
space sharing among various street users and desirable features. See 
Supplementary Material 1 for the semi-structured go-along field guide. 
One-on-one go-along interviews took place in a mundane and day-to- 
day setting outdoors in the daytime, mostly starting from a mass rapid 
transit (MRT) station or the participant’s residence. The participants’ 
choice of interview location was consistent with the general mobility 
pattern of older adults in Singapore, which is concentrated in public 
housing estates that are typically equipped with retail and recreational 
facilities (Cao et al., 2019; Koh et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 1, the 
interview locations in urban areas were geographically spread across the 
main island of Singapore. The interviews were recorded using an action 
camera. The length of an interview depended on the route that the 
participant and the interviewer discussed and agreed on at the outset as 
well as the participant’s willingness to show the interviewer around. 
These factors resulted in an effective audio/video length (on foot or 
wheelchair and excluding trip portions on a bus or any other transport 
mode) ranging from 10 to 70 minutes (with a median of 28 minutes). 
Approval for the go-along method was obtained from the Institutional 
Review Board of Nanyang Technological University prior to the in­
terviews. All participants were offered a supermarket voucher worth 20 
Singapore dollars as reimbursement for their time. 
The go-along interviews (n = 33) were conducted in two phases. In 
the first phase in the first quarter of 2017, convenience sampling using 
the contacts of previous research projects, which was later supple­
mented by snowballing, was employed to recruit older adult participants 
(n = 26) who were between 64 and 79 years old and were able to travel 
independently without the use of mobility devices or walking aids. In the 
second phase in March 2018, outreach to enlist the help of relevant 
voluntary welfare organisations (VWOs) resulted in additional partici­
pants (n = 7) being introduced from the PWD groups with vision or 
physical impairment (two partially sighted people, one blind person and 
four wheelchair users between 21 and 49 years old). In the combined 
sample, there were 14 males and 19 females; 26 interviews were con­
ducted in English, and seven were conducted in Chinese. All participants 
were Singapore citizens. 
3.2. Coding the interview transcripts and thematic analysis 
Field notes were produced within two days of each go-along inter­
view to describe the route taken and to record pertinent responses and 
observations of interest. The audio recordings were then transcribed 
verbatim, and the transcripts were imported into NVivo 12. Two-cycle 
descriptive coding was performed to facilitate theme identification 
(Salda˜na, 2016). The first cycle of descriptive coding employed text 
searches for any mentions of built environmental factors, and further 
inspection excluded search results that yielded irrelevant meanings. This 
count component, which shows the frequency and distribution of spe­
cific keywords, is intended to aid in the extraction of themes in 
perspective and to complement the subsequent in-depth thematic 
analysis (Guest et al., 2011). The second cycle relied on the primary 
qualitative data obtained in both verbal (i.e., reading the transcripts) 
and nonverbal (i.e., viewing the video footage) forms. The data involved

4. Descriptive results – a snapshot of the interview contents 
This section briefly presents an overview of what types of road users, 
as well as the interactions among them, that the participants mentioned 
during the go-alongs and the participants’ reactions to the free associ­
ation question on inclusive streets. Many older adult participants 
mentioned pedestrians in the context of pedestrian footpaths and in­
teractions with on-road motorists and other users. However, this 
keyword was not mentioned as frequently as other transport modes, 
such as cycling and public transit, perhaps because being engaged in the 
practice of walking reduced the need for self-referencing as a pedestrian. 
The even lower frequency of mentions of pedestrians by only one PWD 
participant may be partially attributed to the fact that four of the seven 
participants were in wheelchairs; thus, the act of walking per se was not 
directly applicable. Nevertheless, as illustrated in Fig. 2, almost all of the 
participants repeatedly mentioned the act of walking and items related 
to transit riders, such as buses and the MRT. This largely reflects the 
nature of go-along interviews, as some entailed transit rides as part of 
the participants’ everyday mobility practices and of Singapore’s typical 
built environment. The absence of intentional prompts in the space- 
sharing questions notwithstanding, motorists were mentioned as 
frequently as cyclists were. As pedestrian travel involves movement 
along paths and negotiation at points of conflict, what first came to 
mind, according to the participants, was the interaction between pe­
destrians and motorists, which poses a safety threat. Although personal 
mobility devices (PMDs) were consciously included in the space-sharing 
questions in response to the introduction of the code of conduct for the 
safe sharing of paths (LTA, 2016b) (for discussion, see Section 6.3) and, 
consequently, the popularity of PMDs, they were not mentioned as 
frequently as cyclists were. This result can be explained by the similar­
ities of the physical setting and the behaviour of cyclists and PMD riders 
along a pedestrian footpath, which is the most likely location on the road 
cross-section for them to coexist; in general, the discussion of such in­
teractions mutually applies to each other. Furthermore, it was implicit in 
the participants’ accounts that the speeds of non-motorised bicycles and 
motorised PMDs make a substantive difference to pedestrians, who are 
often the most vulnerable users in terms of the potential impact of 
collision and perceived safety. 
The free association question about the phrase “inclusive streets” led 
to confusion among almost all of the participants, who asked for a 
repetition or explanation. After the researcher explained the concept in 
lay terms by saying that inclusive streets are designed for everyone (i.e., 
transport modes and mobility capability), most participants still did not 
understand the terminology, which resulted in a relatively low fre­
quency of the word “inclusive”. However, the participants shared their 
mostly positive opinions about the concept and were quick to voice some 
desirable features of their own. For example, participant A11 (female, 
68, part-time worker) did not understand the “terminology”, but as we 
walked through the apartment blocks in Tiong Bahru Estate (Fig. 3), she 
expressed that inclusive streets could “provide for people more facilities 
that they need like shelter, easier access … and safety for the elderly, 
things to hold on for safety” along streets. This response pattern reaf­
firmed a communicative challenge that had been foreseen from the 
outset in terms of the semi-structured interview design. In general, the 
participants tried to understand the concept of inclusive streets, prob­
ably in part due to the widely known phenomenon of social desirability 
or because they wanted to help in a researcher-participant setting. 
Including the terminology in the questions enriched the verbal aspect of 
the interviewing process insofar as it acted as a cognitive stimulus. 
5. Thematic analysis results – sociospatial themes 
This section presents the sociospatial themes that emerged from the 
verbal and nonverbal data collected. The field notes prepared immedi­
ately after each interview also served as useful supplements for identi­
fying emerging themes, contradictions or ambivalent attitudes through 
triangulation with the two aforementioned major data sources. The 
pedestrian routes taken were plotted and exported in KML format (see 
Supplementary Material 2). For visualisation purposes, when the file 
was opened in Google Earth, the base aerial image was selected such that 
the image date was as close to the interview date as possible. The 
overlaid images are screenshots of the go-along video footage. The 
footage was taken by the action camera fixed on the researcher’s 
shoulder to avoid causing discomfort to the participants, and the 
perspective of the researcher could be approximated as that of the 
participant during the go-alongs. As with the routes, a high proportion of 
semi-outdoor (e.g., sheltered walkways) and indoor locations prevented 
the action camera from obtaining good-quality satellite signals, so the 
pedestrian routes were plotted manually instead. 
5.1. Cut-throughs and informal paths 
Cut-throughs and informal paths were found to form part of the 
extended pedestrian network, including for less mobile pedestrians. As 
expected, footpaths along streets, which were apparently preferred 
among older adults (Booth et al., 2000), were only one component of a 
wider pedestrian network. Participants would at some point veer off 
major roads and enter a local, intricate pedestrian network. In the order 
of relative position from major roads, the elements of such a pedestrian 
network include walking surfaces outside buildings; sheltered footpaths 
along building frontages, which are a prominent feature of traditional 
shophouses as well as commercial buildings; footpaths linking to other 
destinations; and alternative routes through buildings, which, for 
instance, are facilitated by a direct linkage between transit nodes and 
commercial buildings. In the case of Singapore’s public housing estates, 
which occupy a large proportion of the urban landscape, crisscrossing 
internal footpaths and void decks, which are typical design features at 
the ground level of public residential blocks, add porosity to pedestrian

5.2. Detouring versus jaywalking 
The adaptive behaviour of detouring and jaywalking occurred during 
the encounter of accessibility barriers, but this behaviour also represents 
the possibility of converting accessibility constraints into opportunities. 
Steps and stairs are major barriers that the participants had to circum­
vent; these barriers were identified in other go-along studies in 
Singapore (Cao et al., 2019; Moˇcnik et al., 2022) and are common in 
densely populated cities. For example, Hong Kong’s convoluted pedes­
trian network of at-grade streets, overhead footbridges and underground 
passageways tends to be a daunting task for older adults to navigate (Sun 
and Lau, 2021). Although Singapore’s urban density may not be as high 
as that of Hong Kong in general, some of the adaptive behaviour of 
detouring that occurred in our study was quite striking. From her home 
to her destination (a shopping mall on a major artery by bus), participant 
A5 alighted on the same side. However, on the way back, she had to 
access the bus stop on the other side of the road, which can only be 
crossed using a pedestrian overhead bridge that has yet to be fitted with 
an elevator. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the unfortunate inaccessibility due to 
her physical constraints was compensated by a bus ride that happened to 
take a U-turn nearby. 
“Because of my legs. And I also cannot take this (bridge). … I can’t 
take, I can take, but I think I need one and a half hours”. [At another 
location, further explaining the same problem] “Long ago, they 
didn’t have the barriers. So, I could just run across. Jaywalked. Now, 
they want us to use the overhead bridge, but then, for people like me, 
it’s difficult. With my legs with arthritis, very difficult. … I will show 
you where I always get another bus to come back here. Crazy, right?” 
(A5, female, 66, retiree). 
“People will feel that it is more convenient to walk (across) than to

flexible” (A12, male, 72, part-time worker). 
To a lesser degree, the stairs in the underground pedestrian network

partially-sighted participant B1 from choosing this apparently more 
comfortable route over the ground-level route (Fig. 6). At the ground

challenge, particularly when crossing the street. However, instead of 
adhering to formal traffic rules, the participants made informal crossing 
decisions based on the need for direct routes, their perceptions of traffic 
safety, their self-awareness of visual disabilities and how visible such

“Here, I will wait and make sure (there is) no car, or I just follow.

people will know. Some passers-by will also help us. Then, if this 
driver sees, they will also slow down” (B1, female, 44, partially

5.3. Ambivalence towards space sharing 
Some go-alongs revealed an ambivalence towards space sharing, 
which was implicated in underappreciated traffic rules as well as the 
perceived identity of other users. The discussion of space sharing among 
pedestrians, cyclists and other road user groups reflects the topic’s 
multifaceted nature beyond the provision of pedestrian and cyclist fa­
cilities and includes a desired mutual understanding of considerate user 
behaviour. Separate paths for pedestrians and cyclists seemed to be an 
obvious infrastructural solution, as suggested by the participants.

6. Discussion 
This section mirrors the structure of the previous section, provides 
further discussion based on the aforementioned sociospatial themes, and 
frames them in broader urban mobility and social contexts. 
6.1. Extended pedestrian networks 
Alternative informal paths and public spaces (Fig. 4), including void 
decks that are ubiquitous at the ground level of public housing estates, 
constitute an important part of the pedestrian network that the partic­
ipants actually use to navigate. Some of these paths or physical elements 
are highly symbolic: they implicitly function in a way similar to traffic 
signs, allowing or prohibiting certain travel behaviours. For example, an 
opening in a median (see Fig. 5) or in a fence subliminally serves as 
permission or as an invitation to cross the road informally at a specific 
location. Another example is the well-trodden paths on the grass verges 
of streets, as a materialisation of desire lines. 
Pedestrian behaviour has been found to be associated with a variety 
of built environment elements (Adkins et al., 2012; Boarnet et al., 2011) 
and is mediated by knowledge and perception (Booth et al., 2000; 
Herbolsheimer et al., 2021; Herrmann-Lunecke et al., 2021). This 
mediation effect is consistent across vastly different urban contexts, such

as North America (Herbolsheimer et al., 2021) and Latin America 
(Herrmann-Lunecke et al., 2021), but close attention to microscale 
characteristics remains imperative. The evaluation of accessibility or 
attractiveness is based on a known set of links; thus, informal or un­
conventional paths, such as the cut-throughs observed in buildings and 
across planted verges, may be overlooked. At the city level, desire lines 
have attracted scholarly attention, and a recent study modelled desired 
transport corridors compared with the existing road network (Bahbouh 
et al., 2017). To borrow a critique of long-haul mobilities, Hughes and 
Mee (2018) caution that the narrow focus on predetermined paths may 
spuriously frame journey disruptions as an intrinsically negative force. 
Regarding active mobility for shorter distances, this calls for a higher- 
resolution survey of extended pedestrian networks and land use pat­
terns (Iacono et al., 2010). Nevertheless, our study suggests that there is 
a major limitation to the sole use of objective measurements given the 
semiotics of the built environment. The missing links in existing GIS 
databases and unmet mobility needs must be identified prior to ana­
lysing accessibility more systematically in conjunction with perceptions 
and emotions. 
Similarly, the connectivity level of pedestrian networks should be 
based on consideration of the informal paths and public spaces that 
already function in people’s everyday mobilities and the desired yet 
missing links, which will benefit not only less mobile pedestrians but 
also all pedestrians. The go-alongs focused on individual pedestrian 
movement and blurred the line between formal and informal compo­
nents in an extended pedestrian network that transcends the public and 
semi-public domains. Moreover, the planning, construction and man­
agement of such an integrated pedestrian network will require the 
continued collaboration of various stakeholders. For example, the route 
choice of participant B1 (see Fig. 6) to avoid underground pedestrian 
networks due to frequent changes in level suggests that, unfortunately, 
the devil is in the details. In 2004, the Urban Redevelopment Authority 
released a master plan for underground pedestrian links in the Orchard 
Road area, which identified critical links whose construction would be 
subsidised by the government and was extended until 2022 (URA, 
2021). The lukewarm adoption of the plan by mall owners and de­
velopers (ST, 2016) and the recently announced plan to revamp Orchard 
Road and to enhance ground-level urban vibrancy indicate that inclu­
sive streets for both traffic and human activity are prioritised in street 
design. Such prioritisation also offers opportunities to make the built 
environment more inclusive by monitoring and rectifying inaccessible 
design elements in a more effective manner. To that end, a variety of 
street auditing instruments have been used to survey the physical ele­
ments along street segments and to evaluate the provision of primarily 
active transport modes (Boarnet et al., 2006; Day et al., 2006; Millstein 
et al., 2013). The findings from older adults and PWDs reveal that more 
attention should be given to the fine details of the built environment to 
ensure a smooth and hazard-free journey for everyone. 
6.2. Converting accessibility constraints into opportunities 
The unintended detour travel (Fig. 5) elucidates the intertwining 
relationship between accessibility and connectivity and the profound 
impact that it has on shaping the travel patterns of less mobile pedes­
trians. One street connectivity measure is the number of connecting 
sidewalks or crosswalks (Clifton et al., 2007), which was found to be 
positively associated with the prevalence of walking on streets (Monir­
uzzaman and P´aez, 2016). In a broader sense, grade-separated and at- 
grade crossing facilities both contribute to street connectivity, 
although accessibility issues for less mobile pedestrians may be masked 
due to the oversight of microscopic elements such as stairs. While 
reducing such inaccessibility, for example, by installing lifts at pedes­
trian overhead bridges, appears to be straightforward, a fuller picture of 
the resulting adaptive behaviour is needed to understand the mobility 
practices of vulnerable pedestrians. In this light, H¨agerstrand’s (1970, 
1989) conceptualisation of space-time constraints is instrumental: 
capability constraints in the form of limited physical ability interact with 
the other two types of time-space constraints: coupling constraints, 
which take the form of “bundles” due to personal commitments or other 
utilitarian purposes, and authority constraints, which control entitle­
ment to resources through “domains”. The observed adaptive detouring 
can thus be regarded as an outcome of the interaction of tightened 
capability constraints and relaxed coupling constraints (H¨agerstrand, 
1970; P´aez and Scott, 2007) among retired older adults. This finding 
also corresponds to previous research that found that the overall impact 
of the built environment on the unmet travel needs of older adults was 
limited, especially for those with a car (Haustein and Siren, 2014; Luiu 
et al., 2017), which can be realised only with substantial extra effort. 
Some interviews included a bus or train ride in between to capture 
the participants’ active mobility behaviour in the first and last stages of 
the trip. The extra time spent could be simply treated as “deadweight”, 
but the transit time was not a complete waste because the conversation 
continued, lending some anecdotal support to the notion that travel time 
is not always unproductive (Lyons and Urry, 2005). Specifically, this 
chained mobility practice, either voluntary or involuntary, resonates to 
Bissell’s (2018), p. 76) participant observation of and introspection into 
how time is produced and reproduced through commuting routines, 
albeit with a warning of over-optimism about commute productivity 
against the precarity of certain commuters, which is applicable to less 
mobile pedestrians. Therefore, an integrative approach is needed to 
understand the negative effects of the remaining inaccessible features in 
the built environment, to address the component of waiting and stillness 
in mobility practices (Cresswell, 2012) and to recognise the potential 
offset of the concurrent use of travel time for transport and activity, 
either on foot or in transit. 
6.3. Towards a space-sharing culture in non-motorised transport 
The challenge of space sharing is more acute in Singapore because 
pedestrian footpaths are typically only 1.5 m wide. This issue is exac­
erbated by the growing popularity of PMDs and the introduction of 
dockless systems of shared bicycles with the ensuing incivility problems 
such as indiscriminate parking. The confusion about and contentious 
claims by the participants over space sharing, particularly between pe­
destrians and cyclists, reflect a potentially steep learning curve of the
