A B S T R A C T
Understanding the relationship between the audiovisual environment of streets and cycling 
behavior is crucial for designing more inclusive and responsive transport environments. However, 
most previous studies have concentrated on the macro-level aspects of the built environment, 
such as land-use mix, while overlooking the street-space quality characteristics that influence 
cyclists’ on-site perceptions. Although research has explored the role of objective visual features, 
studies on the audiovisual environment of streets and its perceived impact remain scarce. This 
study combines street view imagery, Strava Heatmap data, and interpretable machine learning to 
investigate the nonlinear and interactive effects of street-level spatial quality on cycling volumes. 
The results indicate that (1) auditory characteristics—especially noise intensity and sound 
quality—exert a significant and often stronger influence on cycling volume than visual features 
alone, challenging visual-centric planning assumptions; (2) several spatial features show 
nonlinear or threshold effects (e.g., green view index and soundscape eventfulness exhibit 
inverted U-shaped curves), indicating that excessive environmental richness may reduce cycling 
appeal (3) synergistic and antagonistic interactions exist between visual and auditory ele­
ments—for instance, natural sounds can mitigate the negative impact of high noise, while 
enclosed visual environments amplify it. These findings provide empirical evidence that opti­
mizing—rather than maximizing—street-level sensory stimuli is essential for promoting active 
travel. Building on this insight, our study offers practical guidance for policymakers and urban 
designers to implement more targeted and context-sensitive interventions, and underscores the 
potential of integrating audiovisual design into sustainable transport planning.

Methods
3.1. Study area
In this study, we selected the inner city of Melbourne, Australia, as our case study area, based on official boundary data (Ma et al., 
2023). This selection is justified by Melbourne’s status as one of the regions with the highest cycling frequency in Australia (Pistoll and 
Goodman, 2014). The area is characterized by a high-density, mixed-use core surrounded by significant urban expansion (Pistoll and 
Goodman, 2014). Additionally, the flat terrain and mild climate of the inner city provide ideal conditions for year-round cycling 
(Pucher, Garrard and Greaves, 2011). Therefore, Melbourne offers an attractive case for studying the relationship between urban 
environments and public cycling behavior.
We utilized the City of Melbourne TravelSmart map, provided by the official Melbourne government (https://www.melbourne.vic. 
gov.au/), to select three types of cycling paths as our samples: off-road shared paths, on-road bike lanes, and informal bike routes. 
Furthermore, since cycling activities are also observed on other types of roads in the Strava Heatmap, we categorized these as 
miscellaneous bike paths and included them in our study sample (Fig. 1).
3.2. Data collection and preprocessing
3.2.1. Cycling volume
Cycling volumes were estimated using the Strava Heatmap, a GPS-based crowdsourced platform widely used by millions of cyclists 
who track their activities via smartphones or GPS devices (e.g., GPS watches). The heatmap aggregates cumulative GPS traces and 
visualizes them through raster tiles, where varying levels of color brightness represent relative cycling frequency (Dong et al., 2023a). 
Although these raster tiles do not provide absolute trip counts, they serve as a practical proxy for cycling volume, and have been widely 
used in urban mobility studies (Fischer, Nelson and Winters, 2022; Gao and Fang, 2025a; Hong, McArthur and Stewart, 2020). We 
extracted raster values using 20-meter buffers around each sample point, following prior work showing that this resolution offers 
strong explanatory performance in spatial modeling (Dong et al., 2023a). Raster values are classified into discrete categories corre­
sponding to brightness intensity, with brighter tiles indicating higher levels of cycling activity. While this classification scheme limits 
the precision of volume estimation, it enables consistent spatial comparison across urban areas.
To construct the cycling volume layer, we used Python to download and mosaic 72 high-resolution tiles (512 × 512 pixels each), 
covering the sampled districts in inner Melbourne. As shown in Fig. 2a, the resulting raster surface allowed us to approximate spatial

We acquired street view imagery data through the Google Street View Image (SVI) API to extract spatial quality features of streets.

cyclists. After filtering out missing points or indoor images, we obtained 35,416 valid SVIs from a total of 8,854 sample points. Next, 
the acquired SVI was classified and analyzed using DeepLabV3+ (Chen et al., 2017a,b), an advanced convolutional neural network 
architecture trained on the Cityscape dataset (Jiang et al., 2021). This model is widely used for accurately and efficiently extracting 
and measuring objective visual indicators (Qiu et al., 2022) and has shown excellent performance in urban street scene analysis (Jiang 
et al., 2021). Finally, the pixel ratio of each objective feature in the four-directional SVI for each sample point was calculated, as 
illustrated in Fig. 3.

ABSTRACT
In this study, five outdoor open areas were selected as the study sites to investigate the effects 
of thermal and acoustic environments, physical activity levels, and pleasantness on evaluations 
of the thermal and acoustic comfort in outdoor open spaces. Survey questionnaires and on-site 
measurements were used to quantitatively assess human thermal and acoustic comfort in 
outdoor spaces. The following findings were obtained in this study. The acoustic environment 
affected the thermal sensation, where reducing excessive outdoor noise levels could enhance 
thermal comfort. Increasing the physiological equivalent temperature amplified the acoustic 
sensation. Individuals reported the most comfortable acoustic perception outdoors at 
a physiological equivalent temperature of 30.1°C. Reducing both the thermal and acoustic 
indices improved the overall comfort to some extent. Decreasing physical activity levels by an 
appropriate amount enhanced the thermal comfort, acoustic comfort, and overall comfort. 
Finally, maintaining a sense of pleasantness was conducive to increasing the thermal comfort, 
acoustic comfort, and overall comfort. In states of moderate and pleasurable feelings (pleasant­
ness level = 0, 1, 2, 3), increased acoustic comfort and pleasantness combined significantly 
enhanced the thermal comfort.

quality (Lin et al. 2022), and interactions between multi­
ple environmental factors (Astolfi, Corgnati, and Verso

2020). These studies encompassed a wide range of sites 
and subjects, including indoor and outdoor spaces, such 
as offices (Bourikas et al. 2021), hospitals (Abd Rahman

ing individuals from various age groups, educational 
backgrounds, and occupations, including students, tea­

members (Kang and Zhang 2010). Climate laboratories, 
questionnaires, and field tests are commonly employed 
as methods in this type of research. Climate laboratories

A B S T R A C T
Although natural sounds are widely known to positively affect human well-being, the influence of natural sound 
tempo, a potentially significant factor in shaping emotional and behavioral responses, remains underexplored 
within environmental psychology. This study examines the impact of the tempo of natural sounds on individuals’ 
willingness for social interaction using a virtual site tour approach. Scenarios were created in Unity 3D, incor­
porating environmental sounds like birdsong and water fountains typically found in urban parks. Participants 
freely explored the simulated site and interacted with non-player characters, with their behaviors recorded. Their 
personal social condition, evaluations of the sound environment, and willingness for social interaction were 
assessed through questionnaires. This study found that faster water sound tempos significantly increased the 
willingness for social interaction among individuals with medium to high levels of personal social condition. 
However, changes in birdsong tempo had no significant effect on social interaction willingness. Participants with 
higher levels of social condition showed over 11 % greater willingness for social interaction and more positive 
evaluations of the sound environment. These findings highlight the role of sound tempo in influencing social 
behaviors, which may contribute to the development of environmental psychology and offer practical insights for 
urban soundscape design.
1. Introduction
As urbanization continues to expand globally, urban planners and 
environmental designers face increasing challenges in creating healthy 
and livable cities. Among the various factors influencing urban life 
quality, the sound environment plays a vital role. Research shows that 
natural sounds in urban spaces not only enhance individual well-being 
and health but also improve the overall urban experience and foster 
social interactions (Alvarsson et al., 2010; X. Chen & Kang, 2023; Fisher 
et al., 2021). Further exploring the specific effects of different natural 
sounds can offer valuable insights for shaping future urban design 
practices. This paper investigates the relationship between the tempo of 
natural sounds and people’s willingness for social interaction, aiming to 
integrate well-paced natural sounds into urban spaces to promote more 
meaningful social engagement.
1.1. Urban sound environment and social interaction
The global urbanization process has rapidly increased urban 
populations, making the creation of better urban living environments a 
significant and enduring focus (Ventriglio et al., 2021). The sound 
environment, a critical component of urban settings, significantly im­
pacts human health, psychology, and behavior (Andringa & Lanser, 
2013; Kang & Schulte-Fortkamp, 2016; Stansfeld et al., 2000). Urban 
noise from sources like traffic, construction, and human activities is 
considered a major threat to the quality of life and health of urban 
residents (Passchier-Vermeer & Passchier, 2000; Salomons & Pont, 
2012). Extensive research has linked prolonged exposure to excessive 
urban noise with numerous health issues, including hearing loss, car­
diovascular diseases, and increased psychological stress (Beutel et al., 
2016; Daniel, 2007; Münzel et al., 2021). Furthermore, noise pollution 
significantly affects work and daily life by reducing work efficiency, 
impairing sleep quality, diminishing life satisfaction, and weakening 
cognitive functions, which can adversely affect children’s development 
and language acquisition (Klatte et al., 2013; Muzet, 2007; Orru et al., 
2016; Pal & Bhattacharya, 2012).
In addition to these health concerns, social interaction is a vital 
component of psychological well-being and social functioning, and it is

Methodology
This study developed a novel virtual site interactive tour method to 
investigate how sound tempo influences individuals’ willingness to 
engage in social interactions among individuals with varying social 
conditions. Traditional methods for studying the influence of sound 
environments on individuals primarily include laboratory experiments, 
field surveys, and online questionnaires. However, these methods are 
insufficient to meet the objectives of this study. Participation in labo­
ratory experiments or surveys inherently involves social interaction, 
which may attract individuals already inclined towards social 
engagement. Since this study also seeks to explore the influence of sound 
environment changes on individuals with lower social interaction levels, 
an online method that avoids direct human contact may reduce partic­
ipant bias. A virtual city park scenario was developed using Unity3D, 
incorporating a social interaction system for participants to explore 
online. During the tour, participants control a character to move freely 
and interact with non-player characters. After the tour, participants 
were asked to complete a questionnaire covering their personal social 
conditions, tour experiences, sound environment evaluations, and per­
sonal information such as gender and age.
2.1. Sound sources
This study selected birdsong and water sounds as sound sources due 
to their prevalence in urban environments and their potential to 
enhance social interactions. Research has identified water sounds, 
birdsong, and music as the three preferred types of sounds in urban 
environments. Surveys indicate that over 75 % of respondents express a 
positive attitude toward water and bird sounds, whereas fewer than 10 
% find these sounds disturbing (Carles et al., 1999; Yang & Kang, 
2005b). This highlights the widespread public preference for natural 
sounds in urban settings. Studies have also shown that different types of 
birdsong exhibit varying effects on perceived restorativeness, but 
overall, the introduction of multiple bird species’ vocalizations tends to 
enhance the perceived restorativeness of a site (Zhao et al., 2020). Given 
that birdsong in natural environments is typically not singular, this 
study selected a mixed birdsong recorded in an urban park as the sound 
source. This mixed birdsong more closely replicates real ecological en­
vironments, providing a better simulation of the actual auditory expe­
rience in urban parks. Regarding water sounds, different types of water 
sounds may evoke distinct feelings. Some research has found that nat­
ural water sounds are more calming than artificial ones (G. R. Watts 
et al., 2009), while other studies have revealed that fountain sounds are 
preferred over waterfall sounds (Galbrun & Ali, 2013). This study 
selected fountain sound as the water sound source, as fountains are 
common landscape elements in urban parks, and their volume and 
tempo can be controlled by adjusting water pressure and flow. It is 
important to note that although both birdsong and water sound are 
categorized as natural sounds, they exhibit significant differences in 
signal characteristics and should be considered distinct auditory stimuli. 
As such, they cannot collectively represent the overall characteristics of 
natural sounds. This study simulated these two sound environments 
separately, and data from each condition were analyzed independently.
As natural sounds differ from music, specific BPM (beats per minute) 
values are not applicable. Therefore, the duration of a selected bird 
sound and a water sound was adjusted by shortening or extending them 
using audio editing software, without altering the pitch, to modify the 
tempo. A preliminary study was conducted to establish the acceptable

A B S T R A C T
Global mental health is facing challenges. Environmental factors, such as enhanced greenspace and reduced air 
and noise pollution, alongside physical activity, are assumed to be significant promoters of mental health. 
However, previous studies have not thoroughly investigated the combined effects of greenspace, air pollution, 
noise pollution, and physical activity regarding their impact on mental health. Moreover, there is a scant 
consideration of the subjective versus objective assessments of greenspace exposure. Therefore, this study aims to 
bridge these gaps by systematically exploring how objective and subjective greenspace, combined with air and 
noise pollution, impacts mental health through the mediation of physical activity. Data were gathered from 683 
participants in Hong Kong between November 19, 2021 and April 6, 2023, supplemented by NDVI data from 
Sentinel-2. Structural equation modeling and mediation analyses were employed. The findings indicated that (1) 
Objective greenspace does not necessarily affect perceived and real usage of greenspace; rather, the quality of 
greenspace plays a more critical role; (2) Physical activity significantly mediates the relationships between 
greenspace, air pollution, and noise pollution and mental health, with a more pronounced effect for greenspace; 
(3) Combined effects showed that greenspace has the most substantial total effect on mental health, followed by 
air pollution and noise pollution. Our study enriches the existing literature and suggests the necessity of inte­
grating urban greenspace planning with environmental governance, focusing particularly on greenspace quality.

ABSTRACT
Background: Urban environments increasingly cause sensory over­
load and psychological fatigue. Green spaces offer some relief, but 
few designs use silence as a therapeutic tool.
Aims: This study introduces Silent Art Landscapes (SALs), urban 
spaces that combine art and sound-moderating features. It explores 
how contemplative, acoustically sensitive landscapes can support 
inner peace and mental well-being in cities.
Methods: Using a mixed-methods approach, we conducted envir­
onmental soundscape assessments, on-site behavioral observa­
tions, and semi-structured interviews at three quiet urban art 
installations in dense metropolitan areas.
Results: Participants reported reduced anxiety, heightened emo­
tional clarity, and a sense of “mental refuge.” Reflective surfaces, 
sound-dampening topography, and interactive light-and-shadow 
installations contributed to a sense of tranquility.
Conclusions: This research demonstrates the potential of SALs as 
non-pharmacological multisensory interventions. This approach 
offers valuable implications for future urban planning, therapeutic 
design, and public health policy.

Introduction
The urban condition and the rise of sensory overload
Modern urban settings bombard residents with constant noise, visual clutter, and infor­
mation overload. These factors cause ongoing psychological strain and emotional imbal­
ance (Fan et al., 2025; Stansfeld & Clark, 2015; Xu et al., 2023). Research shows that noise 
from traffic and construction, especially, increases stress, worsens sleep, and lowers focus 
(Basner et al., 2013; WHO, 2018).
Green spaces in cities are known for their restorative effects (Hartig et al., 2014; Zhao 
et al., 2023). However, designers do not always prioritize tranquility and silence. Outdoor 
fitness zones, playgrounds, and visual installations often turn these spaces into busy hubs. 
They rarely become quiet places for retreat or contemplation. This highlights a design 
gap: cities need quiet refuges that combine art and landscape to enable healing through 
silence.

Urban sensory overload limits emotional recovery, especially in poorer neighborhoods.

Silence as a therapeutic medium
Silence is not just the absence of sound. It has cognitive, emotional, and even spiritual 
implications (Walkikar, 2024). The health literature links silence to lower cortisol levels and 
better autonomic balance. It is also tied to greater introspective awareness (Bernardi et al., 
2009). Neurologically, quiet environments stimulate the brain’s default mode network. 
This network helps with self-reflection and mental restoration (Raichle, 2015).
Few designers intentionally set silence as a design goal. Most healing landscapes 
emphasize appearance or ecology, using plants and visuals to calm visitors (Bell et al., 
2022; Marcus & Sachs, 2013). SALs differ by using art to enhance quiet rather than just 
blocking noise, as in parks. Designers consciously blend art and sound design to support 
well-being (Kang & Schulte-Fortkamp, 2016). Hosseini and Kowkabi (2023) showed that 
sound shapes well-being, but many urban designers neglect this need.
From soundscapes to silent art landscapes
SALs are small city landscapes that combine art and sound control. These spaces reduce 
noise and feature art that encourages calm and emotional recovery. 
These spaces may include elements such as: 
Sound-dampening topography (e.g. berms, earth mounds) 
Reflective surfaces that direct light but diffuse sound 
Art installations that create minimal, ambient, or meditative sounds 
Visual and spatial cues that encourage stillness and introspection
SALs embody a design philosophy that combines quiet landscapes with sound ecol­
ogy. Designers use earth mounds to block external noise, surfaces to reflect beneficial 
sounds, and calming sound art installations. These elements aim to create peaceful 
environments (Marcus & Sachs, 2013; Paraskevopoulou & Kamperi, 2018). Sound design 
in landscape projects directly reduces stress and improves well-being (Aletta, Kang, et al., 
2016; Payne, 2012).
Art as environmental intervention in public health
Art projects now promote mental health, such as through murals or workshops (Del Río 
Diéguez et al., 2024; Fancourt & Finn, 2019). These activities lower anxiety, help people 
manage emotions, and build connections (Camic & Chatterjee, 2013; Guo et al., 2025).

This study demonstrates the critical role of landscape contextual factors in shaping the acoustic

open spaces in densely populated cities. Specifically, this study aims to reveal the impacts of 
microscale functional space types, their interactions with specific types of ongoing activities or

Methodology
2.1. Soundwalk
Soundwalk approach was adopted in this study to collect field data capturing the participants’ perceptual responses to various 
attributes of the acoustical environment, including loudness, sound source dominance, affective qualities, and compatibility between 
soundscape and landscape (Jo & Jeon, 2021). Soundwalk can be regarded as a semi-controlled form of on-site surveys, having many 
advantages over traditional crowdsourced perceptual surveys and on-site investigation methods or controlled laboratory experiments. 
Typically, guided by a moderator, soundwalks lead individual participants or groups along a predefined route or asks them to walk 
within a predefined area to their preferred spots for in-situ collection of their feedback responses and descriptions of the audio-visual 
environment. Soundwalks provide context-rich and ecologically valid insights by capturing in-situ acoustic experiences, although they 
inherently involve less control over confounding factors related to site conditions and individual differences. Despite this, the 
soundwalk approach allows for the inclusion of a broader range of real-world variables than controlled laboratory experiments, 
making it particularly suitable for investigating complex, everyday acoustic environments. Furthermore, soundwalks offer a practical 
balance between methodological depth and time–cost efficiency.
In this study, the soundwalk protocol was informed by the assessment principles outlined in Section 5.2 of ISO/TS 12913-2:2018 
(ISO/TS 12913–2, 2018), with methodological adaptations made to suit the specific objectives and constraints of the research. The 
soundwalk trips were conducted during favourable weather conditions to minimize the influence of environmental disturbances such

as rain or strong winds. To capture temporal variations in the acoustic environment associated with daily activity patterns, each 
soundwalk route was conducted across three time sessions: morning (am), afternoon (pm), and evening (eve). Each soundwalk session 
lasted approximately 40 min. During each session, a research staff member acted as the moderator and guided a group of four to six 
participants along a predefined route within the study area. Participants were instructed to walk at a comfortable pace while paying 
attention to the surrounding acoustic and visual environment. At several predetermined observation points along the route, the group 
paused briefly to allow participants to focus on the sound environment. At each stop, participants were asked to listen to the sur-
rounding acoustic environment for approximately 1 min before completing a structured questionnaire evaluating their perception of 
the soundscape and associated environmental attributes. The moderator ensured that participants followed the same procedure at each 
stop and avoided discussing their evaluations with one another during the survey in order to minimize potential bias.
Participants who took part in the soundwalks were mainly students or acquaintances recruited from one university in Hong Kong. 
The participant selection was informed by observations from a pilot soundwalk conducted prior to the formal survey. Local residents of 
the public housing estates were found to be highly familiar with the surroundings and frequently interacted with ongoing activities in 
the area. During the pilot soundwalk, several residents greeted acquaintances or engaged in casual conversations, which distracted 
their attention from the auditory environment and shifted their focus toward social or visual interactions. To ensure that participants 
could concentrate on evaluating the acoustic environment during the soundwalk, local residents were therefore excluded from the 
formal survey. University students were considered appropriate participants because the objective of the study was to examine general 
perceptual relationships between soundscape quality and landscape contextual factors rather than long-term residential experiences or 
place attachment. Participants who were less familiar with the sites could provide more neutral evaluations of the environmental 
soundscape. In addition, students are commonly used in soundscape perception studies as they can follow structured soundwalk 
protocols and rating procedures consistently. Using a relatively homogeneous participant group also helps reduce variability caused by 
demographic differences, allowing the analysis to focus more clearly on the relationships between sound sources, spatial functions, and 
visual environmental factors. The group walked to and from specific locations, stopping at several selected points along the way.

Abstract The navigation of urban environments by blind adults and children is a complex activity. While
research on blind people’s navigation has often focused on assistive technologies, less attention has been
given to the embodied nature of their daily walking experiences. In this study, we apply chromatic
recurrence-based analysis to characterise the joint walking dynamics of a blind mother–child dyad and
explore their behaviour in diﬀerent acoustic contexts. Video-recorded sessions were coded, and their walk-
ing behaviour was characterised across diﬀerent acoustic environments. Results showed that mother and
child exhibited spontaneous synchronisation of their steps. Recurrence analysis indicated that the syn-
chronous walking intervals were shorter and more predictable, whereas asynchronous walking was longer
and less predictable. This case study suggests a ﬂexible use of synchronisation and non-synchronisation
patterns in a system of unstable coordination that is open to environmental contingencies.
1 Introduction
Shared walking among parents and children is a daily practice that has broad implications for child development,
dyadic dynamics, and interaction with the urban environment. Walking in urban settings provides children with
opportunities to exercise and consolidate basic motor skills on varying surfaces and along diﬀerent trajectories [1,
2]. For blind people, independent travel is possible supported by spatial cognition capabilities developed through
multisensory integration processes, cortical reorganisation, and enhanced spatial hearing [3, 4]. Traﬃc and other
urban sounds guide locomotion by enabling blind people to locate sound events, identify distant sources, and avoid
obstacles [5–7]. From infancy to adulthood, travel-related expertise is acquired through both informal practice and
systematic training in mobility aids such as white canes or guide dogs [8]. Urban street environments have been
described as ideal laboratories for studying blind people’s mobility-related strategies [9, 10]; however, few studies
have been conducted in such contexts. Most of the research has addressed urban walking from the viewpoint of
road safety for the blind [11–13], navigation aids [14, 15], or has examined how the absence of vision aﬀects adult
walking in controlled laboratory conditions [16].
Urban navigation requires parents and children to coordinate their steps to cope with changing conditions
that are not always predictable, for example, stopping when a car approaches a street intersection or resuming
walking after rounding a billboard located on the sidewalk. Although it is known that people who walk together
tend to spontaneously generate patterns of synchronised steps, no studies have been found that examine the
emergence of these types of coordination patterns in blind dyads. Chambers et al. [17] found spontaneous movement
synchronisation in naturalistic situations extracted from videos of adult people walking in pairs. Hajnal and Durgin
[18] observed that casual walkers tend to adjust their frequency and step length toward one another without
deliberate eﬀort. Sylos-Labini et al. [19] demonstrated that this occurs without vision if hand contact is allowed
between blindfolded adult pairs walking on treadmills placed side by side. Zivotofsky et al. [16] demonstrated that
participants who walked with tactile and auditory feedback showed greater synchronisation than those who only

A B S T R A C T
Urban commercial streets, as critical nodes of economic activity, exert substantial influence on consumer 
behavior and commercial performance. However, empirical research investigating how soundscape character­
istics and crowd density interact to shape economic behaviors in complex urban open spaces remains limited. 
This study addresses this gap through a case analysis of a typical commercial district in Yangzhou, China, 
constructing two integrated indicator systems. From a behavioral economics perspective, five economic behavior 
metrics are developed: Consumption intention intensity (CII), Affective consumption willingness (ACW), 
Queuing time (QT), Recommendation to friends (RTF), and Revisit intention (RE). Results reveal that both 
objective acoustic indicators (e.g., LAeq, L10, L50, L90) and perceptual dimensions of the soundscape (eventfulness, 
calmness, chaoticness) are significantly associated with these behavioral outcomes. Sound source typologies 
further moderate behavioral responses via perceived loudness, event frequency, and preference. In terms of 
spatial social density, the study introduces both planar crowd densities (CPD4, CPD8, CPD12) and façade crowd 
density (CFD) to capture horizontal and vertical perceptions of crowding. Among these, CFD demonstrates strong 
and consistent correlations with all economic behavior indicators. Based on these findings, the 
“Sound–Human–Economic (SHE)” model is proposed and validated to conceptualize the interaction mechanisms 
among acoustic environment, social density, and consumer economic behavior. Sudden sound pressure (SSP), 
CFD, and Educational level emerge as robust predictors. This research contributes to theoretical advancement in 
urban acoustic-behavior studies and offers practical implications for evidence-based soundscape design, crowd 
management, and consumer-centered urban commercial planning.

2.1. Study area
This study selects Dongguan Street in Yangzhou as the research site, 
primarily due to its vibrant commercial activity and its representative 
characteristics as an urban commercial street. As one of the core com­

Furthermore, according to official reports from the Yangzhou Daily, 
Dongguan Street received approximately 434,000 visitors during the 
2025 May Day holiday alone, underscoring its exemplary status in terms 
of commercial vitality and pedestrian density. These attributes make it a 
suitable and ideal case for soundscape studies in urban commercial 
environments [28].
