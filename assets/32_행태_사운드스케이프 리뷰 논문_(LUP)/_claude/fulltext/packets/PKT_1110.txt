A B S T R A C T
Understanding the relationship between the audiovisual environment of streets and cycling 
behavior is crucial for designing more inclusive and responsive transport environments. However, 
most previous studies have concentrated on the macro-level aspects of the built environment, 
such as land-use mix, while overlooking the street-space quality characteristics that influence 
cyclists’ on-site perceptions. Although research has explored the role of objective visual features, 
studies on the audiovisual environment of streets and its perceived impact remain scarce. This 
study combines street view imagery, Strava Heatmap data, and interpretable machine learning to 
investigate the nonlinear and interactive effects of street-level spatial quality on cycling volumes. 
The results indicate that (1) auditory characteristics—especially noise intensity and sound 
quality—exert a significant and often stronger influence on cycling volume than visual features 
alone, challenging visual-centric planning assumptions; (2) several spatial features show 
nonlinear or threshold effects (e.g., green view index and soundscape eventfulness exhibit 
inverted U-shaped curves), indicating that excessive environmental richness may reduce cycling 
appeal (3) synergistic and antagonistic interactions exist between visual and auditory ele­
ments—for instance, natural sounds can mitigate the negative impact of high noise, while 
enclosed visual environments amplify it. These findings provide empirical evidence that opti­
mizing—rather than maximizing—street-level sensory stimuli is essential for promoting active 
travel. Building on this insight, our study offers practical guidance for policymakers and urban 
designers to implement more targeted and context-sensitive interventions, and underscores the 
potential of integrating audiovisual design into sustainable transport planning.

Methods
3.1. Study area
In this study, we selected the inner city of Melbourne, Australia, as our case study area, based on official boundary data (Ma et al., 
2023). This selection is justified by Melbourne’s status as one of the regions with the highest cycling frequency in Australia (Pistoll and 
Goodman, 2014). The area is characterized by a high-density, mixed-use core surrounded by significant urban expansion (Pistoll and 
Goodman, 2014). Additionally, the flat terrain and mild climate of the inner city provide ideal conditions for year-round cycling 
(Pucher, Garrard and Greaves, 2011). Therefore, Melbourne offers an attractive case for studying the relationship between urban 
environments and public cycling behavior.
We utilized the City of Melbourne TravelSmart map, provided by the official Melbourne government (https://www.melbourne.vic. 
gov.au/), to select three types of cycling paths as our samples: off-road shared paths, on-road bike lanes, and informal bike routes. 
Furthermore, since cycling activities are also observed on other types of roads in the Strava Heatmap, we categorized these as 
miscellaneous bike paths and included them in our study sample (Fig. 1).
3.2. Data collection and preprocessing
3.2.1. Cycling volume
Cycling volumes were estimated using the Strava Heatmap, a GPS-based crowdsourced platform widely used by millions of cyclists 
who track their activities via smartphones or GPS devices (e.g., GPS watches). The heatmap aggregates cumulative GPS traces and 
visualizes them through raster tiles, where varying levels of color brightness represent relative cycling frequency (Dong et al., 2023a). 
Although these raster tiles do not provide absolute trip counts, they serve as a practical proxy for cycling volume, and have been widely 
used in urban mobility studies (Fischer, Nelson and Winters, 2022; Gao and Fang, 2025a; Hong, McArthur and Stewart, 2020). We 
extracted raster values using 20-meter buffers around each sample point, following prior work showing that this resolution offers 
strong explanatory performance in spatial modeling (Dong et al., 2023a). Raster values are classified into discrete categories corre­
sponding to brightness intensity, with brighter tiles indicating higher levels of cycling activity. While this classification scheme limits 
the precision of volume estimation, it enables consistent spatial comparison across urban areas.
To construct the cycling volume layer, we used Python to download and mosaic 72 high-resolution tiles (512 × 512 pixels each), 
covering the sampled districts in inner Melbourne. As shown in Fig. 2a, the resulting raster surface allowed us to approximate spatial

3.2.2. Street view image
We acquired street view imagery data through the Google Street View Image (SVI) API to extract spatial quality features of streets. 
First, sample points were extracted at 50 m intervals along street segments from OpenStreetMap. The 50 m sampling interval was 
chosen for its representativeness and better model fit, as demonstrated in previous studies (Jiang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024c). For 
each sample point, SVI was downloaded in four directions (headings 0◦, 90◦, 180◦, and 270◦) to cover the complete field of view of 
cyclists. After filtering out missing points or indoor images, we obtained 35,416 valid SVIs from a total of 8,854 sample points. Next, 
the acquired SVI was classified and analyzed using DeepLabV3+ (Chen et al., 2017a,b), an advanced convolutional neural network 
architecture trained on the Cityscape dataset (Jiang et al., 2021). This model is widely used for accurately and efficiently extracting 
and measuring objective visual indicators (Qiu et al., 2022) and has shown excellent performance in urban street scene analysis (Jiang 
et al., 2021). Finally, the pixel ratio of each objective feature in the four-directional SVI for each sample point was calculated, as 
illustrated in Fig. 3.

Based on existing research (Song et al., 2024; Zhou, Feng and Kemperman, 2023), spatial qualities are considered potential pre­
dictors that may influence cycling volumes. These features are detailed in Table 1 and are categorized by their nature into visual, 
auditory, and perceptual characteristics. The objective of our study is to explore the relationship between cycling volumes and the 
spatial qualities of streets by evaluating these qualities. Specifically, the spatial qualities of streets encompass landscape features, 
soundscape features, and the perception features of the street environment.
3.3.1. Landscape features
Landscape features are commonly defined as the pixel ratio of visual elements within Street View Imagery (SVI), widely used to 
represent the importance of eye-level perspective in activity studies (Ito and Biljecki, 2021). We selected 5 visual landscape indicators 
as the variables according to prior works (Rui, 2023; Yang et al., 2024c), including green view index (GVI), sky view factor (SVF), 
visual enclosure (VE), visual bikeability (VB), visual humanization index (VHI), and imageability (IMA). Specifically, the GVI aims to 
actively increase awareness of street vegetation distribution, which is well-documented to significantly influence cycling and jogging
