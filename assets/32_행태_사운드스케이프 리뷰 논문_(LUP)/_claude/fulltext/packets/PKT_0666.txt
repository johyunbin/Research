A B S T R A C T
With an emphasis on the role of parks in urban environments, studies on soundscape, which assess sound quality,
have focused on human perception. In particular, various studies have been conducted on the relationship
between human behavioral characteristics and their sound environment. However, there is still insufficient
research on human behavioral characteristics in the context of urban parks. The purpose of this study was to
investigate the effect of human behavioral characteristics on perceptions of soundscapes in urban parks.
Participants were asked to subjectively evaluate the soundscapes of three parks in Paris, France, using three
cognitive stages: indicators (perceived audio and visual elements), emotions (semantic expression), and cogni-
tion (overall quality of the park’s soundscape). The human behavioral characteristics that the participants
witnessed were categorized into whether there was social interaction, whether people remained in the park or
were passing through, and what kind of activities took place. To secure high ecological validity, audio-visual
information was provided to the participants using virtual reality equipment. The results demonstrated that
sounds made by other people decreased the perceived tranquility or peacefulness while the experience of the
park as being dynamic and lively increased. We also found that the primary determinant of park preferences was
not the type of human activities but the presence or absence of people.
1. Introduction
1.1. Soundscape research in urban parks
As the negative effects of noise in urban environments on people’s
health and social well-being became apparent (Passchier-Vermeer &
Passchier, 2000; Pulles, Biesiot, & Stewart, 1990; Tzoulas et al., 2007),
parks have come to be regarded as essential elements of sustainable and
healthy cities. Subsequently, the positive effects of urban parks on the
ecological environment and public good have been confirmed (Brown,
Schebella, & Weber, 2014; Tse et al., 2012). In particular, urban parks
are believed to offer important social and psychological advantages
(sources of positive emotions and beneficial services), and thereby en-
rich human lives (Chiesura, 2004). As a result, visual aesthetics and
sound quality have become important factors in designing urban
landscapes (Zhang, Yang, & Yu, 2006). In this context, the concept of
soundscapes, focusing on people’s perception of sound, has emerged
(Corburn, 2009; Kang & Schulte-Fortkamp, 2018).
International standard ISO 12913-1 (2014) defines soundscapes as
“acoustic environment as perceived or experienced and/or understood
by a person or people, in context.” To evaluate soundscape perception
in various spaces such as parks and open spaces, many studies have
examined the relationships between indicators (e.g., acoustical indices
and contextual factors) and descriptors (e.g., comfort, tranquility, and
soundscape quality; Brown, Kang, & Gjestland, 2011; Kang & Zhang,
2010; Yu & Kang, 2009). Among the studies on soundscape in urban
parks, there have been attempts to identify the factors that positively or
negatively affect perception using interviews (Szeremeta & Zannin,
2009); categorize parks according to their physical sound (Brambilla,
Gallo, & Zambon, 2013); explore non-sonic components (presence of
greenery, tranquility, and so on) that positively affect soundscape be-
sides sonic components, such as sound pressure level (Brambilla, Gallo,
Asdrubali, & D’Alessandro, 2013); and investigate the components that
affect soundscape comfort through audio-visual interactions (Zhang,
Zhao, Zenz, & Qiu, 2019). Deep learning methods to classify sounds
made in parks using an auditory attention model have also been used
(Filipan, Boes, Oldoni, De Coensel, & Botteldooren, 2014).
Based on previous studies, a soundscape perception model for urban
planning and design has been proposed (Herranz-Pascual, Aspuru, &
García, 2010; Hong & Jeon, 2015; Liu, Kang, Luo, & Behm, 2013).
Despite these efforts, urban soundscapes are occasionally unsatisfactory
because there is a gap between academia and practice that prevents

Methods
2.1. Study design
This study collected audio-visual soundscape stimuli from three
urban parks and analyzed the changes in soundscape perception caused
by changes in human behavioral characteristics using the type of park
and time as variables. Three different types of parks were selected (four
evaluation sites in each), and five temporal variations were set 30 s
apart from one another for each evaluation site. The study used the two
within-subjects design on the same participant group. To assess the
same experimental stimuli, a VR-based laboratory experiment, and a
questionnaire composed of three parts (indicators, emotions, and cog-
nition) were used to quantify soundscape perception. Human beha-
vioral characteristics were quantified and grouped into different cate-
gories (social interaction, stay conditions, and activity type) through
observations.
2.2. Soundscape stimulus
2.2.1. Case study area
To examine the behavioral characteristics of people in urban parks,
and their impact on soundscape perception, three parks with different
uses that are located near the Seine River in Paris, France, were se-
lected. Each park has a different size and use. Arènes de Lutèce (Park A)
is the smallest park and is surrounded by dwellings. Compared to the
other parks, it is relatively calm; a few people spend some time there,
and it has a courtyard in the center. Champ de Mars (Park C) is a large
park of 245,000 m2, and it includes the Eiffel Tower as a Paris landmark
and tourist destination. Jardin des Tuileries (Park J) is 280,000 m2 and
is similar to Park C as it is a tourist destination, but it is also used as a
place to relax. Therefore, Park J is a combination of Park A’s and Park

2.3. Assessment of soundscape perception
2.3.1. Participants
A total of 30 participants (male = 17, female = 13) aged from 20 to
31 years (mean age: 25.2, standard deviation: 2.11) took part in the
soundscape evaluation. The sample size was calculated using G-power
software version 3.0, and 27 participants were required for the medium
effect size (f = 0.25) with a statistical power of 0.8. Based on the re-
sults, the final number of participants was set to 30.
To reduce the variation, students from the same university enrolled
in architecture, urban, and sound-related studies were selected. To
reduce observation bias, they were trained in the soundscape evalua-
tion method through an audio-visual example stimulation recorded in
the city center (Hong & Jeon, 2017). However, the final purpose of this
experiment was not divulged to avoid prejudice in the evaluation re-
sponse. Additionally, they were thoroughly familiar with the use of the
experimental equipment, signed a consent form indicating their vo-
luntary participation, and were paid a participation fee afterward. The
participants had normal hearing.
2.3.2. Questionnaire
The questionnaire for assessing soundscape perception was com-
posed of three parts (indicators, emotions, and cognition) according to
the perception process, as illustrated in Table 2. Emotions and cognition
interact in complex ways, but emotions are related to “the conscious (or
unconscious) evaluation of events” and can be defined as “states eli-
cited by rewards and punishers.” Cognition can be defined as compre-
hensively controlled processes such as “memory, attention, problem-
solving, and planning” and “acquiring knowledge and understanding
through thought, experience, and the senses” (Pessoa, 2008). In other
words, emotions represent the affective state and affect cognition,
which is a comprehensive judgment. From this perspective, a ques-
tionnaire was constructed by separating emotions and cognition.
First, to examine the identification of the audio and visual indicators
that constitute the sound environment at each evaluation point, the
sound was evaluated with four elements (traffic, humans, birdsong, and
water) and the visuals with eight elements (vehicles, buildings, roads,
people, open space, greenery, water, and sky) using a five-point Likert
scale. The type of sound was set by referring to the five classification
systems of previous soundscape assessment studies (Hong & Jeon, 2015;
Liu et al., 2013) and the Soundscape Questionnaire Protocol (traffic,
fan, other noise, sounds from people, natural sounds; Berglund &
Nilsson, 2006; Nilsson & Berglund, 2006). For visual elements, the in-
dicators (vehicles, buildings, roads, open space, greenery, water,
people, and sky) used to evaluate the visual environment in landscape
studies were selected (Hong & Jeon, 2013; Jiang, Masullo, Maffei,
Meng, & Vorländer, 2018; Liu et al., 2014).
Second, in the emotions section, to examine the affective quality of
the soundscape, four studies (Axelsson, Nilsson, & Berglund, 2010;
Kang & Zhang, 2010; Kawai, Kojima, Hirate, & Yasuoka, 2004;
Raimbault, Lavandier, & Bérengier, 2003) were used to develop a five-
point Likert scale for eight adjective attributes (pleasant, unpleasant,
eventful, uneventful, exciting, monotonous, calm, and chaotic).
Finally, in the cognition section, the participants’ overall percep-
tions of urban parks were defined as perceived loudness, perceived
crowd density, and comfort, and evaluated on a five-point Likert scale.
The various direct outcomes and indirect outcomes presented for the
soundscape preference assessment by Brown et al. (2011) and the de-
scriptors that were used to examine the preferences for commercial
space behavior by Meng et al. (2017) were used.
