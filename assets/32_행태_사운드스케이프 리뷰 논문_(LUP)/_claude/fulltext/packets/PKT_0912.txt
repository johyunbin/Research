Urban environment (e.g. greenspaces, air pollution and trafﬁc noise) and individuals' behaviours (e.g. physical activ-
ity) have all been associated with mental wellbeing. The large majority of studies on the inﬂuence of nature exposure
on mental wellbeing assumed that multiple pathways act independently, ignoring the interactions among potential
correlated pathways that engage simultaneously. The parallel mediation approach fails to explore the complex associ-
ations of combined exposure to air pollution, trafﬁc noise and nature exposure with physical activity, which in turn
affect mental wellbeing. Hence, the interest of understanding the sophisticated interactions among different pathways
is warranted. We utilized structural equation modelling to simultaneously evaluate whether actual and perceived
trafﬁc-related pollution and physical activity mediate the associations between mental wellbeing and nature exposure,
which was assessed by Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), green view index (GVI), green space density
and park accessibility. In summer 2022, we conducted questionnaires from 1772 adults residing in 117
neighbourhoods in Shenzhen, China. Nature exposure was positively and directly associated with mental wellbeing
in the single mediator model that considered physical activity only. The indirect effects of nature exposure on mental
wellbeing were observed through all pathways in all models, except through the perceived acoustic quality pathway in
the serial mediation model. In addition, the percentage mediated by perceived air quality was higher than that of per-
ceived acoustic quality. The inﬂuence of nature exposure on mental wellbeing was only for a small proportion medi-
ated by the physical activity pathway. The associations between nature exposure and mental wellbeing were modiﬁed
by individual characteristics, such as gender, age, income level and alcohol usage, but not employment status and
smoking behaviour. These ﬁndings point out the importance of both objective and subjective environmental features
and human behaviours on mental wellbeing, as well as the necessity of considering multiple pathways simultaneously.

1. Introduction
1.1. Mental health beneﬁts of nature exposure: What are the pathways
Green spaces, which provide a multitude of physical and mental beneﬁts
to the urban population (Diener and Mudu, 2021; Hartig et al., 2014), are in-
valuable natural resources. Natural outdoor environments offer solutions to
the adverse inﬂuence of rapid urbanization on human health and wellbeing.
Contacting with nature (e.g. urban parks, forests and street greenery) exerts
various beneﬁcial inﬂuence on mental health, including stress recovery
(Kajosaari and Pasanen, 2021; Markevych et al., 2017), attention restoration
(Bijnens et al., 2022), depression prevention (Liu et al., 2019b) and mood en-
hancement (Tyrväinen et al., 2014), as well as alleviating mental fatigue
(Jiang et al., 2020). Pathways through which nature exposure relates to men-
tal health include reducing harm (i.e. mitigating trafﬁc-related pollution), re-
storing capacities (i.e. physiological stress recovery) and building capacities
(i.e. promoting physical activity and facilitating social cohesion) (Hartig
et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2019a; Markevych et al., 2017; Roberts et al., 2021).
Trafﬁc-related noise and air pollution are two crucial environmental
risk factors which are detrimental to mental health (Bloemsma et al.,
2022; Dzhambov et al., 2017; Klompmaker et al., 2019; Wang et al.,
2020) and were suggested to be considered together on health issues
(Tezel et al., 2019). Mounting evidence indicates that urban greenness
can not only greatly improve air quality by inhibiting the dispersion of pol-
lutants (Diener and Mudu, 2021), but also mitigate noise pollution by
damping trafﬁc and vehicle noise (Oquendo-Di Cosola et al., 2022). A vari-
ety of cross-sectional studies have demonstrated that more residential expo-
sure to pollution may present barriers to physical activity and discourage
social interactions among neighbours in the outdoor settings (Dzhambov
et al., 2017). Therefore, less nature exposure and greater exposure to air
and noise pollution may exert negative inﬂuence on the mental health of
urban populations (Wang et al., 2020). The impacts of air pollution and
trafﬁc noise on psychological wellbeing have been distinguished, the re-
sults of which are ambiguous (Hao et al., 2022; Klompmaker et al., 2019;

June 2022. With a population of 17.68 million, Shenzhen is a modern inter-
national mega city in South China covering a land of 1997.47 km2. Data

which were randomly selected from four inner-city districts (including
Baoan, Nanshan, Futian and Luohu) utilizing a multi-stage stratiﬁed proba-

The survey yielded a total of 1772 valid participants and a response rate
of 98 %. The study protocol was approved by the Shenzhen University Re-
search Ethics Committee.
2.2. Mental wellbeing assessment
Self-reported mental wellbeing outcomes were measured by happiness,
the 5-item World Health Organization Wellbeing Index (WHO-5) and the
Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (SWEMWBS). These
three existing instruments have been validated in previous nature-health
research (Davison et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Hap-
piness was assessed with a single item (Fordyce, 1988) by asking “In gen-
eral, how happy would you say you are on a scale from 1 (extremely
unhappy) to 10 (extremely happy)?” The WHO-5 is one of the most sensi-
tive and valid instruments to evaluate respondents' subjective wellbeing
over the previous two weeks (Topp et al., 2015). Each item (including “I
have felt cheerful and in good spirits”, “I have felt calm and relaxed”, “I
have felt active and vigorous”, “I woke up feeling fresh and rested” and
“My daily life has been ﬁlled with things that interest me”) is scored on a
6-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (always). The total score
ranges from 0 to 25, with higher values representing better mental
wellbeing. The SWEMWBS is a 7-item scale evaluating respondents' posi-
tive mental wellbeing over the previous two weeks. These items contain
both hedonic (e.g. pleasure and satisfaction) and eudemonic (e.g. meaning-
fulness and cultivation of personal strengths) elements of mental health
(Melin et al., 2022). The SWEMWBS questions use positive wording, in-
cluding feeling optimistic about the future, useful, relaxed, able to deal
with problems, think clearly, close to others, and make up ones' mind
about things. Each item is scored 1 (none of the time) through 5 (all of
the time). Summing item scores leads to an initial raw score, which was
then transformed to a ﬁnal metric score to improve scaling properties
(Stewart-Brown et al., 2009). The ﬁnal score ranges from 7 to 35, with
lower values indicating poorer psychological wellbeing. The WHO-5 and
SWEMWBS both indicated excellent internal consistency, with the
Cronbach's α coefﬁcients of 0.948 and 0.915, respectively.
2.3. Nature exposure assessment
2.3.1. NDVI, green space density and park accessibility
Following previous studies, we employed Normalized Difference Vege-
tation Index (NDVI), green space density, park accessibility and green view
index (GVI) to assess residents' exposure to nature (Wang et al., 2020;
Zhang et al., 2022). The top-down greenness was quantiﬁed by NDVI, a
classic satellite-based measurement of vegetation density. The index was
extracted from Landsat 8 OLI (Operational Land Imager) and TIRS (Ther-
mal Infrared Sensor) data at 30 m spatial resolution via the United States
Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer website (https://earthexplorer.
usgs.gov). The all cloud-free images during the period from 3rd to 13th Sep-
tember in 2022 were acquired. The NDVI was calculated as the following
equation: NDVI = (NIR −RED) / (NIR + RED), where NIR and RED are
the near-infrared and red visible radiation, respectively. Pixels with nega-
tive NDVI value were excluded as recommended elsewhere (Rugel et al.,
2017). The range of NDVI was between 0 and 1, with higher values repre-
senting denser vegetation. Residential exposure to top-down greenness was
measured by the mean NDVI value within 1 km buffer around the geo-
graphic centroid of each neighbourhood. The green space density was
quantiﬁed by the proportion of green environmental exposure within the
buffers. Park accessibility was deﬁned as the network distance from each
neighbourhood to the nearest park. The QGIS network analysis toolbox
(QNEAT3) was applied to calculated the shortest network distance, with
higher values indicating poorer park accessibility.
2.3.2. Green view index
The eye-level greenness was assessed by GVI utilizing street view imag-
ery data. The sample sites for collecting street view images (SVI) were set
up at 50 m intervals along the road network (excluding motorways, high-
ways and tunnels) in Shenzhen which were obtained from OpenStreetMap
(Haklay and Weber, 2008). The GVI was calculated based on the SVI from
Baidu (Zhang et al., 2022), which were collected in August 2021. All pic-
tures were taken between March and September in 2019–2021. The coordi-
nates of the sample sites were utilized to generate the IDs of the respective
SVI, which were then entered into a Python script to download the street
view panoramas from Baidu API (Application Programming Interface).

semantic segmentation networks, including U-Net, SegNet and PSPNet
(Xia et al., 2021). Our training model was based on the Cityscapes dataset
