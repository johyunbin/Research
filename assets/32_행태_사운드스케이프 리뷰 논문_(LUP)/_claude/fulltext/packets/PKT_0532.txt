Many studies have explored the eﬀects of auditory and visual stimuli on the perception of
an environment. However, there is a lack of investigations examining direct behavioral
responses to noise in speciﬁc environments. In this study, a behavioral variable, walking
speed, was analyzed, as a response to the sounds and visual features of a speciﬁc
environment. The study examined the eﬀects of birdsongs compared to traﬃc noise on
walking speed in a real outdoor urban environment. It was supposed that the interaction of
audition and vision in the perception of an environment may also be shaped by the
perceived congruence of the visual and auditory features of the environment. The
participants (N = 87 and N = 65), young university students, walked along a 1.8-km urban
route. They listened to a soundtrack of crowded city noise or birdsongs, or they walked in
the real outdoor environment without listening to any acoustic stimuli. To investigate the
eﬀect of the congruence between acoustic and visual stimuli, the experiment was
conducted in two diﬀerent seasons (fall and spring). The results did not show signiﬁcant
diﬀerences between the crowded city noise condition and the real outdoor condition.
Listening to the soundtrack with birdsongs decreased walking speed, but this eﬀect was
signiﬁcant only in the experiment conducted in spring. These ﬁndings can be explained in
terms of the congruence between the sounds and the visual environment. The ﬁndings
raise questions regarding the restorative function of urban greenery during diﬀerent
seasons.

17

18

19

20

21

INTRODUCTION
37
38
Several decades ago, some cultural anthropologists created the concept of pace of life. This 
39
concept proposes that daily life follows a certain rhythm. The pace of life has been studied in 
40
large cities across geographic locations, operationalized as the temporal aspect of various daily 
41
behaviors and activities. Early research examined walking speed, the speed with which postal 
42
clerks completed a simple request, time punctuality, etc., in downtown areas of large cities 
43
(Werner, Altman, and Oxley, 1985). More recent studies searched, for instance, for a rate of 
44
tweets on the social media platform Twitter as a function of population density (Gross, Murthy, 
45
and Varshney, 2017). A fast pace of life may be a response to stimulatory overload and various 
46
urban stressors, including crowding and traffic noise (e.g., Bornstein & Bornstein, 1976). There 
47
is evidence that stressful sounds increase arousal, which activates the internal clock. They may 
48
also result in the acceleration of walking speed (Ozel, Larue, and Dosseville, 2004).
49
50
Urban pedestrian walking speed was investigated mainly in the 1970s and the 1980s 
51
(Bornstein and Bornstein, 1976; Kirkcaldy, Furnham, Levine, 2001; Levine and Bartlett, 1984; 
52
Lowin et al., 1971; Walmsley and Lewis,1989). Importantly, the results of more recent research 
53
suggest that the walking speed in large cities may gradually increase (Wiseman, 2007). Previous 
54
investigations also documented the negative health consequences of a fast pace of life, including 
55
walking speed. It was shown that a fast pace of life is associated with a high likelihood of heart 
56
attacks (e.g., Evans, 1984; Levine and Bartlett, 1984). A fast movement speed and speed of other

METHODS
282
283
Stimulus material
284
285
There were three conditions. The participants listened to a soundtrack with relaxation 
286
nature sounds or a track with traffic noise; they did not listen to any soundtrack under the real 
287
outdoor noise condition. The soundtrack from the video “Forest Birdsong - Relaxing Nature 
288
Sounds - Birds Chirping”, available on YouTube 
289
(https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Qm846KdZN_c), was selected as the nature sound. The 
290
track consists of the sound of birds singing. The track lasted approximately 2 hours. The 
291
soundtrack from the video “Hectic Kolkata (Calcutta) – India”, available on YouTube 
292
(https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IFc2KhKLiho), was selected as the crowded city noise. The 
293
track contains noise from motorized vehicles, engine sounds, automobile horns, and human 
294
voices. The track was modified using the software Audacity because of its short length to have a 
295
length of 38 minutes and 43 seconds, corresponding to the length of the participants’ walk. The 
296
sound levels of the tracks were adjusted to a comfortable level and did not differ among 
297
participants. The mean sound pressure levels were as follows: forest birdsong - 56 dB(A) (a 
298
relatively high magnitude of the mean sound pressure level reflects higher differences between 
299
thesound levels of a silent background and the birdsongs), and crowded city noise - 53 dB(A). 
300
The participants listened to the tracks, which were played on a cell phone.
301
302
The sounds were listened to through lightweight headphones Genius HS-M200C. For 
303
safety reasons, headphones did not entirely mask sounds from the outside. Participants assigned 
304
to the real outdoor noise condition did not wear headphones while walking. 
305
306
Walking route
307
308
The experiment was conducted in Hradec Králové in the Czech Republic. This city is 
309
located in the northeastern part of the Czech Republic and has approximately 100,000 
310
inhabitants. The walking route was a circuit with a length of approximately 1.8 km (see Figure 
311
1). The route was located in areas with minimal movement of other pedestrians; thus, walking 
312
speed was not affected by the flow of other pedestrians. Eight sections were chosen for the 
313
analysis of walking speed. The sections were chosen to provide a direct route and avoid 
314
crossroads or other obstacles. The lengths of the sections ranged from 45 to 100 meters. The first

Measurement of walking speed
328
329
The participants walked with a small video camera (i.e., a Sony Bloggie MHS-PM5K) on 
330
a belt around their waist (size 19 x 108 x 55 mm, weight 110 g). The environment, the 
331
participant’s feet, and the participant’s arms were captured through a fish eye lens. Beginning 
332
and end of each section of the route was indicated by a line drawn on the sidewalk. An evaluator 
333
marked two frames of the video recording to create the beginning and end of the annotation for 
334
each particular track section in the software Elan (see https://tla.mpi.nl/tools/tla-tools/elan/). 
335
Every annotation represented the entire section of the track, so that the extent of time subjects 
336
spent there could be determined. This enabled to calculate the average speed reached by the 
337
participants in all sections.

339
 Evaluation of walk experience
340
341
The participants rated their experience during their walk and their enjoyment of the 
342
environment using the following four items: (1) I was fine during the walk, (2) It was a pleasant 
343
time, (3) I liked the route I went through, and (4) The sounds I listened to from my headphones 
344
bothered me. They were required to rate agreement or disagreement with these items using a 7-
345
point Likert-type scale with anchors 1 = absolutely disagree and 7 = absolutely agree.
