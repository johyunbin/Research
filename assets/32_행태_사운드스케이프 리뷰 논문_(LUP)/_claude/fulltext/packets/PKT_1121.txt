ABSTRACT
In this study, five outdoor open areas were selected as the study sites to investigate the effects 
of thermal and acoustic environments, physical activity levels, and pleasantness on evaluations 
of the thermal and acoustic comfort in outdoor open spaces. Survey questionnaires and on-site 
measurements were used to quantitatively assess human thermal and acoustic comfort in 
outdoor spaces. The following findings were obtained in this study. The acoustic environment 
affected the thermal sensation, where reducing excessive outdoor noise levels could enhance 
thermal comfort. Increasing the physiological equivalent temperature amplified the acoustic 
sensation. Individuals reported the most comfortable acoustic perception outdoors at 
a physiological equivalent temperature of 30.1°C. Reducing both the thermal and acoustic 
indices improved the overall comfort to some extent. Decreasing physical activity levels by an 
appropriate amount enhanced the thermal comfort, acoustic comfort, and overall comfort. 
Finally, maintaining a sense of pleasantness was conducive to increasing the thermal comfort, 
acoustic comfort, and overall comfort. In states of moderate and pleasurable feelings (pleasant­
ness level = 0, 1, 2, 3), increased acoustic comfort and pleasantness combined significantly 
enhanced the thermal comfort.

1. Introduction
People often experience discomfort under various 
environmental conditions due to the constantly chan­
ging climate and local temperature, humidity, air con­
vection, and other factors that affect urban surfaces, as 
well as the urban heat island effect. Studies of these 
issues have generally focused on the thermal environ­
ment (Lai et al. 2014), acoustic environment (Zhang and 
Kang 2007), lighting environment (Shi et al. 2022), air 
quality (Lin et al. 2022), and interactions between multi­
ple environmental factors (Astolfi, Corgnati, and Verso 
2003; Du et al. 2023; Ricciardi and Buratti 2018; Wu et al. 
2020). These studies encompassed a wide range of sites 
and subjects, including indoor and outdoor spaces, such 
as offices (Bourikas et al. 2021), hospitals (Abd Rahman 
et al. 2022), libraries (Tang et al. 2023), sports stadiums 
(Huang, Ma, and Zhang 2019), and urban squares (Ren 
2023). Campus open spaces (Khalili, Fayaz, and 
Zolfaghari 2022) have a diverse array of spatial typolo­
gies and they are important research sites for investigat­
ing individuals from various age groups, educational 
backgrounds, and occupations, including students, tea­
chers, administrative personnel, and certain family 
members (Kang and Zhang 2010). Climate laboratories, 
questionnaires, and field tests are commonly employed 
as methods in this type of research. Climate laboratories 
allow active control and restriction of the research con­
ditions by eliminating most interference (Zhang et al. 
2018). Combining questionnaire surveys with field tests 
allows basic information and subjective experiences to 
be collected from respondents’ (Wu et al. 2023), as well 
as providing real-time measurements of the physical 
environment at the survey location (Mohammadzadeh, 
Karimi, and Brown 2023). However, a single environ­
ment is not sufficient to meet the needs of some 
research, and sites where multiple environmental fac­
tors interact are more similar to real-world scenarios 
(Astolfi, Corgnati, and Verso 2003; Du et al. 2023; 
Ricciardi and Buratti 2018; Wu et al. 2020).
In a study of the thermal environment, Chen et al. 
found that the thermal comfort was influenced by the 
climate and regional diversity, and the thermal sensa­
tion was mainly related to the air temperature. In 
Harbin, China, the acceptable range for the physiolo­
gical equivalent temperature (PET) was determined as 
2.5–30.9°C, and the neutral temperatures for PET in 
spring, summer, autumn, and winter were obtained 
as 18.7°C, 20.0°C, 21.9°C, and 18.0°C, respectively 
(Chen et al. 2018). A study of the outdoor thermal 
comfort in two ethnic groups with different thermal 
perception and adaptation backgrounds found cross-

--- METHODS/RESULTS EXCERPT ---
Methods
2.1. Study area
The study was conducted in Xi’an City, which has 
a warm temperate semi-humid continental monsoon 
climate. The winter is cold and the spring is warm and 
dry, with a hot and rainy summer, and a cool autumn. 
In the study area, the average annual temperature 
ranges from 13.1°C to 14.3°C, with average tempera­
tures of −1.2°C to 0.5°C in January and 26.5°C to 27.0°C 
in July. The extreme lowest and highest annual tem­
peratures are −21.2°C and 43.4°C, respectively. The 
annual precipitation ranges from 528.3 mm to 716.5 
mm. The annual sunshine hours range from 1595.6 h to 
2035.8 h. The prevailing wind in the Xi’an urban area is 
the northeast wind throughout the year.
The field measurements and other investigations in 
this study were conducted at Xingqing Campus, Xi’an 
Jiaotong University, Xi’an, Shaanxi Province (108.9902° 
E, 34.2524°N). The campus is 1538 m wide and 1084 m 
long, with diverse landscapes and acoustic environ­
ments that are suitable for studying thermal – acoustic 
interactions. Initially, a preliminary investigation was 
conducted in the campus environment by considering 
different environmental types and seasonal features. 
As shown in Figure 1, we then selected five outdoor 
spaces as the study sites comprising Cherry Blossom 
East Road (CR), Phoenix Tree East Road (PR), Four Great 
Inventions Square (IS), beside the Pool at Tengfei 
Tower (TP), and East Garden Lawn (GL).
2.2. Experimental design
2.2.1. Meteorological measurements
The survey was conducted during the spring in May 
and June, 2022. The investigators collected physical 
environment measurements in five survey areas, and 
recorded meteorological parameters at a height of 
1.2 m from the ground. Meteorological parameters 
such as Ta, RH, Va, G, and Tg were measured 
every minute, and the acoustic levels were measured 
simultaneously. Table 1 shows the abbreviation.

2.2.2. Questionnaire survey
Two investigators conducted a random survey of 
pedestrians at the five study locations, where the 
meteorological data and acoustic levels were simul­
taneously measured, and relevant records were 
made. The research questions were divided into 
three parts. The first part collected general informa­
tion about the respondents, including their demo­
graphic characteristics (gender, age, ethnicity, place

2.3. Thermal and acoustic indices
Several heat indices have been formulated and the 
most commonly used validated indices include PET, 
predicted mean vote, UTCI, and standard effective 
temperature (Yin et al. 2022). PET is utilized as a tool 
for assessing thermal comfort, and it allows compar­
isons and evaluations of thermal comfort in various 
environments. In recent years, PET has been applied 
increasingly widely in research (Chen et al. 2018; 
Mohammadzadeh,
