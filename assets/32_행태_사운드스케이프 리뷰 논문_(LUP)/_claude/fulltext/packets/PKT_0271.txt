Abstract: The nature of the problems in cities are increasingly interdisciplinary, as are the solutions and the research 
needed to address them. Understanding the intersection between modern urban life and its implications for the built form 
requires research on many aspects of contemporary human experience in cities. Urban squares are a central component 
of the urban fabric. They provide respite from the intensity of the built form, public spaces for recreation, entertainment 
and trade. However, the efficacy of their design and the inherent experience available to the public is seldom evaluated. 
The aim of this study was to begin developing a collection of cross-disciplinary assessment tools that could be used to 
evaluate urban squares and their social value. In 2012, a pilot study was completed exploring the major influences on the 
social success of Oxford Square, Sydney, Australia. A mixed methods approach was used which included three methods: 
Behaviour Mapping, Physical Mapping, and a Soundscape Analysis assessment. The instruments and methods used 
revealed that the physical and sensory characteristics of the Square substantially influence the social use of the Square. 
Keywords: Urban Squares, Social Places, Place-Making 
Introduction 
he interest in urban squares as social places is part of a post-modern planning approach.
Place-based planning and the practice of place-making are key postmodern frameworks
for the disciplines of planning and built environment design. No longer is planning done 
at a meta-level, but rather it is done at the local or place level. In support of this recent approach, 
the practice of place-making, as noted by scholars and practitioners such as Landry (2008), 
Sandercock (2003), Cuthbert (2011) is both an art form and a professional science. Both concepts 
have become common in planning and design discourse and praxis. However, the social success 
of urban planning and design is increasingly of interest to social sciences. In particular, 
environment-behaviour research is fundamentally interested in the relationship between people 
and place (Gifford 2014), recognising that the social success of any place is much more complex 
than providing well designed physical form. 
Place-making policies, programs and projects (not to mention consultancies) abound and are 
attempting to design, plan for and retrofit cities to be animated, vibrant and socioeconomically 
viable, people-based activity centres. Convivial public places like urban squares are used to 
reflect a community’s identity, ambitions and values and have been shown to support greater 
physical activity, social interaction and social connection amongst community members (Frank, 
Andresen and Schmid 2004; Frank and Engelke 2001; Giles-Corti 2006a, 2006b). Improving the 
quality of community places, resources and opportunities for community life are topics of 
increasing interest and focus in the developed world, by all levels of government. This is in 
response to growing concerns over community health issues such as increasing obesity and the 
rise in mental health problems; youth disenfranchisement; the ageing population and the need for 
equitable, sustainable communities. Supporting the development of socially inclusive, physically 
supportive environments, both built and natural, is essential for the success and health of any 
community. Increasingly, individual and community well-being and health are understood as 
being intricately linked with the quality of the places in their lives (Kent, Thompson and 
Jalaludin 2011).

--- METHODS/RESULTS EXCERPT ---
Methodology and Methods 
In response to the literature discussed and the contextual challenges surrounding Oxford 
Square, the focus of this pilot study was on identifying correlations between the characteristics of 
Oxford Square and the social patterns of use and behaviour that occur in the Square. The 
overarching research question included: How do the physical and sensory characteristics of 
urban squares influence the use and behaviour in an urban square? What relationships can be 
identified? 
A mixed methods approach was appropriate for this research because of the interdisciplinary 
nature of the research question (Tashakkori and Teddlie 2003). The benefit of this 
methodological approach was that multiple sources and types of data can be used to describe, 
define and delineate the subject in focus, drawing on a number of disciplinary perspectives. 
These sources of data can then be analysed individually for their particular contribution to the 
research as well as triangulated (Lincoln and Guba 1985) with all other data to support a more 
extensive, theoretical discussion. In this study qualitative methods are employed to establish a 
rich description of the research site across time. The quantitative methods are employed to 
establish patterns of use and behaviour and the key characteristics influencing these. In order to 
answer the overarching research question for the project it was necessary to understand: 
1.
The physical and sensory characteristics and environments of the square
2.
The social use of the Square and the behaviour within the square
The characteristics of the Square and its use patterns were measured using a number of 
place-based research tools designed to explore the physical, sensory and social features of 
a ‘place’. These were developed from the literature (Cooper- Marcus1998; Gehl 2010; Whyte 
1980; Zeisel 2006). Three methods were used which provided the basis of the findings and 
the discussion of the research questions: 
1.
Behaviour Mapping – to establish patterns and volumes of pedestrian traffic and
behaviours on site. (Zeisel 2006)
2.
Physical Mapping – to assess the physical characteristics, activities and amenities
on-site against Whyte’s (1980) criteria for a ‘successful public space.’
3.
Soundscape Analysis – to record and evaluate the volume and intensity of noise and
overall quality of the sonic environment of the site. (Schafer 1977)
Findings 
The Physical Characteristics and Function of the Square 
The physical mapping tool assessed the topography and natural elements of the Square, its 
context, its built form, its amenities and sensory elements. The Square provides a break in the 
line of shop windows and walls that are built to the property line on the south side of the 
street. The Square illustrates the difficulty of making a sunken plaza an active one. This is 
especially the case in the face of the sensory conditions that this square experiences. It is a 
cold, dark square that often smells unpleasant and is surrounded by high levels of noise 
most of the time. These conditions are not conducive for encouraging people to stay in the 
Square for long periods. Key findings included: 

The Square serves primarily as a link between the three streets with a slow but
steady flow of pedestrians passing through it for much of the day.

General Analysis 

The Square was not designed as a typical urban square. It is a closed-off junction in
the road. It does act as a pedestrian link between three streets; the steps are not easy
to negotiate but they do follow the desire lines of pedestrian travel.

The Square is in shadow too much of the day to be an attractive spot even on hot
summer days.

Although a café is located in the square, it does not attract a large clientele at any
time other than lunch time so it does not activate the square to any significant
degree.

The windows in the buildings surrounding the square do afford opportunities for
natural surveillance but these spaces are not heavily occupied during the day.

Although the rims of the planter boxes afford places to sit, they are seldom sat on.
In contrast a single place on the edge of a planter box on Oxford Street is quite
frequently used; it provides a better view. It is also a sunnier spot than the edges of
the planter boxes in the Square itself.

Although the people walking through the Square provide some potential interest to
others sitting in it, there is not really much to engage the user in the Square.

The paving is greatly stained with the residue of chewing gum – an unobtrusive
indicator of the people who frequent the night club. These concrete pavers are not
easy to clean.

The area smells bad a lot of the time due to the lack of nearby public toilets.
Soundscape Analysis 
The soundscape of the Square is the most significant sensory consideration which impacts its use. 
The tool used physical measures (acoustic recording) and perceptive measures (descriptive, 
experiential and psychoacoustic). Both the physical and perceptive measures found that sound 
levels at the site are high. The site contains a range of man-made, natural and mechanical sounds.
