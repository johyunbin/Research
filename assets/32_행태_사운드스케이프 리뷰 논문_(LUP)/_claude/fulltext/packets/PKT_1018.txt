A B S T R A C T 
Urban parks are vital green spaces where people can participate in various activities. A favorable acoustic-visual 
environment is crucial for satisfaction, and acoustic-visual perception may vary depending on the behavioral 
state. This study aimed to examine the acoustic-visual perception of people undertaking different behaviors 
(sitting, walking, and acoustic) and explore the relationship between acoustic and visual perceptions and 
satisfaction. The study was conducted using questionnaire surveys (N = 1044) in three urban parks in Quanzhou, 
China. The results showed that (1) respondents undertaking different behaviors had different acoustic-visual 
perceptions, (2) satisfaction was influenced by acoustic-visual perception, (3) overall satisfaction was influ­
enced by both acoustic and visual satisfaction, and (4) acoustic, visual, and overall satisfaction could be pre­
dicted via multiple linear regression. Based on the results and design strategies of this study, a more comfortable 
activity environment for people in urban parks is proposed. 
1. Introduction 
With the rapid growth of cities, the density of buildings and popu­
lation in built-up areas is increasing (Liu et al., 2020; Ning et al., 2022) 
while the amount of vegetated area is declining (Lin and Wu, 2019; Yu 
et al., 2022). China, the world’s largest developing country, has un­
dergone rapid urbanization over the past 20 years (Wei et al., 2022), and 
the importance of green spaces is attracting considerable attention in the 
country (Lee and Maheswaran, 2011). Urban parks offer residents the 
opportunity to connect with nature while promoting physical activity 
(Feng et al., 2021; Marquet et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 
2023), and the environmental conditions of urban parks influence the 
perception (Wu and Chen, 2023) and feelings (Chiesura, 2004) of resi­
dents, particularly the acoustic (Gozalo et al., 2018) and visual (Xiang 
et al., 2022) environmental conditions, for which changes may influence 
the overall environmental perception (Ta et al., 2021). 
People’s perceptions of a given context are shaped by the informa­
tion received through their visual, auditory, and olfactory senses (Stein, 
2012). Studies investigating the perception of acoustic and visual en­
vironments have been performed from multiple perspectives. In terms of 
the acoustic environment, the International Standard Organization has 
defined the soundscape as “the acoustic environment as perceived or 
experienced and/or understood by a person or people, in context” 
(Schulte-Fortkamp et al., 2023). Currently, the subjective perception of 
an acoustic environment includes annoyance (Nang Li et al., 2012), 
sound characteristic descriptions (Nitidara et al., 2022), personal noise 
sensitivity (Van Renterghem and Botteldooren, 2016), and personal 
sound source preferences (Yong Jeon et al., 2011). To intuitively 
determine the influence of the acoustic environment, one study 
continuously measured the sound pressure level for an acoustic comfort 
survey (Yang and Kang, 2005), while to better describe the perceived 
acoustic environment, other studies have investigated the type of sound 
(Kang et al., 2017), acoustic indicators (Yang and Kang, 2005) or 
perceived affective qualities (Xiang et al., 2022). Liu et al. (2018) con­
ducted field surveys in Fuzhou and found that the type of sound in­
fluences the tranquility of a soundscape, while Hasegawa et al. (2022) 
evaluated the perceived affective quality (PAQ) of soundscapes based on 
guidance provided by the soundscape indicators in (ISO 12913-2: 2018). 
Although PAQ has been a standard for many years, relatively few studies 
have applied this scale to the evaluation of soundscapes. In addition, 
sound-related activities generate diverse sound-level environments, 
leading to different levels of acoustic comfort for respondents (Meng and 
Kang, 2016). Hence, it is imperative that perception is classified into 
different activity groups. The development of soundscape research has

Methods 
2.1. Study design 
The proposed framework for assessing acoustic-visual perception 
and its relationship with satisfaction based on different behaviors in 
urban parks is illustrated in Fig. 1. The research framework was based on 
the conceptual framework of the soundscape (ISO 12913-1:2014)and 
consisted of five parts focused on three research questions based on 
different behavioral states. Respondents were categorized into behav­
ioral types based on their behavioral states in the first part, 
acoustic-visual perception was evaluated in the second part, and the 
relationship between acoustic and visual perception and satisfaction was 
investigated in the third part. The relationships between acoustic satis­
faction (AS), visual satisfaction (VS), and overall satisfaction (OS) were 
also explored. The construction of prediction models for AS, VS, and OS 
based on acoustic-visual perception was performed in the fourth part. 
Finally, variations in perceptions associated with different behaviors 
were discussed in the fifth part.

2.3. Questionnaire design 
The questionnaire consisted of four sections, with the first part used 
to collect personal information, including gender (male, female), age 
(≤18, 19–35, 36–50, 51–65, and 65 + ), and behavioral states (sitting, 
walking, and acoustic behavior). In the second part, eight indicators 
from previous studies (Grahn and Stigsdotter, 2010; Kytt¨a and Kahila, 
2005; Stigsdotter and Corazon, 2017; Appleton, 1996): ‘nature,’ ‘beauty,’ 
‘absorb,’ ‘open,’ ‘coordinate, ‘gradation,’ ‘diversity,’ and ‘order’ were 
selected to evaluate the perceived visual quality (PVQ). International 
Standards (ISO, 2018) were used to evaluate the soundscape in the third 
section. This evaluation encompasses two aspects: sound source identi­
fication (SSI) and PAQ for the surrounding acoustic environment, with 
sound sources including cars, buses, trains, and airplanes included as 
traffic noise; live, broadcast, and cellphone music as music; conversa­
tion, laughter, and footsteps as human sounds; and singing birds, flow­
ing water, and wind as natural sounds. The PAQ included ‘pleasant,’ 
‘chaotic,’ ‘vibrant,’ ‘uneventful,’ ‘calm,’ ‘annoying,’ ‘eventful,’ and ‘monot­
onous.’ The fourth section addressed participant satisfaction with the 
acoustic, visual, and overall environments (Jeon and Jo, 2020). All 
questions were based on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5. 
2.4. Data collection 
Sound pressure measurements were taken using a BSWA 308 sound 
level meter used to record LAeq. The microphone was pointed approx­
imately 1 m away from any reflective surfaces and 1.2–1.5 m above the 
ground to reduce the effects of acoustic reflection (Meng and Kang, 
2016). Measurements were continued for 10 min at each study site 
following the methodology of previous studies (Axelsson et al., 2014; Liu 
et al., 2014; Jabben et al., 2015; Lobo Soares and Bento Coelho, 2016). 
Fig. 2 shows the locations and LAeq values obtained at each sampling 
point. 
Behavioral data were collected concurrently with sound levels. Based 
on the specific activities and number of people in the park, the activities 
were divided into three distinct behavior groups: sitting, walking, and 
acoustic (Table 2). The behavior of the respondents in each space was 
recorded via both questioning and observation. Only tourists who 
engaged in one of the studied behaviors for over 15 min were included. 
Questionnaire collection, behavioral observations, and sound level 
measurements were conducted in autumn of 2022 at Citong Park 
(November 17, 18, 19, and 20), East Lake Park (November 26, 27, 28, 
29, and 30), and West Lake Park (December 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, and 10). Data 
collection spanned both weekdays and weekends from 8:00 am to 6:00 
pm and was performed under favorable weather conditions with no rain.
