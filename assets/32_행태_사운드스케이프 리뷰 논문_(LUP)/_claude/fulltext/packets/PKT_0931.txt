Natural sounds can encourage social interactions in urban parks 
Xiaochao Chen, Jian Kang * 
Institute for Environmental Design and Engineering, The Bartlett, University College London, London WC1H 0NN, United Kingdom 
H I G H L I G H T S 
• Natural sounds increase about 10% of people engaging in social interactions. 
• Natural sounds increase the frequency of group social interactions. 
• Natural sounds increase the total duration of social interactions by about 10%. 
• Natural sounds affect group interactions more than one-to-one interactions.

A B S T R A C T 
Urban parks play an essential role in hosting social activities and providing spaces for social interactions. Pre­
vious studies have examined the effects of the sound environment on individuals, particularly in terms of 
perception and behavior; however, few studies have investigated the impact of the sound environment on social 
interactions in urban parks. In this study, an experiment was conducted involving sound interventions and covert 
behavioral observations in a typical urban park activity space to determine whether different interventional 
sounds influence social interaction behaviors. In this experiment, four sound intervention groups and a control 
group were established and three variables describing the social interaction situation in the study area were 
defined: proportion of people engaging in social interaction, frequency of social interaction, and percentage of 
time spent on social interaction. These findings indicate that changes in the sound environment have a significant 
impact on social interactions. The natural sound intervention was found to increase the proportion of people 
engaged in social interactions and the frequency and time spent on social interactions. The positive effects of 
natural sounds primarily promoted social interactions within groups. In addition, the noise interventions in this 
experiment had no significant negative effects. These findings suggest that protecting and augmenting positive 
sounds are sometimes more important than noise control and that design strategies that focus on the sound 
environment could facilitate social interactions in urban park spaces and long-term social cohesion. 
1. Introduction 
Urban parks represent a key component of the green infrastructure of 
cities and are essential for ensuring the sustainability of urban envi­
ronments (Chiesura, 2004), particularly in terms of providing ecosystem 
services, mitigating the negative effects of climate change, and pro­
moting public health (Toth, 2003; Wolch, Byrne, & Newell, 2014). 
Numerous studies have found that urban parks provide residents with a 
wide range of benefits, which include contributing to physical and 
mental health (Han, Cohen, & McKenzie, 2013; Sturm & Cohen, 2014) 
and encouraging social interactions (Maas, van Dillen, Verheij, & 
Groenewegen, 2009). Among the characteristics that a well-designed 
urban park should possess, a high-quality sound environment is essen­
tial because environmental and mechanical noise can diminish the 
positive impacts of parks on citizens (Nilsson & Berglund, 2006). 
Previous studies have focused on assessing the quality of the sound 
environment in urban parks (Brambilla, Gallo, Asdrubali, & D’Alessan­
dro, 2013; Nilsson & Berglund, 2006) and the impact of different sounds 
on people (Axelsson, Nilsson, Hellstr¨om, & Lund´en, 2014; Fuda, Aletta, 
Kang, & Astolfi, 2015). In consideration of the relationship of physio­
logical, psychological, and social factors to the soundscape in urban 
open public spaces, Kang and Zhang (2010) identified four major 
soundscape factors: relaxation, communication, spatiality, and dy­
namics. Growing evidence has shown that the park visitor experience is

Methods of noise elimination that simply reduce sound levels do not 
always show improvements for quality of life (Aletta & Kang, 2019; 
Kang, 2017). Compared with such noise control approaches, the 
soundscape approach considers environmental sound as a resource and 
explores the positive effects of sound rather than just reducing the vol­
ume of unwanted sound (Kang et al., 2016; Kang & Schulte-Fortkamp, 
2016). The addition of pleasant sounds to a public space not only 
masks unwanted sounds but can also enhance the existing soundscape 
(Van Renterghem et al., 2020). A number of studies in urban parks and 
public spaces have examined the positive effects of interventional 
sounds on people’s perceptions (Van Renterghem et al., 2020; Yang & 
Kang, 2005), with several exploring how human behavior can be 
changed through soundscape interventions (Aletta et al., 2016; Lavia, 
Dixon, Witchel, & Goldsmith, 2016; Steele, Bild, Tarlao, & Guastavino, 
2019). Meanwhile, some studies have found that person-related vari­
ables such as age, gender, personality traits, and participation, or a lack 
of such, in social interactions can influence individuals’ evaluation of 
soundscapes (Steffens, Steele, & Guastavino, 2017; Tarlao, Steffens, & 
Guastavino, 2021). In consequence, a cycle of positive feedback is made 
possible, in that once individuals perceive changes in the sound envi­
ronment, this can interact with specific personal factors, which subse­
quently influences their evaluation of the overall soundscape. Although 
the potential connection between the acoustic environment and social 
interactions might be recognized, the existing literature has not 
adequately studied the impact of different sound sources on social in­
teractions in parks. 
Social interaction is a fundamental aspect of human behavior 
because humans are a highly social species with complex social char­
acteristics (Adolphs, 2003). There is substantial evidence that social 
interactions are essential to human well-being and are strongly corre­
lated with human health (Cohen, Gottlieb, & Underwood, 2001; 
Umberson, Crosnoe, & Reczek, 2010). In addition, interest in promoting 
social interactions is growing (Matsuoka & Kaplan, 2008; Peters et al., 
2010). Many sound-related studies have explored the effects of the 
sound environment on various aspects of human experience, but there 
has been little research on its impact on social behavior. Thus, the effect 
of acoustic environments on social interaction behaviors is a valid 
subject of investigation. Given the significance of urban parks as sites of 
social interaction, this study aimed to investigate whether sound in­
terventions alter the social behavior of individuals in urban parks. 
The following research questions were addressed by conducting on- 
site interventional soundscape experiments in urban parks. Do inter­
ventional sounds change a) the proportion of people engaging in social 
interactions, b) the frequency of social interactions between individuals, 
and c) the duration of social interactions between individuals? Specif­
ically, the interventional sounds deployed in the experiment consisted 
on the one hand, of environmental noise, and on the other hand, of 
natural sounds typical of urban parks. Accordingly, this study deter­
mined whether natural sounds facilitate people’s social interactions in 
urban parks and whether noise inhibits such interactions. 
2. Methods 
The sound intervention experiment was conducted in Tieren Park, 
Daqing, China. Using loudspeakers, the experiment altered the site’s 
acoustic environment by introducing interventional sounds. Remote 
behavioral observations were conducted by using hidden cameras to 
record the actual responses of park visitors to different sound conditions. 
The participants’ behavior was observed and coded based on these video 
recordings, and the resulting data were analyzed to determine whether 
the sound environment influences social interactions. 
2.1. Study area and participants 
The selection of our experimental site was primarily based on two 
considerations: the feasibility of conducting sound intervention experi­
ments and the representativeness of the location. In terms of feasibility, 
we took into account the size of the site and its acoustic environment. As 
for representativeness, we chose an urban park activity space that is 
surrounded by greenery and used by a diverse range of people. While it 
may not represent all park spaces, it is a common type of activity space 
in urban environments. As a result, a rectangular recreational area 
(approximately 30 m wide and 35 m long, with central coordinates of 
46◦36′42.1′’N, 124◦52′45.6′’E) in the southwest corner of Tieren Park 
was selected as the experimental site (Fig. 1). Given the diverse land uses 
surrounding the site, including residential, commercial, and office 
space, and based on preliminary observations during our site survey, we 
discovered that the users of the site represent a range of ages and 
different occupations. The participants in our study were adults, and all 
were treated equitably in the course of the experiment. Their social 
behaviors over a period of time reflected the possibilities for social 
interaction afforded by the site. To limit to a minimum the study’s 
interference with the participants’ behavior on the site, we did not 
directly interact with them; this, unfortunately, constrained our ability 
to gather detailed information on all participants. The site included a 
central soft play area for children and outdoor gym equipment and 
benches at the perimeter. According to a preliminary survey, the site had 
a stable acoustic environment.
