ABSTRACT
Despite the long-standing appeal for tourism researchers to pay more attention to the
eﬀects of soundscape, there remains a serious lack of in-depth studies on the topic.
Drawing on moderating eﬀects of tourists’ soundscape emotion, this study
examines certain determinants of tourists’ pro-environmental behaviour. Kulangsu
was used as a source of empirical data for its abundant soundscape resources. The
results revealed the explicit connections of human-environmental relationships to
pro-environmental behaviour. All the paths are more signiﬁcant when grouped with

with

demonstrates the importance of soundscape to promote sustainable development
in tourist destinations.

Introduction
A critical part of the tourist experience is associated
with sounds in their environment (Waitt & Duﬀy,
2010; Weinzimmer et al., 2014). For example, the sen-
sation of listening to the sounds at the Kulangsu—a
famous Chinese destination—whether they are the
piano tone, pop music, birds cackling, crowd noise, or
even the natural quiet may initially appear too
mundane. However, if the radio is switched oﬀ, the
acoustical environment changes, or if the tranquillity
is gone, a known and viscerally sensed displacement
occurs, and the destination may become less attractive
(Bernat, 2014). As an attractive tourist destination, the
outstanding universal value of Kulangsu is not just
limited to visual stimuli (Dann, 2002). In fact, the
“soundscape” which is deﬁned as the acoustic environ-
ment that can be perceived by humans (ISO, 2014) is at
least equally important (Son & Pearce, 2005). This gen-
erates several questions: “What is so visceral about
tourists’ perceptions of their auditory environment?”
Moreover, “what are the implications of the visceral
connection and disconnection in terms of tourists’
pro-environmental behaviour in certain destinations”?
Previous studies have shown that tourists’ PEB safe-
guards the lasting attractiveness and economic vitality
of a tourist destination (e.g. Harrington, Lane, & Mercer,
2006; Seabrook, McAlpine, & Fensham, 2008; Smith,
2008). More recently, research has shown that the
human ties to nature and place, which encompass con-
nectedness to nature (CTN) and place attachment
(TPA), are important drivers of environmental actions
(e.g. Kals, Schumacher, & Montada, 1999; Scannell &
Giﬀord, 2010; Vaske & Kobrin, 2001). Given that tourists’
visual experiences have been emphasized more recently
over other experiences in tourism studies (Urry, 2008),
these studies seem to assume that tourists’ perceptions
and behaviour largely rely on visual factors and, thus,
they failed to consider the importance of the sounds-
cape. As argued by Ulrich (1983), as well as Conniﬀand
Craig
(2016),
concentrating
exclusively
on
visual

complex

because

only
depicts a fragment of a given setting (Nordh, Hartig,
Hagerhall, & Fry, 2009). The soundscape, by contrast,
can induce some speciﬁc perceptions not experienced
throughvisualstimuli,helpingpeopleactmorerationally

Methodology
Study sites, participants, and data collection
Kulangsu is a tiny island located on the estuary of the
Chiu-lung River, facing the city of Xiamen. As a well-
known tourist destination in China, Kulangsu enjoys
the reputation of a great resort. On 9 July 2017, the
UNESCO added Kulangsu to its list of world heritage
sites in recognition of the site’s outstanding value to
humanity. It is a destination that typiﬁes a soundscape
environment. Its name is also believed to have audi-
tory origins. Ku means drumbeat, Lang means the
tide, and Su refers to the island. It is said that when
the tide hits the huge rocks on the southwest coast,
it sounds like the beating of several drums. Thus, the
rock is named the “drum wave stone” and the island
is also named after the rock as “Kulangsu.” Moreover,
as a unique place, which has witnessed multicultural
exchanges and integration, it contains a traditional
soundscape rich in southern Fujian culture (e.g. the
tranquillity of classical gardens), a typical soundscape
representing Western culture (e.g. piano music), the
unique soundscape of its white beaches and oddly
shaped rocks (e.g. the drumming when rocks are
lashed by waves), and the soundscape of tourists
(e.g.
tourists’
voices).
The
various
soundscapes
enamour tourists with the island (see Figure 2).
According to the administration of Kulangsu, it
attracted over 348 million tourists and generated
more than 10 million RMB in tourism revenue in
2015. However, UNESCO has shown great concern
for the sustainable development of Kulangsu, and
the organization has pointed out that tourism press-
ures present signiﬁcant threats to the integrity of the
island and, hence, require strict controls. Further, this
is a universal phenomenon at other tourist desti-
nations, especially in developing countries (Chubchu-
wong, Beise-Zee, & Speece, 2015). Therefore, Kulangsu
is appropriately suited to the exploration of the char-
acter of a soundscape in alleviating tourism-posed
environmental pressures by motivating a change in
tourists’ behaviour.
The target research population included all dom-
estic adult tourists who visited any of the most impor-
tant scenic areas in Kulangsu (e.g. Sunlight Rock, Piano
Museum, and Shu Zhuang Garden) between 13
January and 19 January 2016. This special period is
Kiangsu’s peak winter season and data collection
during this time ensures the inclusion of tourists
diverse in age, profession, province of origin, and
length of stay. Keeping the sample size requirements
of all the analyses in mind, trained research assistants
distributed 700 questionnaires on the island. The
research assistants made contact with domestic tour-
ists who were resting or relaxing and recruited them
for participation, selecting one in every 10 people.
The 10-person interval was designed to improve data
reliability by reducing the possibility of accumulating

Instrument development
The questionnaire consisted of ﬁve parts: participants’
demographics, PEB, connectedness to nature, place
attachment, and TSE. The items used to measure con-
nectedness to nature were from the studies by
Gosling and Williams (2010) and Nisbet, Zelenski, and
Murphy (2009). These two studies are very similar in
how they capture subjective connectedness with
nature. The scale from Gosling and Williams (2010) con-
tains seven items and has been well tested in empirical
studies. However, the study of Nisbet et al. (2009) adds
one more item to measure how participants’ actions
aﬀect the natural world. Therefore, we borrowed this
item as well (‘I have a deep understanding of how my
actions aﬀect the natural world’) from their study. A
scale with eight items was developed to measure the
extent to which tourists connect with nature.
The Place attachment dimensions were measured
by 11 items (six items for place dependence and ﬁve
items for place identity). To distinguish from the con-
struct of connectedness to nature, items that reﬂected
the inﬂuence of social bonding were intentionally
incorporated. Speciﬁcally, in addition to the three
items borrowed from Williams & Vaske (2003), the
measures of place dependence also included two
items of social bonding (“If I were to stop visiting
Kulangsu, I would lose contact with a number of
friends” and “My friends/family would be disap-
pointed if I were to start visiting other costal desti-
nations”) and one item of “everybody’s happy” (I rely
on Kulangsu to provide an enjoyable experience for
my family/group of friends), ﬁne-tuned, respectively,
from Ramkissoon et al. (2013) and Tonge, Ryan,
Moore, and Beckley (2015). The latter three items
were merged, as they best reﬂected place depen-
dence based on social relationships and/or reference
group norms. Similarly, the measures of place identity
borrowed two items of social bonding (“The friend-
ships and associations I have with other people here
at Kulangsu mean a lot to me” and “A feeling of com-
munity runs between me and other leisure tourists
here at Kulangsu”) from Tonge et al. (2015) to inte-
grate the identiﬁcation with place derived from inter-
personal interaction and resonance in addition to the
three items borrowed from Ramkissoon et al. (2013).

(2010). This scale explores how much value tourists
