Abstract
1. Natural soundscapes experienced in parks are increasingly recognised as a valu-
able ecosystem service, yet urban parks soundscapes also commonly contain 
anthropogenic noise.
2. While studies show bird calls can promote psychological restoration, the factors 
that might influence actual experiences of these sounds by individuals in urban 
parks are less clear. Equally, continual exposure to anthropogenic noise, such as 
vehicle traffic, is associated with negative health outcomes, but determinants 
influencing negative experiences of such sounds within a park soundscape are 
poorly understood.
3. To address this gap we used an ex situ survey of over 4000 Australian residents 
and examined a range of spatial and social factors in influencing perceptions 
towards noticing and pleasantness of bird sounds and traffic noise within urban 
parks.
4. The results indicate older age, recent park use, and valuing parks for uses such as 
exercising, socialising and to hear nature, were positively associated with pleas-
ant bird sound experiences in parks. For traffic sounds, we found indicators of 
disadvantage, such as renting a home were associated with a higher likelihood of 
unpleasant experiences of traffic noise within parks. Notably, individuals using 
parks for social activities were still positively associated with pleasant bird sound 
experiences and less likely to perceive traffic sounds as unpleasant. Conversely, 
whilst those with greater nature-­relatedness had a strong positive association 
with pleasant bird sound experiences, they were more likely to perceive traffic 
as unpleasant.
5. Overall, these findings suggest that whilst many types of park users can poten-
tially experience the beneficial effects from bird sounds, the detrimental effects 
of traffic noise may constrain these benefits in certain social groups, particularly 
those of greater socio-­economic disadvantage. We discuss strategies to pro-
mote greater equity of therapeutic experiences of bird sounds in parks, whilst 
outlining traffic mitigation measures within urban parks.

1  |  INTRODUCTION
Urbanisation is an ongoing phenomenon around the world with 
over half the world's population now residing in cities (United 
Nations,  2018). As this number continues to rise, green and blue 
spaces are of key importance in promoting the social and ecologi-
cal sustainability of cities, and critical in providing a range of bene-
fits to urban residents that can improve physical and psychological 
well-­being (Benedict & McMahon, 2012; Chiesura, 2004; Elmqvist 
et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2020). Increasingly, natural sounds and sound-
scapes, such as bird calls, have been shown to promote greater 
mental well-­being amongst people (Chuengsatiansup, 1999; Francis 
et al., 2017; Frumkin et al., 2017; Yang & Kang, 2005). Yet, compared 
to visual experiences, factors influencing restorative nature interac-
tions experienced through the auditory pathway are poorly under-
stood (Pijanowski, Farina, et al., 2011; Ratcliffe, 2021).
Ecosystem services are defined as the benefits human pop-
ulations derive, directly or indirectly, from ecosystem functions 
(Costanza et al., 1997) and have become a key framework for link-
ing ecosystem function and human welfare (Fisher et al., 2009). The 
Common International Classification of Ecosystem Services (CICES; 
Maes et al., 2013; Potschin & Haines-­Young, 2016) defines cultural 
ecosystem services as intangible ecosystem outputs that can facilitate 
a range of experiential and intellectual activities, such as recreation, 
proximity to wildlife, social interaction, spiritual inspiration and reflec-
tion (Potschin & Haines-­Young, 2016). Given their potential to pro-
mote stress recovery (Aletta et al., 2018; Alvarsson et al., 2010) and 
attention restoration (Krzywicka & Byrka, 2017; Payne, 2013; Ratcliffe 
et al., 2013; Uebel et al., 2021), natural soundscapes commonly ex-
perienced in urban parks, are increasingly recognised as important 
cultural ecosystem services in their own right (Francis et al., 2017). 
There is also growing evidence of an important interplay between 
visual and auditory experiences of nature (Zhang et al., 2019), with 
studies showing visual stimuli of nature settings (i.e. photographs) are 
perceived more positively when accompanied by congruent natural 
sounds (Hedblom et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2018). Yet, while this re-
search is becoming established, less attention has focussed on who is 
most likely to experience these sounds within urban parks (Pijanowski, 
Villanueva-­Rivera, et al., 2011). This is a crucial gap given that urban 
populations and the spatial characteristics of where people live are

METHODS
2.1  |  Participants and procedure
Our primary research questions involved investigating how peo-
ple across social and spatial gradient notice and perceive bird and 
traffic sounds in park soundscapes. Given this, we favoured an 
ex situ survey over an in situ approach for its ability to sample a 
much greater number of participants in cities and regions through-
out Australia across a wide sociodemographic and socioeconomic 
spectrum. Further, in situ approaches utilising intercept techniques 
can sometimes result in samples biased towards over-­representing 
participants with greater interest in the survey topic, reducing rep-
resentation of groups with lower rates of park use or nature appre-
ciation. An ex situ approach also followed methodologies of similar 
studies, such as Shanahan et al. (2015) and Lin et al. (2017).
Prior to survey deployment, we conducted internal pilot testing 
to assess the clarity and suitability of questions; a number of minor 
changes were made to improve the clarity of questions. We col-
lected data as part of a national survey, conducted in January 2019 
on Australian residents in urban, peri-­urban and rural locations, and 
recruited by a social research company (PureProfile). Participants 
were provided with standard incentives, accruing points that could 
later be redeemed as cash payments or giftcards to the value of a 
few dollars. All participant voluntarily enrolled and provided writ-
ten consent. We targeted a broad sample of Australians, based on 
gender, age and state of residence, covering a range of demographic 
groups to assess our research questions across a broad spectrum 
of the population. The survey was conducted in accordance with 
approved guidelines and following Institutional Human Research 
Ethics Approval by The University of Queensland (Approval 
#2018002524). Participants provided informed written consent.
In total there were 4105 respondents, with just over half (52.7%) 
male and a mean age of 48.24 years (SD = 16.9). Most participants 
lived in urban areas (75.4%), which are largely found in coastal areas 
of Australia (Figure 1). Almost three-­quarters (74.0%) were Australia 
born and the overwhelming majority (87.5%) only spoke English at 
home. Just under a third (30.7%) had completed up to Year 12 or 
equivalent, a similar amount (30.2%) held a professional diploma or 
certificate and over a third (37.8%) held a university degree. Just 
over a third (34.1%) earned above an income of $80,000, and almost 
two-­thirds (64.2%) owned or were buying their residence. Whilst not 
a true representative sample given the median age in Australia is 
38 years with 50.7% female, this still represented a broad sample 
of the Australia public (ABS, 2016). Differing education levels were 
also close to national breakdowns between these three categories 
(ABS, 2016).

2.2  |  Survey content
The survey questioned participants about various aspects of their 
use, appreciation and subjective proximity to green and blue spaces, 
as well as noticing and perceived pleasantness of bird sounds and 
traffic sounds within a local park they last visited. There were also 
a range of sociodemographic and socioeconomic questions which 
included age, gender, education level, income level, home owner-
ship status and language other than English (LOTE) spoken at home 
(Table S1).
2.3  |  Measures
2.3.1  |  Dependent variables—­noticing and 
perceived pleasantness of bird sounds
Both noticing birds and perceiving them as pleasant have been asso-
ciated with a greater potential for psychological restoration (Uebel 
et al., 2021) and, as such, both of these measures were included in 
the survey.
• Noticing bird sounds: based on Payne (2008) and evaluated with a 
single item (‘Thinking about your last visit to a park, please rate how 
often you had the following experiences: I noticed bird sounds’), with 
4 response options (Not at all; A little; A lot; I do not remember). 
A binary variable was created with (1 = A little or A lot) responses 
and (0 = Not at all) options.
• Perceived pleasantness of bird sounds: based on Payne (2008), was 
assessed with a single item (‘During this recent visit, how pleasant 
or annoying were these experiences?’), on a 6-­point scale (1 = Very 
unpleasant, 6 = Very pleasant) with an additional option (I did not 
have this experience). A binary variable was created with (1 = Very 
pleasant or pleasant) responses and (0 = all other options).
2.3.2  |  Dependent variables—­Noticing and 
perceived unpleasantness of traffic sounds
Both noticing and perceived unpleasantness of traffic sounds are as-
sociated with reduced perceived restorative potential of park sound-
scapes (Uebel et al., 2021) and, as such, both of these measures were 
also included in the survey.
• Noticing traffic sounds: based on Payne (2008) and modified to a 
four-­point scale to reflect the more binary nature of noticing a 
sound. Evaluated with a single item (‘Thinking about your last visit 
to a park, please rate how often you had the following experiences: I 
noticed traffic sounds’), with 4 response options (Not at all; A lit-
tle; A lot; I do not remember). A binary variable was created with 
(1 = A little or A lot) responses and (0 = Not at all) options.
