A B S T R A C T 
This study focuses on how urban functions influence the soundscape in streets by exploring associations among 
human behaviors, soundscape, acoustic indicators. On-site soundscape assessments were conducted in eight 
urban streets located in Seoul employing a soundwalk method. The eight streets were classified into two place 
functions: central business district (CBD) and commercial areas. Through the soundwalks, acoustic recordings 
and perceptual responses to soundscapes in the streets were collected. As a human behavioral indicator, crowd 
density was calculated in the streets. The results showed that place function plays a critical role in soundscape 
evaluation in urban streets. Pleasantness decreased with increasing Eventfulness on streets in CBD. In contrast, 
Pleasantness had a positive correlation with Eventfulness on commercial streets. This difference in the correlation 
could be attributed to the difference in the compositions of sound sources between central business and com­
mercial areas. Traffic noise was the most dominant sound source in CBD, which increased Eventfulness and 
decreased Pleasantness of the soundscape in the streets. Meanwhile, sounds from human activities contributed to 
an increase in both Pleasantness and Eventfulness in commercial streets. Predictive models of soundscape 
reflecting the contexts of CBD and commercial streets were developed using acoustic parameters and crowd 
density. In the context of CBD, acoustic parameters describing spectro-temporal characteristics of traffic noise 
were important indicators, whereas crowd density was found to be a critical indicator to predict soundscape in 
the commercial streets. These findings could help in better understanding the relationships between the 
soundscape and place functions in urban streets. 
1. Introduction 
Urban streets are common open spaces that citizens usually experi­
ence in their daily lives. According to the report of the United Nations 
Human Settlements Programme in 2013, the proportion of land allo­
cated to a street in the urban core area ranged from 18% to 33% in cities 
in Europe, North America, Oceania, and Asia [1]. Because they play a 
multi-faceted role in urban environments by enhancing community ac­
tivities [2], fostering urban economies [3], and providing vibrant urban 
environments [4], urban streets have been recognized as vital compo­
nents of sustainable development and the improvement of quality of life. 
Sound environment in urban streets is one of the critical environ­
mental factors in judging the experience of the urban streetscape. 
Several studies have identified that the perception of the streetscape is 
affected not only by visual but also acoustic components [5–8]. Hong 
and Jeon [5] investigated the effects of audio and visual components on 
perceptions of streetscape quality and found that acoustic comfort plays 
a significant role in the assessment of the perceived streetscape quality 
when A (weighted traffic noise level) is high (i.e., 70 dB). Echevarria 
Sanchez et al. [6] also showed that the pleasantness rating of urban 
streetscapes increased with decreasing noise levels. Aside from traffic 
noise levels in urban streets, Meng et al. [7,8] revealed that sounds from 
human activities in urban commercial streets could positively or nega­
tively affect acoustic comfort of urban streets depending on crowd 
density. They observed that acoustic comfort in commercial streets 
exhibited a parabolic change trend of increase followed by a decrease as 
a function of crowd density. These studies have stressed the importance 
of soundscape design for creating a sustainable urban street 
environment.

soundscape evaluation and design. Herranz-Pascual et al. [10] have 
proposed a holistic model that illustrates how the context interacts with

Method 
2.1. Study area 
As shown in Fig. 1, eight urban streets in Seoul, Korea, were selected 
across the north (Joong-Gu and Jongro-Gu area) and south (Gangnam- 
Gu) parts of Seoul for this field study. Two factors, main street function 
[12,15,24] and street morphology [25–27], which were found to affect 
urban soundscapes in previous studies, were considered for choosing the 
study areas. The eight streets were classified into two urban functions, 
central business district (CBD) and commercial areas, to explore the 
effect of the place function on soundscapes in urban streets. Three urban 
streets were selected in the CBD in Seoul. Since the streets mainly consist 
of office buildings with major roads, they were labelled O1–O3. Spe­
cifically, O1 (Teheran-daero) and O3 (Secho-daero) are located in the 
representative central business district in Gangnam-Gu, while O2 
(Jongno 1ga) is situated in Jongno-Gu which is Seoul’s traditional 
business district. 
Five urban streets were selected in commercial districts in Seoul. 
Since the commercial streets are located in shopping areas primarily 
composed of retail stores and restaurants, they were labelled S1–S5. The 
selected commercial shopping streets are popular places in Seoul, where 
both residents and tourists frequently visit. S1 (Gangnam-daero) and S4 
(Garosoo gil) are popular shopping and entertainment areas in Gangnam 
District. Meanwhile, S2 (Myeong-dong gil) is the main street of Myeong- 
dong district which is one of the primary shopping districts in Joong-Gu 
area. S3 (Insa-dong gil) is a popular street where galleries and traditional 
restaurants, teahouses, and cafes are located. S5, located in Euljiro 4ga 
area, is an old commercial space of Seoul. S5 mainly consists of small 
metal workshops, print shops, and lighting stores, with relatively fewer 
restaurants and retail stores along the street compared with other 
commercial streets (S1-4). 
In terms of street morphology, road width and building height varied 
across the selected urban streets. The width of the streets varied from 10 
to 42 m, while the mean building height in the streets ranged from 12 to 
115 m. Overall, the streets in CBD had wider street width and higher 
mean building height than the commercial streets except for O3 and S1; 
the width of O3 was 12 m, which was narrower than those of other 
streets in CBD. Meanwhile, the street width of S1 was 40 m which was 
much wider as compared to those of the other commercial streets. The 
street length of the eight streets was approximately 300 m. Mean height- 
to-width ratios (H/W) of the urban streets ranged from 0.5 (S5) to 6.1 
(S3), which is within the range of H/W in high-density cities [28,29]. 
Since some of the eight urban streets were located too far from each 
other, the urban streets were grouped into two soundwalk routes A (O2, 
S2, S3, and S5) and B (O1, O3, S1, and S4) to save on traveling time 
between the locations. Each soundwalk route consisted of urban streets 
within a radius of 2 km. 
2.2. Soundscape and human behavior data collection 
Subjective responses to acoustic environments (Section 2.2.1) and 
physical acoustic measurement (Section 2.2.2) were measured based on 
on-site soundwalk method. In addition, human behavior data in urban 
streets were collected based on the observation method described in 
Section 2.2.3. Procedure and data analyses were delineated in Section 
2.3 and 2.4, respectively. 
2.2.1. Questionnaire 
A questionnaire survey based on Swedish Soundscape-Quality Pro­
tocol [30] was conducted for collecting data on how people perceive an 
acoustic environment. The questionnaires were translated into Korean. 
In the first part of the questionnaire, the dominance of different sound 
source types was assessed using a 5- point scale (0: do not hear at all; 1: 
hear a little; 2: hear moderately; 3: hear a lot; 4: dominates completely). 
In the questionnaire, sound sources were classified into five categories: 
traffic noises (cars, buses, motorcycles, etc.), sounds from human beings 
(conversations, footsteps, laughter, etc.), natural sounds (water, birds 
chirping, wind sounds, etc.), music (music sounds from shops, street 
busking music sounds, etc.), others (construction noise, mechanical 
noise, etc.). 
The second part of the questionnaire dealt with the perceived af­
fective quality of soundscape. The perceived affective quality of 
soundscape was assessed using the eight unidirectional attributes: 
pleasant, annoying, eventful, uneventful, chaotic, calm, vibrant, and 
monotonous with the following question, “To what extent do you agree 
with the eight attributes below on how you experience the present surrounding 
sound environment?”. A five-point scale (0: strongly disagree, 1: slightly 
disagree, 2: neither disagree nor agree, 3: slightly agree, and 4: strongly 
agree) was used to evaluate the eight adjective attributes. 
2.2.2. Binaural measurement and acoustic indicators 
The binaural measurement is recommended in the soundscape 
assessment to capture the acoustic environment as close as possible to 
the human auditory sensation [31]. While the participants were 
assessing the soundscape using the questionnaire, 5-min binaural re­
cordings were conducted by a non-participatory operator using a 
binaural microphone (Type 4101, B&K) and a portable field recorder 
(Zoom, H4n). The sampling frequency and the bit depth of the binaural 
recording were 44.1 kHz and 24 bits, respectively. During the binaural 
measurement, the operator wearing the binaural microphone with a 
windshield was standing at the most typical listener positions and the 
operator’s head was facing toward the participant group without 
rotating his head to conduct the recording in a stationary condition. The 
height of the binaural microphone was approximately 1.6 m above the 
ground.
