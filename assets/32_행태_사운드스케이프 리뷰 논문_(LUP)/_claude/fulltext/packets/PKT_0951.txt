A B S T R A C T 
Understanding the influence of built environment on running behavior is a significant step towards developing 
related landscape strategies. This paper adopted a volunteered geographic information (VGI) approach to 
measure urban runnability by quantifying environmental features that encourage or hinder running activities. 
The GPS-based routes collected from Strava were used to compute the running intensity of street segments in 
Helsinki, Finland. We applied multilevel regression models to assess the spatial-varied impacts of street envi­
ronment on running intensity, so as to elicit runner’s preferences and investigate their associations with socio­
demographic characteristics at higher hierarchical level (neighbourhood). The results showed street greenery 
assessed by Green View Index (GVI) represented the greenness exposure to runners better than top-down 
greenness assessed by Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), and thus can be considered as a more 
reliable predictor for running behaviours. Blue space density was the predominant factor and associated with 
running intensity positively. Running intensity negatively correlated with urban density, connectivity and traffic 
accidents, and positively correlated with traffic noise and air pollution. Population density and income level were 
positively associated with running intensity. These results provide profound insights and boost our capacity to 
design more attractive and sustainable streets for physical activity. 
1. Introduction 
In sustainable city design, making a rational use of the natural and 
technological resources that its population requires is one of the critical 
issues. Both natural landscapes and public spaces are vital in the global 
city to avoid the burden of concrete by means of enhancing the 
communication between residents and promoting sports. Physical 
inactivity is one of the most significant contributors to the deterioration 
of health and wellbeing (Althoff et al., 2017; Kohl et al., 2012). As a 
convenient, comparatively low-cost and non-organized exercise for all 
ages (Hodgson & Hitchings, 2018; Janssen et al., 2017), running activity 
has become one of the most popular sport activities in Europe (Scheerder 
& Breedveld, 2015). Running exercise is beneficial to reduce risks of 
chronic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, obesity and cancers, as well as 
boosting emotional wellness by releasing stress, avoiding cognitive 
decline and preventing memory disorders (Lee et al., 2017; Oja et al., 
2017). Overlapping with the ideas of walkability and cycle-friendly 
cities, running-friendly cities also require superior accessibility, link­
ages, safety, esthetic and design considerations (Boakye et al., 2021; 
Schuurman et al., 2021). However, compared to walking and cycling (Ki 
& Lee, 2021; Lu et al., 2019), the associations between running activity 
and environmental features have rarely been investigated (Ettema, 
2015), leaving a gap on how urban street environment affects running 
exercise. Moreover, finding the balance between conflicting political 
and social demands on limited space has become a new challenge in 
urban design (Meseneva, 2020), which requires both qualitative and 
quantitative studies focusing on the various effects of natural environ­
ment and built environment characteristics on residents.

2.1. Study area 
Helsinki, the capital of Finland and the hub of the Helsinki Metro­
politan Area, covers 217 km2 of land and boasts a population of 658,457 
residents (Official Statistics of Finland OSF, 2022). Helsinki consists of 
60 subdistricts, which can be further divided into 137 neighbourhoods 
(excluding Aluemeri that consists of outer coastal water only). Helsinki 
offers a total of approximately 2100 outdoor public sports facilities, 
including sports parks, fields, neighbourhood sports sites, jogging tracks 
and skiing tracks (City of Helsinki Executive Board, 2018). These out­
door sports services almost cover every neighbourhood in the city of 
Helsinki. 
2.2. Data description 
2.2.1. Dependent variable: Running intensity of street segments 
The running activity VGI data for this study were obtained from 
Strava in March 2021. By the end of May 2022, Strava had more than 
100 million registered users and 7 billion uploaded sports activities all 
over the world. It is one of the most popular sports-tracking and social 
platforms in Finland. The GPS-based routes were collected among run­
ners who posted their running experiences on Strava between 2018 and 
2021. We used the geo-referenced data of running routes without per­
sonal data. The running routes for length less than 1 km were excluded, 
because they are too short to indicate the segment preferences of 
runners. 
The running intensity of street segments were calculated as follows. 
First, we obtained the street network for city of Helsinki from the public 
available mapping platform OpenStreetMap, and broke the street 
network into segments by intersections. Then, we computed a 20 m 
buffer around each street segment to define the exposure to environ­
mental conditions as utilized elsewhere (Salazar et al., 2021). Next, we 
summed up the length of all running routes that are partly overlap or 
entirely fall within the respective buffer of each street segment. Finally, 
we calculated the running intensity by dividing the total length of 
running routes by its buffer zone area, which solved the problem that the 
total lengths of running routes in large buffers tend to be longer than 
those in small buffers. Our final sample comprises 13,322 street

2.2.2. Covariates at street segment level: Environmental predictors 
2.2.2.1. Natural environment factors. Natural environment factors for 
explaining running activity intensity were selected based on the previ­
ously described literature, which include Green View Index (GVI), 
Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), blue space density and 
terrain slope (Campbell et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2022; Ki & Lee, 2021; 
Lu et al., 2019). The top-down greenness and blueness were assessed by 
NDVI and blue space density respectively. The NDVI index was collected 
from Landsat 8 OLI (Operational Land Imager) and TIRS (Thermal 
Infrared Sensor) data at 30 m spatial resolution via the United States 
Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer website (https://earthexplorer 
.usgs.gov). The all cloud-free images during the period from 6th June to 
7th July in 2021 were obtained. The NDVI was calculated based on the 
wavelengths of the light absorbed by green plant canopies. Pixels with a 
negative NDVI were excluded before computing the average NDVI per 
street segment as suggested elsewhere (Rugel et al., 2017). The NDVI 
values range from 0 to 1 with higher values indicating denser vegeta­
tion. The blue space density was assessed by the ratio of aquatic envi­
ronmental exposure within the street segment buffers following the 
approach employed in the previous study (Huang et al., 2022). The slope 
within a street segment buffer was determined by the average value of a 
slope map which was created from a GDAL-supported elevation raster 
(Huang et al., 2022). 
The GVI, which represents the eye-level greenness exposure to run­
ners on streets (Huang et al., 2022), was calculated by averaging the GVI 
values of Google Street View (GSV) images collected within the street 
segment buffers. First, sample points for generating GSV images were 
created with a uniform distance of 50 m along street network. The co­
ordinates of these points were input into a Python script and were uti­
lized to download six GSV images for each point. The six GSV images 
contained 0◦(North), 60◦, 120◦, 180◦, 240◦and 300◦headings, and each 
one was with a 60-degree field of view. Because the amount of street 
greenery varies by seasons, only the images taken between May and 
September were utilized. The street view image data for this research 
were obtained from Google Maps using Python scripts in 2021. The 
calculation of GVI was performed by a deep learning technique of fully 
conventional neural network for semantic segmentation (Huang et al., 
2022; Toikka et al., 2020). The average value of six images for each 
sample point was used to assess the amount of street greenery of that 
location.
