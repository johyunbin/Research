A B S T R A C T
This study examines factors beyond macroscale built environments (BE) influencing cycling 
behavior, integrating both microscale visual and auditory perceptions, especially soundscapes, 
while their impacts on cycling have not been explicitly investigated. Leveraging massive bike­
share trip data in Shenzhen, China, this study employs spatial explainable machine learning to 
uncover the associations among these three dimensions and cycling behavior using a multi-scale 
analytical framework. The results show that: (1) Macroscale BE remains dominant, while micro- 
scale soundscapes outweigh visual perceptions in explaining cycling usage. (2) Both perceived 
streetscape and soundscape indicators exhibit significant nonlinear effects on cycling behavior. 
(3) Synergistic interactions between visual streetscape and soundscape are observed, where 
maintaining audio-visual consistency (e.g., high perceived safety with high natural sounds 
perception) can positively affect cycling more substantially. Our findings encourage planners to 
prioritize strategic micro-level modifications targeting perceived street visual and auditory as­
pects over conventional macroscale BE interventions as cost-effective alternatives.
1. Introduction
Cycling offers numerous advantages, notably promoting sustainability by reducing car travel dependent on fossil fuels, thereby 
supporting environmentally conscious mobility. It fosters healthier lifestyles, and its affordability improves urban inclusivity, aligning 
with human-centric planning concepts such as the 15-minute city (Pozoukidou and Chatziyiannaki, 2021). Moreover, as an indis­
pensable multimodal travel component, cycling, particularly bikeshare systems can complement public transit as a feeder mode, 
addressing the “first and last mile” challenge and thereby effectively enhancing public transit efficiency and fostering interconnected 
urban environments (Bullock et al., 2017). Additionally, bicycling is gaining popularity and expanding rapidly in densely populated 
countries like China (Zhou and Schwanen, 2024), offering significant daily travel benefits by providing a flexible, efficient, and cost- 
effective means of urban mobility, such as commuting to work. However, the low usage rates (T. Gu et al., 2019) present profitability 
challenges for operators, jeopardizing the long-term sustainability of these systems. To fully harvest these benefits, it is imperative to

Extensive empirical evidence highlights the significant impact of the built environment (BE) on cycling behavior (Etminani- 
Ghasrodashti et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2019). While earlier studies primarily focused on macro-scale BE, including 
cycling infrastructure (Mateo-Babiano et al., 2016) and land use types like recreational areas (Etminani-Ghasrodashti et al., 2018), 
recent research also revealed the non-negligible effects of micro-level, human-perceived environmental features, such as fine-grained 
streetscape elements (Gao and Fang, 2025; Song et al., 2024c). According to environmental psychology theory (Mehrabian, 1974), 
these perceptions could influence emotions and consequently shape on-route behavioral decisions.
Leveraging advanced modeling and urban imagery data, emerging research attempted to assess the effect of micro-level streetscape 
perceptions using street view images (SVI) on cycling behaviors (Yang et al., 2024). However, these studies objectively quantified 
image pixel ratios through “machine-style” segmentation and focused on features like trees and sky, failing to account for compre­
hensive human-perceived streetscape qualities that are inherently subjective and vary among individuals (Ramírez et al., 2021). 
Moreover, the potential nuanced non-linear influence of these subjective perceptions (e.g., perceived aesthetics, safety, lively) on 
cycling behavior remains poorly understood (Gao and Fang, 2025; Song et al., 2024c).
