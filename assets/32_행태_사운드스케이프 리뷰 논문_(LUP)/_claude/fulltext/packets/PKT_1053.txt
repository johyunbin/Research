Abstract
Online platforms have broadened the opportunities of people to interact with nature through community/citizen science, 
especially in urban areas. However, there is a lack of comprehensive understanding of the social and environmental factors 
that influence nature interactions in cities. Here, we aim to identify the social and environmental predictors that impact 
nature interactions, by combining citizen science data with environmental and demographic data in New York City. We 
applied generalized linear models to identify which of 12 social and environmental factors influence nature interactions in 
public parks (n = 355) in the Borough of Queens, New York, New York (USA) in two scenarios, small-medium sized parks 
only (n = 355) and all parks (n = 359). We used iNaturalist records, including the number of users (Observers), Observa­
tions, Observed Biodiversity, and a calculated interaction effect (number of users × observations, Interaction), as metrics 
of nature interactions. For small-medium parks, all nature interactions were significantly influenced by park area, canopy 
cover, percent population with blood pressure and asthma conditions, noise, and summer mean temperature. Observers and 
Interaction were positively associated with median income. Observers, Observations, and Interaction were predicted by 
percent water cover, impervious cover, distance to public transportation, and ethnic diversity. In the analysis that included 
all parks, the results were similar with minor differences. This study demonstrates a holistic approach to a very specific 
type of human-nature interaction newly made available with technological advances, seen through an interdisciplinary 
lens and will help inform planners, residents, and city government on creating more interactive and socio-environmentally 
beneficial urban green spaces.

Understanding human-nature interactions helps us 
develop strategies to maximize ecosystem services (Kluger 
et al. 2020), increase public health (Keniger et al. 2013), 
engage people with the surrounding environment, and raise 
awareness of environmental issues (van Noordwijk et al. 
2021). There are many ways of assessing the level and types 
of interaction people make with nature (Auyeung et al. 
2016). One of the ways of interacting with nature, enabled 
through technological advances (Arts et al. 2021), is uti­
lizing citizen science platforms. iNaturalist and eBird are 
considered the most popular and globalized citizen science 
data platforms. iNaturalist allows users to record the loca­
tion, time, and picture (or sound) of organisms they encoun­
ter, of 11 taxa from fauna and flora to an online database 
(Callaghan et al. 2022). eBird has similar features with taxa 
specialized to avifauna. Once observations are made, other 
users may share their opinion or confirm the identification 
of the species. These platforms enable users to interact eas­
ily and quickly with local biodiversity and discuss findings 
with other users (Altrudi 2020; Silk et al. 2021; Callaghan 
et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2023).
Many attempts have been made to examine the spatial 
patterns of citizen science users and data. Studies have 
found that the locations of citizen science data were closely 
related to density of paths (Tiago et al. 2017), distance to 
human settlement (Cretois et al. 2021), population density 
(Mair and Ruete 2016), or the knowledge of rare species 
sightings (Tulloch et al. 2013). Studies such as Tiago et al. 
(2017), also found that accessibility and landscape charac­
teristics of a location, such as habitat type and presence of 
water features, impacted the type of taxa observed. Some 
studies argue that these patterns of participation for citizen 
science are a result of bias (Cretois et al. 2021; Tang et al.
