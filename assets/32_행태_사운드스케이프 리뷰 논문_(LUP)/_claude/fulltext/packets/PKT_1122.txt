A B S T R A C T
Although natural sounds are widely known to positively affect human well-being, the influence of natural sound 
tempo, a potentially significant factor in shaping emotional and behavioral responses, remains underexplored 
within environmental psychology. This study examines the impact of the tempo of natural sounds on individuals’ 
willingness for social interaction using a virtual site tour approach. Scenarios were created in Unity 3D, incor­
porating environmental sounds like birdsong and water fountains typically found in urban parks. Participants 
freely explored the simulated site and interacted with non-player characters, with their behaviors recorded. Their 
personal social condition, evaluations of the sound environment, and willingness for social interaction were 
assessed through questionnaires. This study found that faster water sound tempos significantly increased the 
willingness for social interaction among individuals with medium to high levels of personal social condition. 
However, changes in birdsong tempo had no significant effect on social interaction willingness. Participants with 
higher levels of social condition showed over 11 % greater willingness for social interaction and more positive 
evaluations of the sound environment. These findings highlight the role of sound tempo in influencing social 
behaviors, which may contribute to the development of environmental psychology and offer practical insights for 
urban soundscape design.
1. Introduction
As urbanization continues to expand globally, urban planners and 
environmental designers face increasing challenges in creating healthy 
and livable cities. Among the various factors influencing urban life 
quality, the sound environment plays a vital role. Research shows that 
natural sounds in urban spaces not only enhance individual well-being 
and health but also improve the overall urban experience and foster 
social interactions (Alvarsson et al., 2010; X. Chen & Kang, 2023; Fisher 
et al., 2021). Further exploring the specific effects of different natural 
sounds can offer valuable insights for shaping future urban design 
practices. This paper investigates the relationship between the tempo of 
natural sounds and people’s willingness for social interaction, aiming to 
integrate well-paced natural sounds into urban spaces to promote more 
meaningful social engagement.
1.1. Urban sound environment and social interaction
The global urbanization process has rapidly increased urban 
populations, making the creation of better urban living environments a 
significant and enduring focus (Ventriglio et al., 2021). The sound 
environment, a critical component of urban settings, significantly im­
pacts human health, psychology, and behavior (Andringa & Lanser, 
2013; Kang & Schulte-Fortkamp, 2016; Stansfeld et al., 2000). Urban 
noise from sources like traffic, construction, and human activities is 
considered a major threat to the quality of life and health of urban 
residents (Passchier-Vermeer & Passchier, 2000; Salomons & Pont, 
2012). Extensive research has linked prolonged exposure to excessive 
urban noise with numerous health issues, including hearing loss, car­
diovascular diseases, and increased psychological stress (Beutel et al., 
2016; Daniel, 2007; Münzel et al., 2021). Furthermore, noise pollution 
significantly affects work and daily life by reducing work efficiency, 
impairing sleep quality, diminishing life satisfaction, and weakening 
cognitive functions, which can adversely affect children’s development 
and language acquisition (Klatte et al., 2013; Muzet, 2007; Orru et al., 
2016; Pal & Bhattacharya, 2012).
In addition to these health concerns, social interaction is a vital 
component of psychological well-being and social functioning, and it is

Methodology
This study developed a novel virtual site interactive tour method to 
investigate how sound tempo influences individuals’ willingness to 
engage in social interactions among individuals with varying social 
conditions. Traditional methods for studying the influence of sound 
environments on individuals primarily include laboratory experiments, 
field surveys, and online questionnaires. However, these methods are 
insufficient to meet the objectives of this study. Participation in labo­
ratory experiments or surveys inherently involves social interaction, 
which may attract individuals already inclined towards social 
engagement. Since this study also seeks to explore the influence of sound 
environment changes on individuals with lower social interaction levels, 
an online method that avoids direct human contact may reduce partic­
ipant bias. A virtual city park scenario was developed using Unity3D, 
incorporating a social interaction system for participants to explore 
online. During the tour, participants control a character to move freely 
and interact with non-player characters. After the tour, participants 
were asked to complete a questionnaire covering their personal social 
conditions, tour experiences, sound environment evaluations, and per­
sonal information such as gender and age.
2.1. Sound sources
This study selected birdsong and water sounds as sound sources due 
to their prevalence in urban environments and their potential to 
enhance social interactions. Research has identified water sounds, 
birdsong, and music as the three preferred types of sounds in urban 
environments. Surveys indicate that over 75 % of respondents express a 
positive attitude toward water and bird sounds, whereas fewer than 10 
% find these sounds disturbing (Carles et al., 1999; Yang & Kang, 
2005b). This highlights the widespread public preference for natural 
sounds in urban settings. Studies have also shown that different types of 
birdsong exhibit varying effects on perceived restorativeness, but 
overall, the introduction of multiple bird species’ vocalizations tends to 
enhance the perceived restorativeness of a site (Zhao et al., 2020). Given 
that birdsong in natural environments is typically not singular, this 
study selected a mixed birdsong recorded in an urban park as the sound 
source. This mixed birdsong more closely replicates real ecological en­
vironments, providing a better simulation of the actual auditory expe­
rience in urban parks. Regarding water sounds, different types of water 
sounds may evoke distinct feelings. Some research has found that nat­
ural water sounds are more calming than artificial ones (G. R. Watts 
et al., 2009), while other studies have revealed that fountain sounds are 
preferred over waterfall sounds (Galbrun & Ali, 2013). This study 
selected fountain sound as the water sound source, as fountains are 
common landscape elements in urban parks, and their volume and 
tempo can be controlled by adjusting water pressure and flow. It is 
important to note that although both birdsong and water sound are 
categorized as natural sounds, they exhibit significant differences in 
signal characteristics and should be considered distinct auditory stimuli. 
As such, they cannot collectively represent the overall characteristics of 
natural sounds. This study simulated these two sound environments 
separately, and data from each condition were analyzed independently.
As natural sounds differ from music, specific BPM (beats per minute) 
values are not applicable. Therefore, the duration of a selected bird 
sound and a water sound was adjusted by shortening or extending them 
using audio editing software, without altering the pitch, to modify the 
tempo. A preliminary study was conducted to establish the acceptable

2.2. Virtual scene reproduction
This study employed Unity3D to develop a virtual city park scene to 
ensure consistency between the auditory stimuli and the environment. 
Introducing natural sounds into an artificial setting lacking natural el­
ements could potentially create a sense of incongruity between the 
auditory stimuli and the visual context, thereby influencing partici­
pants’ authentic responses. It is acknowledged that some studies have 
demonstrated the positive impacts of introducing natural sounds into 
artificial or urbanized environments (Coensel et al., 2011; Hong et al., 
2020; Van Renterghem et al., 2020). However, to minimize confounding 
factors and ensure a focused assessment of sound effects on social 
interaction, the present study employed a park environment as the 
experimental setting. The scene design primarily consists of four mod­
ules: physical environment, non-player character system, character 
control and dialogue system, and acoustic environment.
