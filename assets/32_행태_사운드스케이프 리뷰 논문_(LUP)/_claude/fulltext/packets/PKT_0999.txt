A B S T R A C T 
Pedestrian spaces adjacent to arterial roads are characterized by the dominance of traffic noise alongside various 
human activities. Research on the impact of traffic noise on the soundscape evaluation of pedestrian spaces has 
not considered human activities spatial contexts. To address this research gap, the present study constructed 
auditory environments for pedestrian spaces in the contexts of commuting, residential, and commercial activ­
ities. A total of seven auditory environments were subjected to laboratory auditory evaluations, including 
perceived dominance of sound source, acoustic comfort, and perceived affective quality of the soundscape. The 
results indicated that in pedestrian spaces with constant traffic noise, the presence of significant human activity 
sounds led to a decreased perceived dominance of traffic noise and an increased acoustic comfort, despite the 
higher acoustic energy. Thus, pedestrian spaces with a variety of human activity received better soundscape 
evaluations. The elements that reflected the human activities spatial contexts, including the types and intensity of 
human activities, played a crucial role in soundscape evaluations. Better acoustic comfort was reported in 
pedestrian spaces characterized by low-intensity residential activities and high-intensity commercial activities. 
Additionally, pedestrian spaces with more intense activities offered an actively engaging soundscape. The 
findings can provide reference for a more accurate evaluation of the soundscape in pedestrian spaces and guide 
the soundscape design of pedestrian environments.

1. Introduction 
The pedestrian space adjacent to arterial roads refers to the linear 
public space enclosed by the interfaces on both sides of the road. With 
the emergence of people-centric concepts, such as the creation of high- 
quality pedestrian spaces (Burns, 2009), extensive research has been 
conducted on the environmental comfort of pedestrian spaces. The 
acoustic environment is a critical environmental factor in judging the 
urban pedestrian space experience (Appleyard and Lintell, 1986). Ac­
cording to the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), noise 
pollution has become one of the major environmental issues faced by 
humanity (United Nations Environment Programme, 2022). Noise 
pollution also raises numerous issues related to social equity (World 
Health Organization, 2019). Therefore, in the planning and design of 
pedestrian spaces, the acoustic environment of these spaces should be 
taken into consideration (Can et al., 2020; Margaritis and Kang, 2017; 
Van Kempen et al., 2014). 
In research on the acoustic environment in pedestrian spaces, 
particular attention is given to traffic noise (Guski, 1997), as these 
spaces, owing to their proximity to arterial roads, are predominantly 
influenced by traffic noise. In China, it was found that one-third of the 
length of arterial roads had traffic noise levels exceeding 70 dBA. The 
areas on both sides of the main traffic arteries exhibited higher traffic 
noise levels due to their proximity to the road noise sources (Ministry of 
Environmental Protection, 2022). With the emergence of the concept of 
inclusive cities, urban development has shifted toward a human- 
centered approach. 
Soundscape focuses on human perception (Kang, 2017; Kang et al., 
2016; Zhang et al., 2024). ISO (International Organization for Stan­
dardization, 2014) defines soundscape as “acoustic environment as 
perceived or experienced and/or understood by a person or people, in 
context”. Set out in the previous research (Herranz-Pascual et al., 2010; 
Tarlao et al., 2021), the context is defined as interrelationships between 
person, activity, and place. After the soundscape concept was intro­
duced, soundscape research is interested in and has recently been pro­
ducing an increasing body of work focusing on – identifying the context 
and exploring changes in soundscape perception (Kang, 2006, Hong 
et al., 2020a, 2020b; Jeon, 2015; Zhao et al., 2018). The morphology of 
the area constitutes the context of a place. Soundscape perception of 
urban pedestrian areas are affected by the morphology of the area, such 
as the height-to-width ratios (H/Ws) in urban street canyon (Yilmaz 
et al., 2023); road characteristics (Lu et al., 2019);

Methods 
2.1. Selection and collection of sound sources 
The study selected pedestrian spaces in three human activities spatial 
contexts: commuting activities, residential activities and commercial 
activities. First, pilot investigations were carried out before recording 
the sound conditions required in the laboratory. We investigated a large 
number of pedestrian spaces and identified and classified sounds into 
three categories. First is traffic noise, present in all three human activ­
ities spatial contexts. Second is conversations and sports sounds, present 
in the residential activities contexts. Third is hawking and music sounds 
from shops, present in the commercial activities contexts. In the pilot 
investigations, we measured the one-minute sound pressure level (SPL) 
in the pedestrian spaces with AWA6228, and found that the SPL varied 
from 62 dB to 69 dB if residential activities dominated, and from 66 dB 
to 74 dB if commercial activities dominated. The SPL of the traffic noise 
was about 68 dB. Measured data are shown in Appendix A. 
We then selected pedestrian spaces in Dalian, China to record the

avoiding peak traffic hours. 
Previous studies have demonstrated that human sounds can effec­
tively mask noise (Meng et al., 2017). However, if the recorded sounds 
include the spatial properties of the sound, the overlaying and repro­
duction may introduce confounding effects of spatial unmasking(Jones 
and Macken, 1995; Hong et al., 2017). For this reason, we used 
monaural recordings, which has been widely used in the study of 
soundscape augmentation of natural scenes. Simultaneous, monaural 
recording methods have been comparable to binaural on most sound­
scape evaluation indicators (Xu and Kang, 2019). We recorded monaural 
audio samples using NTI XL2. The recording levels were calibrated using 
the B&K 4231 sound calibrator. We set the sampling rate and bit depth 
to 48 kHz and 24 bits. In each recording location, we selected a site 
where we could capture a “pure” sound source and then captured a six- 
minute audio recording of the sound environment. For example, when 
recording traffic noise, we positioned the microphone facing the main 
road. When capturing activity sounds, we made recordings on the side 
away from the main road. During recording, we mounted the NTi 
Acoustic Analyzer on a stand at a height of 1.5 m above the ground. The 
sound parameters measured in the field are shown in Table 1.

2.2. Auditory environments 
We used the recorded sound clips to construct auditory environments 
of pedestrian spaces with different human activities spatial contexts. In 
pedestrian spaces predominantly characterized by commuting activities 
(PSCTA), the primary noise source was TN, with a relatively low average 
human activity intensity, making human activity sounds negligible. In 
pedestrian spaces were predominantly characterized by residential ac­
tivities (PSRA), the main sounds included TN and RS. In pedestrian 
spaces predominantly characterized by commercial activities (PSCA), 
the primary sounds were TN and CS. We all know that the more intensity 
of human activity in a pedestrian space, the higher SPL of RS or CS. 
As such, we extracted a one-minute audio sample from the TN, RS 
and CS, and Fig. 2 shows the spectrograms of the sound samples. In 
particular, RS and CS fluctuated less in the high frequency range and the 
overall frequency was relatively homogeneous compared to TN. The 
concentration frequency of the high SPL of RS was lower than that of CS. 
When studying the influence of natural elements on soundscape 
perception, natural sounds have been found to mask noise, the signal-to- 
noise ratio (SNR) between natural sounds and target noise is used to 
characterize the level of natural elements (Coensel et al., 2011; Hong 
et al., 2020b; Jeon et al., 2012), The more natural elements present, the 
louder are the natural sounds, and then the greater is the SNR. Human 
sounds also have a masking effect on noise (Meng et al., 2017; Meng and 
Kang, 2015), Therefore, we utilized the SNR between activity sounds 
(signal) and traffic noise (noise) to represent the intensity of activities.
