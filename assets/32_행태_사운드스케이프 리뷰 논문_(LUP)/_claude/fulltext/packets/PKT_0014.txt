Abstract 
The effects of environmental overload and noise on helping behavior is assessed 
through a 2 x 3 experimental design including two levels of environmental overload 
and three conditions on each site: without roadworks, roadworks without noise, 
and noisy roadworks. 
Results are analysed in terms of the cognitive appraisal of the situation and the 
narrowing of attention. The subject's behavior when confronted with explicit and 
implicit helping requests shows that noise appears to be the most important com- 
ponent of overload, affecting both the subjects' attentiveness in implicit helping 
demands, as well as the refusal to engage in verbal interaction. 
Since Simmel (1950), who describes urban residents as more reserved, indifferent 
and latently hostile than rural residents, there has been much concern about social 
behavior in cities. In large towns people trust one another less (Lowin et al., 1971; 
Korte and Kerr, 1975), pedestrians look straight ahead, ignoring what is happening 
immediately around them (Korte and Grant, 1980), often avoiding eye-contact 
(Newman and McCauly, I977) and are more reluctant to assist a stranger seeking 
help (Milgram, 1970; Korte, 1978). Essentially three conceptions of the behavioral 
effects of urban settings can be distinguished: 
(1) Urban life is stressful. According to Baum et aL (1981), the behavioral effects 
of urban settings can be explained in terms of reactions to stressful events. People 
living in large cities are exposed to a variety of stimuli including visual stimuli, 
noise, pedestrian density, traffic, etc., forming what has been called 'urban stress' 
(Glass and Singer, 1972). Cities as stressful situations, inhibit activity and produce 
avoidance reactions (Lazarus and Cohen, 1977). 
(2) Undesirable or inadequate environments are constraining. Environmental 
overstimulation produces behavioral constraints (as has been shown in the case of 
crowding by Stokols, 1978), and can lead to a feeling of losing control. The subject 
then seeks to gain cognitive control over the inadequate situation by, for instance, 
isolating himself, engaging thus in behavior described as psychological reactance 
(Brehm, 1966). If the coping process is unsuccessful, it produces helplessness. The 
possibility of controlling the noise reduces adaptative stress (Glass and Singer, 1972; 
Sherrod and Downs, 1974). 
(3) Environmental overstimulation: urban environments are characterized by a 
multitude of information demanding the attention and specific responses of urban- 
ites. The complexity and abundance of urban stimuli produce what has been called

'environmental overload' (Cohen, 1978). An individual's capacity to process infor- 
mation is limited and 'overload' occurs when the urban environment exceeds this 
limit (Korte, 1978). Subjects exposed to overstimulation tend consequently to neglect 
or ignore peripheral stimuli (Cohen and Lezak, 1977; Broadbent, 1979) and react 
more strongly to the dominant aspects of different situations to which they are 
exposed. 
In conclusion, it seems that two types of possible effects of urban environments 
are described: on the one hand the narrowing and focusing of attention, and on the 
other hand the cognitive appraisal of the stressful condition leading to coping 
strategies like avoidance reactions (if control of the specific environmental situation 
seems impossible or if it does not reach expectation). 
The description of the environmental conditions of cities, as a whole (Milgram, 
1970) or as specific conditions in some areas of it (Korte, 1980), always includes the 
noise conditions. The models of the effects of urban environments, whether they 
refer to urban stressors, to the inadequacy of urban environments, or to environ- 
mental overload, are often based on laboratory studies concerning the effects of 
noise on man which extensively show that social responsiveness is largely affected 
(Bull et al., 1972; Matthews and Cannon, 1975). In spite of the fact that descriptions 
of the urban environment are essentially based on noise, they do not clearly define 
its importance in relation to the other urban stressors. Due to the difficulty of 
isolating noise from the other components in a natural urban setting, researchers 
referring, for instance, to the concept of 'environmental overload' have not tried to 
distinguish what is specially due to noise in these effects (Korte and Kerr, 1975 and 
Korte, 1980). 
In order to clarify the part played by noise in the effect on social behavior of 
urban environments with different 'overloads', we introduced an additional stress: 
the noise of heavy roadworks in two environments, one with overload, the other 
without overload. Since heavy roadworks also alter the immediate environment by 
narrowing the available space, thus restricting pedestrian circulation, an intermediate 
condition has to be taken into account: the noise-free roadwork site. This leads to 
three conditions per type of environment. Social responsiveness in these environ- 
mental conditions is analysed through altruistic behavior. The introduction of dif- 
ferent kinds of helping demands enables the testing of, on one hand, the hypothesis 
of the narrowing of attention by choosing a helping demand requiring the subjects' 
attentiveness vs. an explicit and verbal demand, and on the other hand the hypothesis 
about the desire to avoid an unpleasant noise situation by comparing requests 
requiring little or much effort. Furthermore, in order to rule out possible bystander-

Methodology 
The choice of the different helping behaviors 
The four helping behaviors chosen are expressed in the following parameters: (l) the 
effort required by the request, and (2) the implicit or explicit nature of the request: a 
situation where the subject's help is solicited or a situation where the subject is 
brought to perceive that an intervention on his part corresponds to being helpful. 
-- For the explicit request requiring little effort, a colleague approaches the subject 
and asks him where the nearest subway station is. 
-- For the explicit request requiring much effort, a colleague approaches the subject 
and asks if he would be kind enough to answer a questionnaire lasting approxi- 
mately 10 minutes. Solicited by these explicit requests, the subject can: (a) ignore the 
request without entering into any verbal interaction; (b) indicate his refusal and/or 
argue his refusal; and (c) agree to the request. 
-- For the implicit request requiring little effort, a person (the colleague) walking in 
front of the subject drops his keys when he takes a handkerchief out of his pocket 
and carries on walking without noticing. Solicited by this request the subject can: 
(a) not notice that the keys were dropped or notice the keys but ignore them; (b) 
approach or call the person, telling him that he has lost his keys and/or showing 
him where they are; pick up the keys, approach the person and hand them back to 
him. 
-- For the implicit request requiring much effort, the subject finds a plastic card 
with the owner's photograph and signature, and the address of a hospital. This card 
is an imitation diabetic card with a request that anyone finding it should return it 
urgently. There is no-one in the vicinity likely to have lost the card. The helping 
behavior in this case, once the situation has been identified, differs in so far as the 
subject cannot approach a hypothetical owner of the lost card. The possible re- 
sponses are then: (a) not seeing the card; noticing the card but ignoring it; (b) 
bending down, reading it but leaving it where it is: picking up the card, reading it 
and throwing it away; picking up the card, reading it and putting it in a clearly 
visible place; picking up the card and taking it away; (c) taking it away and for- 
warding it to the given address. 
For each of the four helping demands, an observation guide was compiled in 
order to inventory the types of behavior in terms of the possible responses. 
Choice of sites 
Two types of sites have been determined: one having low environmental overload, 
and the other being overloaded. In addition, in order to introduce extra noise stress 
to both sites, we made use of the presence of municipal roadwork sites, with intense 
noise caused by pneumatic drills, particularly when the road surface is being dug 
up. However, the presence of roadworks is liable in any case to cause a noticeable 
disturbance, regardless of the additional noise. In order to analyse and isolate the 
effects of the intense noise from those caused by the presence of the roadworks 
themselves, we compared, on each site, the three following conditions: (1) a condition 
without roadworks; (2) the presence of roadworks without the noise of pneumatic 
drills ('dormant' or noise-free roadwork site); and (3) the presence of roadworks 
together with the noise of pneumatic drills.

The presence of large roadworks and the type of population (districts of Paris 
reputed to have business buildings as well as low- and high-standard residential 
buildings) guided the choice of the experimental sites from a list of projected road- 
works provided by the Highway Department at the ParisTown Hall.* All the sites 
chosen, except one, are located within the XVth district of Paris. 
The list of possible sites was then analysed in terms of environmental descriptions. 
Since overall environmental criteria have to be considered with respect to general 
standards of the urban environment, we considered the maximum and the minimum 
on each criterion (Table 1). 
We consequently considered a site as having a low or high overload, depending 
on whether it presented all the criteria simultaneously, allowing it to be classified as 
being environmentally overloaded or not.
