A B S T R A C T 
Walking is important as a sustainable and healthy transport mode. Yet, walking is strongly influenced by the built 
environment and the socio-cultural characteristics of the walker. However, the impact of the built environment 
on walking and “walkers” has scarcely been studied in a Latin American context, such as Chile. This research 
explores which elements of the built environment ease and, contrarily, which elements hinder walking in six 
pericentral neighbourhoods in Santiago, Chile, according to self-reported walking experiences of local residents 
of different socioeconomic backgrounds, ages and genders. Through “walking interviews” with one hundred and 
twenty residents conducted during spring 2018, this article examines how walkers perceive elements of the built 
environment and how those elements elicit emotions, affecting people’s travel behaviour. Self-reports from 
walkers reveal that the presence of trees, wide sidewalks and active uses ease walking, eliciting wellbeing and 
happiness. On the contrary, residents declare that traffic noise, motorized traffic, narrow and deteriorated 
sidewalks, and difficult crossings hinder walking, especially for older adults and women, causing them stress, 
fear and anger. Finally, this article reflects upon how walking can be promoted through a more pedestrian- 
friendly design of the built environment, by considering self-reported walking experiences of local residents. 
1. Introduction 
Walking is a sustainable and healthy transport mode. Studies show 
that walking improves the physical and mental health of urban pop­
ulations (D’Haese et al., 2015; Hill et al., 2003; Morgan et al., 2010; 
Siqueira Reis et al., 2013; Williams and Thompson, 2013), reducing 
obesity and enhancing cardiovascular health. Moderate to vigorous 
walking can reduce tension and stress, and even improve sleep (CDC, 
2012). Walking is also crucial for urban vitality and social interaction 
(Jacobs, 1961; Hermann and Mora, 2017), enhances “citizenship” 
(Loukaitou-Sideris and Ehrenfeucht, 2009) and contributes to the se­
curity of urban spaces (Hillier, 1996). 
For these reasons, walking has received worldwide attention since 
the mid-1990s. For example, in the United States and United Kingdom, 
walking has been promoted as an effective strategy to enhance physical 
activity and mitigate obesity (CDC, 2012; Lean et al., 2006). Meanwhile, 
in Chile, the recent National Urban Development Policy has set out that 
walking should be promoted in urban environments (MINVU, 2014). 
It has been argued that walking is strongly conditioned by the socio- 
cultural characteristics of the walker (e.g. gender, age, income, culture) 
and environmental conditions such as noise, motorized traffic, weather 
and topography, among others (Solnit, 2000). Furthermore, a prominent 
role has been given to the built environment, since the urban form and 
the presence of certain physical elements along streets—and their 
characteristics—seem to ease or, on the contrary, make it difficult to 
travel by foot (Valenzuela-Montes and Talavera-García, 2015). 
The term “walkability” was introduced into planning literature 
during the nineties, arguing that increased walkability could lead to 
more walking. However, no clear definition of walkability exists among 
practitioners and urban planners. Some definitions relate walkability to 
the built environment and its characteristics linked, either positively or 
negatively, to walking, while others focus on the social or health-related 
outcomes of walkable places (Forsyth, 2015; Dovey and Pafka, 2019). In 
other cases, walkability has even been used as a proxy for good urban 
design (Forsyth, 2015; Speck, 2012). It is important to point out that 
most approaches to walkability have focused on meso-scale, rather than 
micro-scale attributes of the built environment such as sidewalk width 
or the presence of trees in a given block (Arellana et al., 2019).
