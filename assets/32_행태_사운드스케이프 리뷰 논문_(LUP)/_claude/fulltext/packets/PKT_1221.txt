A B S T R A C T
Urban commercial streets, as critical nodes of economic activity, exert substantial influence on consumer 
behavior and commercial performance. However, empirical research investigating how soundscape character­
istics and crowd density interact to shape economic behaviors in complex urban open spaces remains limited. 
This study addresses this gap through a case analysis of a typical commercial district in Yangzhou, China, 
constructing two integrated indicator systems. From a behavioral economics perspective, five economic behavior 
metrics are developed: Consumption intention intensity (CII), Affective consumption willingness (ACW), 
Queuing time (QT), Recommendation to friends (RTF), and Revisit intention (RE). Results reveal that both 
objective acoustic indicators (e.g., LAeq, L10, L50, L90) and perceptual dimensions of the soundscape (eventfulness, 
calmness, chaoticness) are significantly associated with these behavioral outcomes. Sound source typologies 
further moderate behavioral responses via perceived loudness, event frequency, and preference. In terms of 
spatial social density, the study introduces both planar crowd densities (CPD4, CPD8, CPD12) and façade crowd 
density (CFD) to capture horizontal and vertical perceptions of crowding. Among these, CFD demonstrates strong 
and consistent correlations with all economic behavior indicators. Based on these findings, the 
“Sound–Human–Economic (SHE)” model is proposed and validated to conceptualize the interaction mechanisms 
among acoustic environment, social density, and consumer economic behavior. Sudden sound pressure (SSP), 
CFD, and Educational level emerge as robust predictors. This research contributes to theoretical advancement in 
urban acoustic-behavior studies and offers practical implications for evidence-based soundscape design, crowd 
management, and consumer-centered urban commercial planning.
1. Introduction
Urban commercial streets serve as critical spatial infrastructures that 
support economic development and contribute to the reconfiguration of 
urban identity [1]. Their researches is shaped by a range of factors, 
including infrastructure development [2], consumer spatial planning 
[3], adaptive reuse of historic buildings [4] and supportive policies.
However, existing research has predominantly concentrated on the 
physical and spatial characteristics of commercial streets, while 
comparatively little attention has been paid to the perceptual environ­
mental factors that influence consumer economic behavior. Defined as 
the decision-making patterns of individuals or groups regarding 
resource acquisition, consumption, and investment, economic behavior 
is highly sensitive to contextual environmental stimuli [5]. Non-visual 
environmental variables such as soundscape characteristics and crowd 
density may exert more pronounced effects on consumer cognition, 
emotional responses, and behavioral intentions, particularly in semi- 
enclosed or linear spatial typologies. Despite their potential impact, 
the complex interplay between these environmental dimensions and 
economic behavior in urban commercial streets remains underexplored 
and warrants systematic empirical investigation.
Previous studies have demonstrated that sound environments influ­
ence consumer decisions by shaping perception and affective states. For 
example, In tourism settings, integrating incentive-based soundscapes 
with tour routes has been demonstrated to enhance visitor experiences 
[6]. Moreover, soundscape quality indirectly influences visitors’ will­
ingness to return by affecting their satisfaction levels [7]. In indoor 
commercial environments, the impact of soundscape on consumer 
behavior in commercial settings has been well-documented. Studies 
indicate that noise levels in restaurants directly affect consumer

Methods
2.1. Study area
This study selects Dongguan Street in Yangzhou as the research site, 
primarily due to its vibrant commercial activity and its representative 
characteristics as an urban commercial street. As one of the core com­
mercial corridors in Yangzhou, Dongguan Street accommodates more 
than 200 businesses, featuring a highly diverse commercial profile that 
includes artisanal crafts, food and beverage services, fashion retail, and 
well-established brands rooted in intangible cultural heritage [27]. 
Furthermore, according to official reports from the Yangzhou Daily, 
Dongguan Street received approximately 434,000 visitors during the 
2025 May Day holiday alone, underscoring its exemplary status in terms 
of commercial vitality and pedestrian density. These attributes make it a 
suitable and ideal case for soundscape studies in urban commercial 
environments [28].
Based on a preliminary field investigation of dominant sound sources 
in the area [29], five typical experimental zones (S1–S5) were identified 
to assess acoustic and crowd-related interventions, as illustrated in Fig. 2
and Fig. 3.
2.2. Date collection
To collect data on soundscape and crowd density, on-site question­
naire surveys were conducted. The research was carried out from 
September to November 2023, during weekdays between 9:00 AM and 
11:00 AM and 2:00 PM and 4:00 PM, which are peak times for outdoor 
activities. A sound level meter (BSWA308) was used to measure physical 
sound indicators, and a wind heat detector (HDD32.3TC) was employed

To capture crowd density, a 360◦panoramic camera (Insta360 ONE X2) 
was used to record the entire experiment [31]. Each measurement point

During the experiment, the questionnaire survey and 360◦pano­
ramic camera recording were conducted simultaneously. Upon 
completing the questionnaire, a five-minute physical sound index 
measurement was immediately performed. To ensure randomization, 
passersby were selected every ten minutes for participation in the sur­
vey. Additionally, a brief training session was conducted prior to the 
survey to help participants understand the questionnaire items and 
soundscape attributes, thereby minimizing observation bias.
This study was conducted in strict accordance with established 
standards for long-term audiovisual environmental monitoring [33], 
protocols for soundscape data collection [32], and relevant ethical 
guidelines. During fieldwork, conspicuous signage was displayed to 
inform the public of the equipment in use and the experimental pro­
cedures, thereby ensuring compliance with local legislation and insti­
tutional ethical requirements.
All data were anonymized throughout the processing stage and 
securely deleted after the completion of the study. No identity recog­
nition algorithms were applied, and the data were not used for any 
commercial purposes. Microphones and cameras were employed 
exclusively for academic research, with a primary emphasis on the 
analysis of spatial crowd behavior.
Visual data were processed using a pixel-grid approach to quantify 
crowd density, with all facial features blurred to safeguard anonymity. 
Audio data were restricted to environmental acoustic indicators (e.g., 
LAeq), with speech and other potentially identifiable content systemati­
cally excluded.
At the start of each measurement session, the wind heat detector 
(HDD32.3TC) was used to measure ambient temperature and wind 
speed. The average ambient temperature was found to be 20.33 ◦C, with 
an average wind speed of 0.54 m/s. No significant differences were 
observed in the environmental conditions across the measurement 
sessions.
As summarized in Table 1, a total of 315 participants were included 
in the study. Regarding age distribution, most of the participants were 
between 18 and 30 years old. In terms of gender, the proportion of male 
and female participants was approximately equal, although females 
slightly outnumbered males by 13 %. Concerning educational 
background, a large proportion of the respondents (81.3 %) had at least 
a bachelor’s degree, indicating a highly educated sample. Regarding 
residential identity, the majority of respondents (79.7 %) were tourists

regarding travel budgets, the largest group of participants (31.7 %) re­
ported a budget range of 1,000 (CNY) to 3,000 (CNY).
2.3. Questionnaire design
The questionnaire covered three aspects of perception (Appendix 
A.1): demographic variables, soundscape perception [32], economic 
behavior. The first section gathered demographic data, including age, 
gender, education, identity, income, travel budget, and occupational 
[34]. The second section employed a 5-point Likert scale to assess par­
ticipants’ on-site soundscape perception [35], this included evaluations 
of dominant sound source loudness, event frequency (the number of 
occurrences in which an individual perceives a particular sound source), 
preference, and overall soundscape quality. Perceptions of the overall 
environment and sound expectations were also assessed. For instance, 
the following scale was used to evaluate subjective loudness [36]: 1-very 
soft, 2-soft, 3-neither soft nor loud, 4-loud, and 5-very loud. Soundscape 
perceived affective quality was evaluated using an 8-item semantic 
differential scale [23]: 1-pleasant, 2- annoying, 3-eventful, 4-uneventful,
