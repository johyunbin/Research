A B S T R A C T 
The Gigli di Nola is an Italian folk ceremony featuring a shoulder-borne procession celebrating the feast of Saint 
Paulinus. Despite its cultural significance as a UNESCO site, the soundscape and atmosphere of the festival has 
scarcely been explored. This study examines how the physical and spatial arrangement of Nola shapes the 
enactment of the festival’s soundscape, atmosphere, and behavior of its participants. It further investigates how 
the rhythmic qualities of the music are related to bearers’ spatial maneuvers and to non-bearers’ participation in 
the festival. Through the use of rhythmanalysis and a mixed method approach, the results reveal the festival to be 
deeply connected to Nola’s urban fabric and community life. The spatial arrangement of the historic streets with 
their nodes and boundaries, forge body–space relationships that indicates place, people, and music, are inter­
connected in the Gigli festival. The soundscape dominates the atmosphere of the festival, which in turn affects 
non-bearers and bearers’ behavior. These rhythmic qualities also guide the parading of the Gigli festival for its 
participants. We conclude by pointing to a lively view of culture to promote UNESCO’s management of cultural 
festivals. 
1. Introduction 
The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organiza­
tion’s (UNESCO) recognition of the intangible aspects of heritage led to 
the inclusion of diverse ceremonies in the Representative List of the 
Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (UNESCO, 2019a, 2019b). 
Amongst these are the “Feste delle grandi macchine a spalla” or celebra­
tions of big shoulder-borne processional structures. The shoulder-borne 
processional ceremonies (SBPC) comprise of a network of four Italian 
religious feasts: La Festa dei Gigli di Nola, Il Trasporto della Macchina di 
Santa Rosa, La Faradda dei Candelieri di Sassari, and La Varia di Palmi 
(UNESCO, 2019b). Overall, SBPC replicate ancient Mediterranean rit­
uals practiced in the central and southern regions of Italy. A common 
feature appertaining to the ceremonies is the presence of machines 
(Macchine) transported on men’s shoulders according to a specific rite, 
along the paths of the historical city centers where they take place. The 
Gigli festival is a popular Catholic feast held every year in Nola to 
commemorate the return of Saint Paulinus. The festival features eight 
obelisks which are called Gigli (lilies). 
A significant amount of the ceremonies included on the Represen­
tative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity are related to 
festivals, feasts, and religious celebrations. Even though one of UNES­
CO’s five domains of intangible heritage includes the “practices, repre­
sentations, expressions, knowledge, skills” and “instruments, objects, 
artefacts and cultural spaces” associated with intangible cultural heri­
tage (ICH), little is known about how ICH is maintained through the 
interplay of physical and non-physical qualities. Inspired by critical 
approaches to heritage studies (e.g., Duffy, 2014; Crociani-Windland, 
2011; Waterton and Watson, 2013; Smith, 2006; Smith and Campbell, 
2017), we argue that festivals are social activities whose affective dy­
namics need to be studied by considering both structure and fluidity. 
The soundscape of festivals such as shoulder-borne processional 
ceremonies are particularly relevant to a critical approach to heritage, as 
soundscapes affect people’s perception of the event, its atmosphere, and 
is strongly connected to the social and cultural heritage of the commu­
nity (Maffei et al., 2015). Communities enact a ‘reinvention of tradition’ 
that is filtered through the needs of the present, where tangible and 
intangible properties of cultural heritage are intertwined. The perfor­
mative quality of festivals, their soundscape, and atmosphere are valu­
able frames through which to promote heritage-in-the-making rather 
than heritage as an unchanging tradition (Wulf, 2017). Community 
engagement in heritage should consider local narratives of tradition and

--- METHODS/RESULTS EXCERPT ---
Methodology 
Fieldwork took place through multiple visits to Nola spreading from 
2011 to 2019 and involved innumerable meetings with informants from 
the city. Interviews and sound recordings were done in 2011. Further 
fieldwork in 2019 involved attending the festival and registering its 
different components through photographs and field notes, as well as 
talking to participants from Nola directly involved in the festival. 
Participants could be categorized as bearers and non-bearers. The 
bearers were usually residents of Nola whose families are part of the 
local guilds (e.g., greengrocers, butchers, innkeepers, bakers, boat 
builders, shoemakers, blacksmiths, and tailors) for which the obelisks 
are created. Bearers are the groups of men who follow the religious 
tradition of carrying the Gigli and making them dance during the day of 
the parade. The non-bearers are not directly involved in carrying the 
obelisks or overseeing the Gigli parade. Non-bearers were either resi­
dents of Nola or tourists from other parts of Italy or abroad. 
After receiving consent, 120 participants were interviewed briefly on 
the day of the ceremony in 2011. It was not feasible to conduct in-depth 
interviews on the day of the festival due to the loudness of the streets, 
lack of private places to invite participants to sit and answer questions, 
and participants’ desire to continue attending the festival. The in­
terviews were semi-structured and later analyzed through content 
analysis (Krippendorff, 2019). A purposive sampling approach was used 
to collect the interview data with non-bearers. 
Participants were asked about their overall perception of the festival 
in terms of their satisfaction with it and if it was something they enjoyed 
or disliked. Participants were also asked about their motivation (e.g., 
curiosity, passion, tradition, or habit) for participating in the ceremony, 
their perception of the different senses (e.g., hearing, sight and smell) 
affecting the atmosphere, and their experience of the festival and 
procession. 
Sound was recorded to understand how music and related rhythms 
were associated with bearers’ movements during the procession. Sound 
was measured in two situations: close to the Macchine (where musicians 
were placed) and along the pathways (where the public was gathered) 
during different phases of the parade to account for the circulation of the 
procession in Nola’s streets. 
In the days prior to the ceremony in 2011, the Gigli di Nola organizing 
committee was informed of the research project and granted consent to 
take audio binaural sound and video recordings. The audio recordings 
were made by employing binaural headphones (Sennheiser HDC 451) 
connected to a portable recorder (M-Audio Microtrack 24/96). Before 
recording, a pure tone of 94 dB at 1 kHz was taken as reference and used 
in post-processing to calibrate all in-situ recordings. The files were 
elaborated using the software ‘dB Sonic’. This software makes it possible 
to analyze acoustic descriptors, to apply specific filters to the original 
audio recordings to isolate the sounds in a specific frequency range, and 
to recognize the rhythmic scan. For example, the bass drum was done by 
applying a bass pass filter with a lower (120 Hz) and higher cutoff fre­
quency (150 Hz). Fig. 3–6 show the spectrograms of the audio re­
cordings. They are a graphical representation of the sound pressure 
levels over time in frequency. 
The use of spectrograms allowed us to identify the specific beats that 
guided the movement of the cradles. In order to identify the beats per 
minute (bpm) and characterize the rhythm and adaptation of the cra­
dles, files of length equal to 1 min or fractions were considered. Through 
listening to the files filtered at the frequencies of the bass drum, a cor­
respondence was found between the ’peaks’ of the history of the tem­
poral trend of the sound pressure level and the beats which followed one 
another at regular intervals. Therefore, it was possible to identify the 
beats corresponding to the 2/4 rhythmic scan both in its musical 
composition of two quarter notes (minimum value = 1/4) and of a 
quarter note and two eighth notes (quaver value = 1/8) or four eighth 
notes. As shown in Fig. 3–7, the different recordings taken as a sample 
for analysis show approximately 108 bpm, which in terms of musical 
trend correspond to an Andante tempo. 
Photographs were taken by researchers in 2019 to document key 
features of the parade and its atmosphere. Fig. 1 shows the Gigli before 
(a-d) and after (e-h) the parade. Photograph ‘a’ shows the assembly of 
the central pole of the Giglio (called borda) in Corso Tommaso Vitale. It is 
resting on the ground, ready with ropes to be raised by the people in 
Nola (both bearers and non-bearers). The borda is used to support the 
Giglio. Photograph ‘b’ shows the edge of the Giglio, which once hoisted, 
is placed on the balconies of the buildings for people to build the Giglio 
around it. Photographs ‘c’ and ‘d’ are in Vicolo del Duomo and depict the 
wooden structure of the Giglio as seen from the rear front in all its ver­
tical development. It also details of the arrangement of the eight fixed 
bars that are used to lift the Giglio on men’s shoulders. 
Photograph ‘e-h’ are in Piazza Duomo. The first photo shows the 
morning of the Festival as the Gigli machines gather in the square to 
receive the blessing of the bishop of Nola. This is followed by photo­
graphs ‘f’ and ‘g’ also taken during the morning of the festival. They 
show the procession of the silver bust of Saint Paulinus for the blessing of 
the Gigli by the bishop. This happens when the eight Gigli and the boat 
arrive at the piazza. Finally, photograph ‘h’ shows Piazza Duomo in the 
afternoon of the festival when the Gigli are waiting in their respective 
positions to start the afternoon procession through the streets of Nola.
