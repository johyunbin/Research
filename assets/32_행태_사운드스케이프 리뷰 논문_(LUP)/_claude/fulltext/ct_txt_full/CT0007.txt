Do soundscape perceptions vary over length of stay within urban parks?

A B S T R A C T 
Outdoor recreational experiences of soundscapes in urban parks are not instantaneous but continuous over time, 
yet most existing studies evaluate soundscapes in one place or one environment at a single moment in time. In 
this study, outdoor users’ perceptions of soundscapes during a visit in varying environments within urban parks 
in Xi’an, China were explored. The results from N = 2034 respondents showed that (1) the level of overall 
perceived loudness peaked between 30 min and 1 h of a visit and then the perception of loudness lessened with 
increased length of stay. (2) Perceived occurrences of both natural and mechanical sounds showed a significant 
increment within 1 h, however, the natural sounds remained relatively stable after the hour, while the me­
chanical sounds rose significantly to a peak between 1 and 3 h (3) Visual sources of birds were not detected until 
after 3 h of the visit, while visual sources of broadcast music were tended to be detected within 1 h (4) Overall 
perceived loudness only varied over time in blue, open green and dense green space; perceived occurrences of 
birds only varied over time in grey and partly open/dense green space; perceived occurrences of broadcast music 
only varied over time in grey space, partly open/dense green and dense green space; perceived occurrences of 
bicycle riding only varied over time in open green and dense green space. The implications of these findings 
include the management of urban parks to improve the recreational experience of visitors, and considerations for 
future research on the soundscape in urban parks. 
Management implications 
Park managers may be able to use the finding presented in this study 
to: 
1) Ensure that visitors can fully experience the biodiversity of urban 
parks, considering their length of stay. 
2) Improve the soundscape of different environments in the park, 
especially in grey and blue spaces. 
3) Increase the presence of natural sounds and background music and 
reduce traffic noise in urban parks for a better recreational experi­
ence for visitors. 
1. Introduction 
In the context of rapid urbanization, urban parks have been regarded as 
essential elements of sustainable and healthy cities for enhancing 
ecological benefits and improving citizens’ quality of life from social and 
psychological aspects (Cheng et al., 2021; Spotswood et al., 2021; Zhang 
et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Marconi et al., 2022). Compared with other 
built environments, urban parks generally contain a prominent feature of 
acoustic environments that these other built environments do not, which is 
promoting visitors’ feeling of tranquility and peace during their outdoor 
recreation experiences (Liu et al., 2018; Rice et al., 2022; Rodrigues, 2018; 
Watts, 2017). For the visitors in the park, the experience of tranquility is 
the main reason for their visit (Han, 2019; Liu et al., 2018; Pheasant et al., 
2010; Vasiljevi´c et al., 2023). Although there are possibly other purposes 
of visits, such as enjoying the scenery, participating in physical activities, 
engaging in social interactions, and seeking factors like reduced traffic, 
enhanced safety, and decreased pollution. These various factors contribute 
to the overall park experience, with the soundscape playing a significant 
role (Jeon & Jo, 2020; Jo & Jeon, 2020). That is to say, the positive 
experience of the soundscape contributes positively to the overall satis­
faction gained from urban parks (Liu et al., 2018, 2019b). Therefore, it is 
necessary to identify visitor perceptions of urban park soundscapes, which 
will help park managers create a pleasant soundscape in the park and 
enhance visitors’ satisfaction with their recreation experience (Mazaris 
et al., 2009; Rice et al., 2022).

Soundscape perception is an umbrella term. There are many de­
scriptors to measure soundscape perception. These descriptors encom­
pass factors such as preferences and perceived loudness of both the 
overall soundscape and individual sounds, as well as considerations 
regarding potential annoyance caused by sound levels, the restorative 
qualities of the overall soundscape or individual sounds, the appropri­
ateness of sounds within the context, and the perceptual location of 
different individual sounds (Fields, 1993; Yang & Kang, 2005; Yu & 
Kang, 2008; Szeremeta & Zannin, 2009; Yu & Kang, 2010; Tse et al., 
2012; Meng & Kang, 2013; Jeon et al., 2014; Aletta et al., 2016; Hong & 
Jeon, 2017; Rey Gozalo and Barrig´on Morillas, 2017; Aletta et al., 2018; 
Sun et al., 2018; Liu, Wang, et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2021c; Ma et al., 
2022). There are also different forms to measure the above descriptors. 
Thereinto,

of soundscape (Axelsson et al., 2010; Cain et al., 2013; Aletta et al., 
2016; Dunbavin, 2018; Hong, Wang, et al., 2020; Jo & Jeon, 2021; Hong 
et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021). 
In real time, a person’s soundscape experience is not instantaneous, 
but continuous (Li & Kang, 2019; Liu, Kang, Luo, Behm, et al., 2013). 
However, previous studies have mainly focused on the instantaneous 
soundscape perception in urban parks. For example, immediate per­
ceptions of respondents with a variety of records and photos as 
audio-visual stimuli were explored in the laboratory through the ques­
tionnaire survey (Hong, Lam, et al., 2020). Visitors’ instantaneous per­
ceptions of soundscape within urban parks were surveyed on site (Liu 
et al., 2019). So far no studies have proven that people’s transient 
soundscape perceptions can represent their full soundscape experience 
during their time in the park. Therefore, there is a need to investigate the 
effect of perception duration, i.e. the length of time spent in the park, on 
the perception of the soundscape in the urban park. 
Past studies have shown that people’s perception of soundscapes in 
different contexts is related to perceptual duration. For example, the 
amount of time that residents spend out-of-doors influenced their per­
ceptions of noise annoyance in residential areas (Fields, 1993), 
perceptual duration influenced the evaluation of acoustic comfort of 
shopping malls (Meng & Kang, 2013), length of visiting time contributed 
to different soundscape experiences in urban green spaces (Liu et al., 
2018), and different time durations resulted in different soundscape 
cognitive persistence (Hong et al., 2019). Moreover, several physiolog­
ical indicators (such as heart rate, amplitude of the R-wave, heart rate 
variability, electroencephalography beta reactivity, eye blink frequency, 
respiratory frequency, respiratory depth, skin conductance level, and 
skin temperature) along with the soundscape perception also proved to 
change over time (Li & Kang, 2019). However, the above studies 
explored the effects of different scales of duration in different contexts 
(residential area, commercial area, urban green area, and laboratory 
virtual setting), their inconsistent findings of soundscape perception 
variation are difficult to apply directly to the context of urban park. 
Thus, this study will explore the changes in visitors’ soundscape per­
ceptions in urban parks over their length of stay, and we hypothesize 
that in the context of urban parks, people’s perception of the soundscape 
is not static, but changes with the length of their stay (hypothesis 1). 
In addition, the urban park is made up of various types of environ­
ments (Carrus et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2019b). Based on the land cover 
and use types, urban parks can be divided as grey space (90–100% 
abiotic area with little greenery), blue space (90–100% water with some 
greenery), and green space (Grilo et al., 2020; Suligowski et al., 2021; 
Mach´aˇc et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022). Based on vegetation patterns and 
plant configurations, the green spaces can be further divided into 
different categories (Du et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2020). Among 
different environments in urban settings, green spaces are popular and 
felt to be restorative. This phenomenon can be elucidated by the bio­
philia hypothesis, which posits that humans have an innate inclination 
to connect with other forms of life (Kellert & Wilson, 1993; el-Baghdadi 
& Desha, 2017; Huang et al., 2020). Also, blue spaces were also proved 
to be favored, explained by an evolutionary perspective - hydrophilia is 
an innate attribute of humans (McDougall et al., 2021; White et al., 
2020). The environmental component would influence visitors’ 
perception (Aletta et al., 2016; Huang et al., 2020; Sadeghi & Haghighat 
Bin, 2023; Zhao et al., 2021), and visitors’ perception of the landscape 
would in turn affect soundscape perception (Chen, Zhu, et al., 2022; 
Hong & Jeon, 2014; Li, Zhai, & Zhu, 2022; Liu et al., 2014). Therefore, 
we hypothesize that people’s perception of the soundscape in the park 
will vary depending on the type of environment they are in (hypothesis 
2). If both hypotheses 1 and 2 hold, the degree of change in visitors’ 
soundscape perception over visit time may vary by environment type 
(hypothesis 3). 
Therefore, the three hypotheses of this study were: 
1) Visitors’ soundscape perceptions in the urban park will be influenced 
by their length of stay. 
2) Visitors’ soundscape perceptions in the urban park will be influenced 
by environment types. 
3) The degree of influence of visitors’ length of stay on soundscape 
perception will be different in different environments. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Study area 
All surveys were conducted in Xi’an, China, known as an interna­
tional metropolis with historical and cultural characteristics. In 2019, 
the permanent resident population was 10,203,500, while the urban 
population was 7,612,800, which equates to a 74.61% urbanization 
rate. In addition, it is the starting point of the Silk Road, and was deemed 
a “World Historic City” assessed by UNESCO, attracting many tourists 
every year. As a national park city, Xi’an has sixteen existing parks. Out 
of these six urban parks were selected based on location, freedom of 
access, number of visitors, and the shape of the site, all representing 
typical Xi’an parks (Fig. 1). The study sites were selected within these six 
parks. 
To make the sites fully representative of the urban park character­
istics, the study sites were selected based on five environment types: 
grey space (GrS, 90–100% abiotic area with little greenery), blue space 
(BS, 90–100% water with some greenery), open green space (OG, <10% 
canopy cover of trees/shrubs), partly open/dense green space (POG, 
30%-70% canopy cover of trees/shrubs), and dense green space (DG, 
>70% canopy cover of trees/shrubs) (Gao et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2019a; 
Gao, Liang, et al., 2019). To gather enough questionnaires in the field, 
two typical sites for each of the five types of urban park environments 
were identified as study sites, for a total of 10 sites (Fig. 1). All of the 
sites are similar in size of approximately 2 hectares, freely accessible and 
having many visitors. 
Prior to conducting the survey, the average sound level of each site 
was sampled for 15 min using a Bruel and Kjaer 2250 class 1 sound level 
meter (SLM; 1-s logging period). In each site, the SLM was set near the 
paths of visitors that were representative of the context. In addition, the 
temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed of the parks were 
measured using Kestrel 5500 weather stations throughout the whole 
survey. Taken together, these measurements revealed an average sound 
level (59dBA), temperature (19 ◦C), humidity (60%) and wind speed 
(0.62 m/s) across all study sites (Appendix - Table 6). 
2.2. Questionnaire survey construction 
The questionnaire consisted of two sections with a total of 23 ques­
tions (Appendix - Fig. 7). The first section was to collect basic (e.g., 
social, demographical, and behavioral) information of participants and 
the length of time they stayed in the site. In this section, the basic in­
formation questions (age, gender, companion, educational background,

occupation, monthly income, dwelling place, residential status, distance 
to the park, visit frequency and potential recreational activities) and 
their option settings were referenced from previous literature on 
soundscape perceptions (Fields, 1993; Liu et al., 2013a, 2018, 2019a; 
Tse et al., 2012; Yu and Kang, 2008, 2010). The length-of-stay option 
setting was referenced from the soundscape literature and validated 
through pilot test 1. Specifically, the pilot test 1 was conducted to 
examine how long visitors typically spend within a site while visiting an 
urban park. It was found that approximately 30% of respondents spend 
less than half an hour in the site of the park, close to 35% of them stayed 
more than half an hour but less than an hour, nearly 30% of respondents 
stayed in the site of the park around 1 and 3 h, while less than 10% of 
respondents would stay for longer than 3 h. Therefore, four options were 
set in terms of length of stay in the questionnaire: (1) less than 30 min, 
(2) 30 min to 1 h, (3) 1 h to 3 h, and (4) more than 3 h. These time frames 
are similar to other findings of length of visits in urban parks (Liu et al., 
2013a, 2019a). 
The second section of questionnaire was to collect soundscape per­
ceptions (the perception of the overall soundscape and individual 
sounds). As the study only investigated the perception of representative 
sounds in the park, pilot test 2 was conducted to select sounds that were 
common in the park and were included in each study site. Specifically, 
pilot test 2 invited 20 landscape and ecology experts with normal 
hearing abilities (ability to hear at and below 25 dB) to conduct the 
soundwalk around the study sites and report the existing sounds in each 
site. 8 individual sounds were found as common in ten sites: natural 
sounds (birds and leaves rustling), mechanical sounds (broadcast music 
and bicycle riding), human sounds (surrounding speech and footsteps), 
and traffic noises (automobile and motorbike). Note that bird’s song and 
leaves rustling are coming from the creatures themselves, not man-made 
sounds. Broadcast music refers to soft and soothing music that is 
broadcasted by mounted speakers within the park. Besides, although the 
motorbike sound was present in all sites, it appeared very infrequently in 
open green spaces. Considering the research potential of the motorbike 
sound as a typical traffic noise, the perception of the sound was still 
investigated in the GrS, BS, POG, and DG.

All the contents of the questionnaire were presented electronically 
through an app named “Wen Juan Xing.” 
2.3. Questionnaire survey administration 
The field survey was conducted over a total of 20 days from 
September to October in 2019. To reduce the deviation caused by the 
selection of a particular time and investigate site users more compre­
hensively, each study site was surveyed twice over different days 
(Table 2). Every survey lasted for 8 h, from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. All 
respondents were randomly selected among visitors of the urban parks. 
Respondents in each selected site were first informed about the survey’s 
objectives and the answering procedure without any hint placed on 
positive sounds, negative sounds or noise pollution. Then, they were 
provided an explanation that their answers would be anonymous. Those 
who consented to participate were given the tablet with the question­
naire and invited to complete it separately to avoid other respondents’ 
interference. The participants were asked to respond about perceptions 
based on their continuous experiences in the study site within the length 
of time selected in the questionnaire.

In total, N = 2068 respondents answered the survey. Excluding 
invalid questionnaires that were not fully answered, 2034 question­

questionnaires in each type of environment was average (387 in GrS, 
442 in BS, 339 in OG, 471 in POG, 395 in DG). The personal basic in­
formation of respondents across five environment types were presented 
in Appendix Table 7. The composition of participants across these five 
environment types was found to be similar.

hypotheses, all 11 basic information items (age, gender, etc.) were 
included as covariates in GLMs. Initial model testing suggested that the 
inclusion of all 11 items was ideal for most of the hypothesized models

For hypotheses 1 & 2, all 35 soundscape perceptions were treated 
sequentially as dependent variables (Fig. 2), with the independent var­
iable being altered consistent with the hypothesis (length of stay for 
hypothesis 1 and type of environment for hypothesis 2). Tests of model 
effects and pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means were 
performed within the GLMs for hypotheses 1 & 2. 
For hypothesis 1, these analyses were to find out whether length of 
stay had a significant effect on soundscape perceptions (significant with 
p < 0.05) and in which interval of time soundscape perceptions changed 
significantly (significant with p < 0.05). For hypothesis 2, these analyses 
were to find out whether environment type had a significant influence 
on soundscape perceptions (significant with p < 0.05) and among which 
environment types that soundscape perceptions changed significantly 
(significant with p < 0.05). 
For hypothesis 3, the effect of length of stay on soundscape percep­
tion needs to be explored under different environment types. The in­
dependent variable was length of stay, and the dependent variable was 
soundscape perception - whichever of the 35 Y(i) were included will be 
jointly determined by the results of hypotheses 1 and 2. This means that 
Y(i), which was influenced by both length of stay and environment type, 
was the dependent variable in hypothesis 3. After identifying the 
dependent variables, all GLMs for these dependent variables performed 
tests of model effects to verify extent of influence of the length of stay on 
soundscape perceptions in different environments. All statistical ana­
lyses were carried out in SPSS 25.0. 
3. Results 
3.1. Overview of respondents’ length of stay in all types of environments 
In general, 26.6% of respondents (N = 542) in this study had spent 
less than half an hour within the park sites, 36.2% of respondents (N =
737) had spent 30 min to 1 h, 29.7% of respondents (N = 604) for 1 to 3 
h, and 7.4% of respondents (N = 151) for more than 3 h. The overall 
standard deviation for various “length of stay” options was 0.91, 
calculated from 2034 individual values. Furthermore, the standard de­
viations observed in different environment types closely approximated 
this value, signifying a consistent distribution of “length of stay” across 
all five environment types (Fig. 3). 
3.2. The variation of soundscape perceptions over length of stay in urban 
parks 
It was originally hypothesized that length of time would affect per­
ceptions of soundscape. Our analysis of 35 different ratings showed that 
only 7 ratings were related to length of time but in a consistent manner 
(Table 3). Specifically, as for the perception of the overall soundscape, 
Overall Perceived Loudness was the only dependent variable influenced 
by the length of stay (p = 0.025), while Overall Soundscape Preference 
and Overall Soundscape Annoyance did not change over time. As for the 
perception of individual sounds, the effect of the length of stay was 
mainly reflected by natural sounds and mechanical sounds, rather than 
human sounds and traffic noises. Regarding natural and mechanical 
sounds, specifically, Perceived Occurrences dimension was the most 
affected, in which birds (p = 0.008), leaves rustling (p = 0.000), 
broadcast music (p = 0.000) and bicycle riding (p = 0.002) were 
significantly influenced by the length of stay. Besides, Visual Sources of 
birds (p = 0.000) and broadcast music (p = 0.026) was also significantly 
affected by the length of stay. However, Preference and Perceived 
Loudness of natural sounds and mechanical sounds were not affected at 
all (Table 3). 
As far as the dependent variables which were affected by the length 
of stay, their variation over time was examined by pairwise comparison 
in each GLM (Fig. 4). The details then were presented from different 
dimensions of perceptions: 
1) Overall Perceived Loudness: The value of OPL significantly peaked 
between 30 minutes and 1 hour, and then showed significant lower 
values after 3 h.

3.3. The effect of environment types on visitors’ soundscape perceptions in 
urban parks 
It was originally hypothesized that environment types would affect 
overall perceptions of soundscape. Our analysis of 3 different ratings 
showed that all ratings were related to environment types. Specifically, 
respondents in partly open/dense green space (POG) showed the highest 
preference towards the soundscape and considered the soundscape as 
the quietest and the least annoying, while respondents in blue space 
(BS), although the soundscape was regarded as not as quiet and calm as 
its counterpart in POG, showed equal preference for BS with POG. The 
soundscape in grey space (GrS), however, was perceived as the loudest 
and most annoying, which was the least popular one in terms of re­
spondents’ preference (Fig. 5). 
It was originally hypothesized that environment types would affect 
perceptions of individual sounds. Our analysis of 32 different ratings 
showed that 21 ratings were related to environment types but in a 
consistent manner (Table 4). Firstly, in terms of natural sounds, Pref­
erence (PR) for leaves rustling was significantly different among five 
environments (p = 0.018) and performed stronger in green spaces (OG, 
POG and DG). Perceived Occurrences (PO) and Perceived Loudness (PL) 
of birds were significantly different among five environments (both p =
0.000), and birdsong was difficult to be perceived in grey space (GrS). 
Visual Sources (VS) of leaves rustling were significantly different among 
five environments (p = 0.000) and they were difficult to be detected in 
open green space (Table 4). Secondly, in terms of mechanical sounds, PR 
for broadcast music and bicycle riding were significantly different 
among five environments (p = 0.000 and p = 0.030 respectively) and 
performed the lowest in grey space (GrS), although mechanical sounds 
(based on estimated marginal means of PO and PL of broadcast music 
and bicycle riding) in GrS were not perceived to be more obvious than 
their counterparts in other spaces (Table 4). Thirdly, in terms of human 
sounds, PR for them were always the same regardless of environment 
type, while human sounds (based on estimated marginal means of PO 
and PL of surrounding speech and footsteps, and VS of surrounding 
speech) were perceived more often in blue and green spaces (Table 4).

ment type were converted to histograms (Appendix - Fig. 8), which 
clearly showed the perceptual ranking of the eight sounds under each 
environment type separately. It was found that respondents had 
different perceptions of different sounds. On the one hand, regardless of 
environment type, preferences for birds, broadcast music, and leaves 
rustling were always the highest; preference for traffic noises (automo­
biles and motorbikes) were the lowest; preference for human sounds 
(surrounding speech and footsteps) generally remained at the medium 
level; Perceived Occurrences (PO) and Perceived Loudness (PL) of sur­
rounding speech were always the highest; and Visual Sources (VS) of 
birds and broadcast music were always difficult to detect, while VS of 
human sounds (surrounding speech and footsteps) were always easily 
recognized. On the other hand, traffic noises (based on estimated mar­
ginal means of PO and PL of automobiles and motorbikes) were domi­
nating in grey space and open green space, whereas natural sounds 
(based on estimated marginal means of PO, PL, and VS of birds) became 
more obvious than traffic noises in partly open/dense green space and 
dense green space. 
Overall, the soundscape in blue space and partly open/dense green 
space was the most preferred, while the opposite was true in grey space. 
People’s preference and perception of sounds were significantly 
different under certain types of environments, and the characteristics of 
the soundscape that people perceived were also different under different 
environment types. However, no matter what type of environment, re­
spondents consistently disliked traffic noises and preferred natural 
sounds and broadcast music. Human sounds were always very common 
in the urban parks and respondents tended to have a neutral regard for 
them. 
3.4. The degree of influence of visitors’ length of stay on soundscape 
perception in different environments 
Taking into account the variations in soundscape perceptions over 
time and the differences observed among the five environment types, 
this study proceeded to examine the variations in these variables, 
including overall soundscape preference, perceived occurrences of 
birds, broadcast music, and bicycle riding, in relation to the length of 
stay within each of the five environment types. 
It was originally hypothesized that the degree of influence of visitors’ 
length of stay on soundscape perception would be different in different 
environments. The results confirmed that this hypothesis is valid. Spe­
cifically, the impact of length of time on Overall Perceived Loudness 
only appeared in BS (p = 0.011), OG (p = 0.024), and DG (p = 0.011) 
(Fig. 6). Perceived Occurrences of birds only significantly changed over 
visiting length of time in GrS (p = 0.028) and POG (p = 0.007) (Fig. 6). 
Perceived Occurrences of broadcast music only significantly changed 
over the visiting time period in GrS (p = 0.035), POG (p = 0.012), and 
DG (p = 0.015) (Fig. 6). Perceived Occurrences of bicycle riding would 
only significantly vary over the visiting length of time in OG (p = 0.018) 
and DG (p = 0.011) (Fig. 6). 
Overall, in terms of hypothesis 1, the length of stay in the park does 
exert an influence on various aspects of soundscape perception, 
although this effect is not consistently observed across all dimensions of 
soundscape perception (Table 5). In terms of hypothesis 2, the type of 
environment does have some impact on soundscape perceptions, but it 
does not significantly alter people’s preferences for human sounds 
(Table 5). In terms of hypothesis 3, the influence of length of stay on 
sound perceptions varies across different environment types, with the 
effect of length of stay on sound perceptions appearing relatively weaker 
in blue space (Table 5). 
4. Discussion 
4.1. Visitors’ soundscape perceptions in the urban park are influenced by 
their length of stay 
This study found that some of soundscape perceptions were dynamic 
over time during a tour in urban parks. Hypothesis 1 is valid. As for 
overall soundscape perception, the perceived loudness was the only one 
linked to the time of the stay. Seemingly people perceived the park to be 
noisier and louder after 30-60 min of being within the park than when 
entering the park. However, after spending 1 h within the park, they 
seemed to be rather neutral in perceiving loudness. Perhaps visitors start 
to become accustomed to the noise after 30-60 min and then begin to 
pay attention to other things, which needs to be further explored in 
future studies combined with neurological science to study the psy­
chological factors at play. As for the perception of individual sounds, the 
change in perception often occurred with natural sounds and mechani­
cal sounds rather than human sounds and traffic noises. Specifically, 
people were paying higher attention to bird songs and leaves rustling as 
well as broadcast music and bicycle sounds from the time of entry to the 
park to 1 h’s stay. After 1 h within the parks, the perception of natural 
sounds seemed to stabilize for the rest of the stay while mechanical 
sounds were heard a greater frequency up to 3 h and after that signifi­
cantly less. Interestingly, people observed an increase in bird songs 
within the first hour, but they did not visually perceive them until 3 h 
later. Therefore, for park managers, when managing the site with high 
ecological value in mind, it is recommended to provide the necessary 
facilities (enough space, comfortable seats, convenient trash cans and 
toilets) ensuring visitors can remain in the site for a long time to truly 
experience the species richness (e.g. birds) of the site (Rushing et al., 
2019; Vasiljevi´c et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022).

environment types

different in five environment types of the urban park. Hypothesis 2 is 
valid. As for grey space, the respondents regarded this space as the most 
annoying and least preferred among five types of environments in urban 
parks. It might be related to the results in this study, as birds’ songs were

could not tolerate hearing mechanical sounds in the grey space the most. 
Therefore, measures for the improvement of the acoustic environment in 
grey areas should be part of the park management. For example, to 
attract more organisms and increase natural sounds by applying the eco- 
permeable pavement (which are made of either a porous material that 
enables stormwater to flow through it or nonporous blocks spaced so 
that water can flow between the gaps) and increasing local plants to the 
site (Cerra & Crain, 2016; Hong, Ong, et al., 2020; Pr¨obstl-Haider et al., 
2023). To reduce the perceived level of traffic noises in the site by 
reasonably selecting the location to keep the grey area away from the 
traffic area (Ow & Ghosh, 2017). 
As for blue space, interestingly, people here did not regard the 
soundscape as the quietest but still considered the soundscape as the 
most preferred one. It might be explained by an evolutionary perspective 
- hydrophilia is an innate attribute of humans (Foley & Kistemann, 2015; 
McDougall et al., 2021). In other words, people naturally like spaces 
related to water. Therefore, the park managements about blue space 
could be to add more elements that interact with water (Di et al., 2022). 
For instance, diversifying the forms and materials of revetment—typi­
cally made of stone, although other successful materials include 
concrete-mat, willow plantings, gabions, and various alternatives—can 
generate a range of water-to-shore sound effects (Jing et al., 2020), 
playing broadcast music related to water (based on the history of China, 
flowing water often reminds modern people of the feeling of being in 
nature) (Corney & Neave, 2019; Lu et al., 2022), providing various types 
of revetment space and other convenient facilities to allow visitors to 
stay closer with the blue space (de Vries et al., 2016; Hu & Zhao, 2022). 
As for partly open/dense green space, people perceived its sound­
scape as the quietest and most favorite. Participants within this study 
easily perceived natural sounds, and thus might feel more connected 
with nature in this type of space and become more relaxed from their 
daily routine (Uebel et al., 2021). Several studies have proved that partly 
open/dense green space is the most preferred space from a visual 
perspective or overall-experience perspective (Gao et al., 2019a; Gao 
et al., 2019b; Qiu et al., 2021; Xiang et al., 2021; Sadeghi & Haghighat 
Bin, 2023), and our results could further support this finding from the 
auditory perspective. 
Despite soundscape perception differences in the different environ­
ments above, the results showed that no matter what type of environ­
ment, people always disliked traffic noises and liked the sounds of birds, 
broadcast music and leaves rustling. These findings are consistent with 
the biophilia hypothesis which explains that people are naturally close 
to and love natural creatures and human beings’ innate musicality 
(Zatorre, 2018). Therefore, urban parks, as outdoor recreation areas 
where residents can experience the relaxing and natural environment 
away from the pressures of daily life, should be managed to increase the 
presence of natural sounds and background music, and reduce the 
interference of traffic noise (Jia et al., 2020). For example, establishing a 
rich and suitable habitat to attract birds and diversify the birdsong (Zhu 
et al., 2020); playing light music which is related to the natural envi­
ronment with soothing tunes to create a relaxed and pleasant environ­
ment for people while not interfering with their daily activities (Van 
Renterghem et al., 2020); setting terrain barriers, plant barriers, or even 
sound barriers at the boundaries of urban parks to reduce the spread of 
traffic sounds as much as possible (Ferrini et al., 2020); and applying 
quiet pavement and reducing speed limits outside the park (Rice et al., 
2022).

open green spaces, and dense green spaces. Results revealed that the 
soundscape in grey spaces was perceived as the loudest while the 
soundscape in partly open/dense green spaces was perceived as the 
quietest (Jin et al., 2020). 
For the perception of birds, broadcast music, and bicycle over 
visiting periods of time, they only showed variation in several kinds of 
environments. Specifically, perceived occurrences of birds only varied

4.4. Limitation and future research 
Firstly, in this on-site survey, the overall sound level, type of indi­
vidual sounds, the environmental condition, and the allocation of the 
respondents in each type of space were generally the same, whereas the 
specific acoustical features of each individual sound (occurrences and 
loudness) could not be easily controlled. This is a conflict that field 
experiments cannot avoid. However, since this study was exploring the 
effect of the time visitors spend at the site on the perception of the 
soundscape, the experiment was required to be realized in the actual 
site. We have randomly selected two sample sites for each environment 
type and collected a balanced number of questionnaires at each sample 
site to ensure the fairness of the total sample. Secondly, the categories 
presented within the two main independent variables (length of stay and 
environment types) of this study were limited. For length of stay, the 
study presented four options of it through questionnaires. For type of 
environment, the study divided the park environment into five cate­
gories. However, this does not affect the validity of the study. Because 
the number of questionnaires corresponding to each type of length of 
time and the number of questionnaires corresponding to each type of 
environment were sufficient to carry out statistical analyses. Thirdly, the 
experiment’s reliance on 15 min of pre-survey sound level measure­
ments may not entirely capture the correlation between actual loudness 
and perceived loudness. However, this limitation does not have 
adversely affected the accuracy of our experiment’s findings. This is 
primarily due to the fact that the sound levels did not exhibit significant 
variations across the various environment types. Consequently, the re­
ported perceptions can be attributed more to the impact of length of stay 
rather than being a result of a specific momentary artifact. Fourthly, in 
hypotheses 1 and 2, it’s noteworthy that only a subset of the various 
soundscape perceptions demonstrates significant influence in relation to 
the length of stay or environment types. However, it is essential to 
emphasize that this study primarily serves as an exploratory investiga­
tion into the influence of length of stay and environment types. Its core 
objective is to shed light on the impact of length of stay on soundscape 
experiences. The presence of any significant test results, related to the 
length of stay and environment types, signifies the importance of 
continued attention and exploration in forthcoming research and design 
endeavors. 
In future field experiments, broadening the scope would provide 
more flexibility in selecting independent variables, enabling a more 
comprehensive exploration of the precise influence of the duration of 
time spent on soundscape perception. This enhancement could be 
further bolstered by integrating real-time objective sound level moni­
toring. And research can also be conducted in the laboratory to further 
confirm the results by controlling variables. To facilitate future parks’ 
soundscape planning and design in detail, future studies could further 
investigate the relationship among the species of birds in the site, peo­
ple’s knowledge of birds, and the perceived visual sources of birds. 
5. Conclusions 
This study focuses on the variations of the on-site users’ perceptions 
of the soundscape during a visit in five types of environments in the 
urban park. The survey was conducted across ten sampling sites from 
typical urban parks in Xi’an China, and 2034 effective questionnaires 
were collected. The results implicated that: 
1) For park management that focus on high ecological values, it is 
recommended that necessary facilities (such as adequate space, 
comfortable seating, convenient trash cans and toilets) be provided 
to ensure that visitors can stay long enough to truly experience the 
richness of the species (e.g., birds). 
2) The soundscape of the grey space in the park needs to be improved. 
For example, applying ecological permeable pavement and adding 
local plants to attract more creatures may increase natural sounds, 
and siting the grey area away from the traffic area may reduce the 
perceived traffic noise on the site. 
3) The blue space in the urban park can add more elements that allow 
visitors to interact with water. Such as enriching the form and ma­
terial of revetment to create various sounds of water hitting the 
shore, playing water-related broadcasting music, providing various
