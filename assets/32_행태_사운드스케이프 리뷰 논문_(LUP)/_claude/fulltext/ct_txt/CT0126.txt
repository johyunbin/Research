Research combining the measurement of objective variables with surveys of people's perception of noise on city streets
is useful in terms of understanding the impact of urban noise on the population and improving the environment. Al-
though previous investigations have analysed the factors that may inﬂuence the noise annoyance of citizens, it is usu-
ally considered as a global aspect. This paper presents research based on in situ surveys and objective variables (urban,
meteorological and noise indicators) to evaluate some speciﬁc effects of noise on pedestrians in urban environments
where road trafﬁc is the main source of sound. The results show signiﬁcant relationships of the effects of noise and per-
ceptions of how noisy urban environments are with variables such as building height, road category and temperature,
with correlation coefﬁcients ranging from 0.37 to 0.64. Signiﬁcant correlations between these subjective variables and
the acoustic variables were also found, with explanations of variability that reached values of up to 50 %. A multivar-
iate analysis revealed that both urban variables (especially the category of street) and environmental variables can be
an alternative or a complement to models predicting the effects and perception of environmental noise based only on
acoustic variables.
1. Introduction
Environmental noise pollution in urban contexts is one of the challenges
facing society today, mainly due to its impact on human health and well-
being (EEA, 2020). Infrastructures for the transport of people and goods
are considered to be the main source of noise in this type of environment.

In fact, transport noise has become the second most important environmen-
tal source of ill health in Europe, after ﬁne particulate matter pollution
(WHO, 2018). Recent research keeps pointing out a close relationship be-
tween trafﬁc noise and different types of diseases and health disorders
such as anxiety (Lan et al., 2020) (Hegewald et al., 2020), depression and
psychological problems (Eze et al., 2020) (Baudin et al., 2018), obesity
(Cai et al., 2020) (Foraster et al., 2018), hypertension and cardiovascular
risk (Baudin et al., 2020) (Khosravipour and Khanlari, 2020), annoyance
and sleep disorders (Paiva et al., 2019) (Basner and McGuire, 2018), and
metabolic diseases (Huang et al., 2020) (Thiesse et al., 2018). Trafﬁc
noise is not an isolated issue, and is associated with other aspects such as
urban planning (Renterghem et al., 2020) (Barrigón Morillas et al.,
2021b) (Yuan et al., 2019), air quality (Silva and Mendes, 2012), socio-
economic factors (Xu et al., 2020) (Tong and Kang, 2021) and weather
conditions (Guan et al., 2020) (Sánchez-Fernández et al., 2021).
The study of environmental noise levels in cities using the noise indica-
tors for day (Ld), evening (Le), night (Ln) and day-evening-night (Lden) estab-
lished in the European Noise Directive (END, 2002) is often carried out by
means of strategic noise maps considering the different sources of noise
(Ozkurt et al., 2014) (Paschalidou et al., 2019) (Hinze et al., 2022) and
following the guidelines of international guidelines and standards (WG-
AEN (Working Group Assessment of Exposure to Noise), 2007) (ISO
1996-2, 2017). In the speciﬁc case of road trafﬁc noise, which is considered
to be the main source of environmental noise (EEA, 2014), simulations
using commercial software are carried out for this purpose taking into
account different variables such as vehicle ﬂow (Ascari et al., 2015)
(Fiedler and Zannin, 2015), sound power (Barrigón Morillas et al.,
2021a), vehicle speed (Ögren et al., 2018), and the characteristics of the
façades (Calleri et al., 2018). These noise models are validated through
long- and short-term in situ measurements (Zagubień and Wolniewicz,
2021) (Montes González et al., 2020a) (Aletta et al., 2020) that follow
different sampling strategies (Quintero et al., 2019) (Gómez Escobar
et al., 2012b) and measurement procedures (Montes González et al.,
2020b) (ANSI S12.18, 1994). Based on the results of noise maps for the
exposure of the population to noise pollution, action plans for the mitiga-
tion of environmental noise are then designed (Ögren et al., 2018)
(Vázquez et al., 2016). In addition, initiatives related to urban planning
that focus on the development and promotion of quiet areas have been pro-
posed in the scientiﬁc literature to try to make these types of environments
more pleasant for the resident population and to improve the sense of well-
being (Calleja et al., 2017) (Rey Gozalo et al., 2019) (Vogiatzis and Remy,
2017) (Hong et al., 2020).
However, there is a growing tendency to complement this type of
research based on objective noise indices with studies that allow us to
obtain an assessment of people's satisfaction with the sound quality of
urban spaces (Koprowska et al., 2018) (Youssouﬁet al., 2020) (Aletta
et al., 2018). The concept of sound quality can be understood as the degree
of adequacy of the acoustic characteristics of a space to the activities carried
out in the area. In this regard, conducting surveys on city streets is an
approach that provides interesting information for an analysis of the degree
of satisfaction and annoyance of residents, not only in relation to the sound
environment but also to other features such as cleanliness, air quality,
aesthetics of the environment, odours, etc. (Lionello et al., 2020) (Engel
et al., 2020) (Jiang et al., 2016) (Ba and Kang, 2019).
Noise indicators such as LAeq, LAFmin, LAFmax y LN are commonly used in
environmental noise studies (Paszkowski et al., 2018) (Maristany et al.,
2016). The effects of noise are generally measured based on indicators
that take the equivalent A-weighted sound level as a reference (WHO,
2011) (ISO 1996-2, 2017) (END, 2002), although the maximum sound
level is also considered in this regard (WHO, 2018). The relationships
between objective acoustic indices and subjective variables related to the
effects of noise in cities can be studied in order to analyse which aspects
can inﬂuence annoyance of citizens and their preferences for the use of
urban spaces (Bouzid et al., 2020) (Van Gerven et al., 2009) (Estévez-
Mauriz et al., 2018) (Ma et al., 2021), so that these can be taken into
consideration by urban planners at the design stage. Most previous research
has only assessed the overall effect of noise in terms of annoyance, but a
consideration of more speciﬁc aspects of the effects of noise on people
may also be of interest. In addition, it is also interesting to analyse the inﬂu-
ence of environmental and urban variables on the perception or effects of
noise in the same way as was done with physical characteristics of sound
and people-related factors (Ouis, 2001).
This paper presents the results of research carried out by means of in situ
surveys and measurements in Cáceres (Spain) to assess the effects of noise
on people in urban environments in which road trafﬁc is the main sound
source and to study its relationships with urban, environmental and acous-
tic variables.
2. Methodology
2.1. Survey, sampling and data collection procedure
The methodology followed in this study was based on a process of in situ
surveys and measurements, carried out simultaneously by four people in
the streets of Cáceres (Spain), in which values of both subjective and objec-
tive variables were collected. This study was carried out on working days
(Monday to Friday) in the time period from 9 a.m. to 7 p.m., during the
year 2020 (January and February) before the COVID-19 alarm state. The
sampling points were randomly selected on urban roads with different
functionality for vehicle mobility. Thus, they were sampled from main
city streets, Category 1, used for connection to other cities or interconnec-
tion of preferred streets, to Category 5 streets corresponding to residential
neighbourhood streets (Barrigón Morillas et al., 2021a). The objective
was to sample different urban settings with variability in urban and envi-
ronmental characteristics. For this purpose, 29 different locations were
sampled in the city of Cáceres as shown in Fig. 1.
The design of the survey was approved by the ethics and bioethics
committee of the University of Extremadura (37//2020), and an informed
consent form was ﬁlled out by each participant in each survey, according to
the Declaration of Helsinki. The effects of environmental noise on people
walking down the street was the dimension assessed in the survey using
nine questions. An 11-point numerical scale was used for each question
(where 0 was “not at all” and 10 “extremely”). The points on this numerical
scale are equally spaced and therefore provides a justiﬁcation for treating
the data as continuous in statistical tests (ISO/TS 15666, 2021). This type
of scale is more suitable for linear regression analysis (Brink et al., 2016).
The 11-point numerical scale is recommended by the International
Standardization Organization (ISO/TS 15666, 2021) and used in current
studies (Brink et al., 2019) (Schäffer et al., 2020) because it also has advan-
tages over verbal scales in its comprehension by people of different nation-
alities (Fields et al., 2001). Face-to-face was the method used for data
collection in the survey. Any evidence of hearing loss detected by the inter-
viewer during the presentation or conduct of the survey led to the exclusion
of the responses obtained. In the ﬁrst seven questions related to effect of
noise (see Table 1), respondents were asked to what extent or how often
the environmental noise in that street causes them: a) irritability;
b) startle; c) annoyance in the ears; d) interrupting a conversation with
someone nearby; e) raising the volume of their voice to speak with someone
nearby; f) interrupting a phone conversation; and g) raising the volume of
their voice on a phone conversation. Respondents were also asked to rate
their perceptions of the environment was h) in the city in general; and
i) on that particular street. Other demographic characteristics were also
collected: age, sex and level of education.
Different aspects have been considered to justify the representativeness
of the sample size. Fritz and Mackinnon (Fritz and MacKinnon, 2007) point
out the importance of having sample sizes necessary for 0.8 power for the
tests to be used. Therefore, the appropriate sample size for correlation
and multiple linear regression was determined using G*Power software
(Kang, 2021). It was deduced that 100 people were required for a power
of 0.95 and a medium effect size according to Cohen (Cohen, 1988). On
the other hand, Nunnally (Nunnally, 1978) and Thorndike (Thorndike,
1982) recommend a sample size 10 times the number of items. The present

study due to COVID's state alarm. A similar number of surveys were com-
pleted at each sampling point.
It is not unusual to ﬁnd previous studies with a sample size similar to
this study. Fritz and Mackinnon (Fritz and MacKinnon, 2007) show results
from a review of the survey literature where >50 % of the studies have a
sample size of <200. Douglas (Douglas and Murphy, 2016), Van Reterghem
(Van Renterghem and Botteldooren, 2012), Pirrera (Pirrera et al., 2014),
Tao (Tao et al., 2020) and Paiva (Paiva et al., 2019) carried out survey stud-
ies in highly populated urban areas with a similar number of surveys to
show the representativeness of the overall perception in the city where
each survey was conducted at a different point/household.
The objective variables were classiﬁed into three different groups (see
Table 1) and were collected both through a GIS database of Cáceres and
with visual inspection on site simultaneously with the surveys. First, a set
of urban variables associated with the features of the street were registered,
such as the length (L) and width (W) of the street, the average height of the
buildings (H), the relationship between building height and street width
(H/W), and the category of street (SC). The streets were classiﬁed into
the following categories (Barrigón Morillas et al., 2021a):
• Category 1: Preferential streets for connection with other towns and inter-
connection of those preferential streets.
• Category 2: Streets that provide access to major distribution nodes in a
town or are used as an alternative to category 1 during trafﬁc saturation.
• Category 3: Streets that lead to regional roads, streets that provide access
from street categories 1 and 2 to centres of interest in a town (hospitals,
shopping malls, etc.), and streets that clearly allow communication
between street categories 1 and 2.
• Category 4: All of the other streets that clearly allow communication
between the three previously deﬁned types and the principal streets in a
town's different districts that were not included in the previously deﬁned
categories.
• Category 5: The rest of a town's streets except trafﬁc-restricted streets.
Next, a group of variables related to environmental conditions were reg-
istered. The temperature (T), relative humidity (RH) and air pressure (AP)

were measured at microphone height by a portable weather station, and
monitoring was carried out to ensure that the wind speed (WS) did not
exceed 5 m/s. The luminosity (LU) at each measuring point was also logged
using a luxmeter. Considering that indicated in the Weber-Fechner Law
(Dehaene, 2003) (Reichl et al., 2010), the logarithm of luminosity
(logLU) was considered to study its relationship with the effects of noise.
Finally, different sound indicators were measured using a type 1
analyser, including the A-weighted equivalent sound level (LAeq), the
maximum sound level (LAFmax) and the percentile levels (L10, L50 and L90).
For this purpose, a type 1 sound level meter-analyser was used, and the
microphone was placed 1.5 m above the ground and at a similar distance
(2 m from the nearest point of the sound source) from the main sound
source of road trafﬁc (Montes González et al., 2020b). Fifteen minutes
sound measurements were made avoiding placing the microphone on
reﬂective surfaces (Montes Gonzalez et al., 2018). If the microphone was
placed at a distance between 0.5 and 2 m from the building façade, a correc-
tion of −3 dB was applied following the recommendations of ISO 1996-2
(ISO 1996-2, 2017; Montes González et al., 2020a). A veriﬁcation of the
calibration before and after each series of measurements was carried out
using a type 1 sound calibrator. The sound measurements were carried
out simultaneously with the surveys as indicated above. The researcher
conducting the sound measurements was separated at a sufﬁcient distance
so that the sound levels registered were not inﬂuenced by the interviewers.
At least one sound measurement was performed at each sampling point. In
addition, sound sources were registered during the measurements. Road
trafﬁc was the main noise source with a signiﬁcant presence at the different
sampling points. In fact, total trafﬁc ﬂow explained 83 % of the variability
of LAeq (dB).
2.2. Statistical analysis
The average values of the objective and subjective variables registered
in the measurements performed at each sampling point (N = 29) were
used in the subsequent descriptive and inferential analyses. The responses
obtained at each sampling point were averaged considering different
aspects. The urban variables are static and the environmental and noise
variables presented a low variability at each sampling point (see Fig. 2S
in the Supplementary Material). The responses given to each subjective var-
iable also presented a stable and low variability at each sampling point as
shown in Fig. 1S in the Supplementary Material. Therefore, similar results
are obtained in the per-survey and per-point correlation analyses as
shown in Tables 1S, 2S, 3S and 4S. However, the analysis by surveys does
not meet the premises of normality, homoscedasticity and linearity (an
expected result given the no or low variability of the objective variables)
leading to a loss of test power (non-parametric tests have lower power)
and the impossibility of performing bivariate and multivariate linear
regression analyses. Furthermore, considering the difference in the number
of respondents between the different points and the fact that the variability
of the variables is due to their different locations, a treatment by surveys
would bias the results towards those points with a greater number of
surveys.
A descriptive analysis of the average values and their deviation was
carried out with the subjective variables, i.e., those related to the effects
of noise. This descriptive analysis was complemented by a comparative
study of the mean values using the t-test. Parametric tests were used since
the variables met the assumptions of normality, homoscedasticity and line-
arity. Next, an analysis of the relationships between the objective and sub-
jective variables was carried out. First, the signiﬁcance of the relationship
between the two types of variables was analysed using Pearson's correlation
coefﬁcient, except for the “street category” variable, where Spearman's
correlation coefﬁcient was used, given its ordinal nature. Next, a bivariate
regression analysis was carried out for those variables that showed a signif-
icant correlation. Finally, only those variables that did not show collinearity
(variance inﬂation factor < 5) and which contributed to a signiﬁcant
increase in the explanation of the subjective variables were included in
the multivariate regression models. Stepwise regression and the Bayesian
information criterion (BIC) were used to choose the best performing model.

variables, and is organised into subsections corresponding to the different
groups deﬁned for the objective variables.

When analysing the values for the participants' perceptions of how noisy
is the city or street in which the survey was conducted, it can be noted that
the city was rated at a value of 5.5, that is, above the average value of the
employed scale. While the street was rated at 4.8, very close to the average
value of the scale. If comparing these results for the noise perception of an
environment with the values obtained for the speciﬁc effects of noise, it can
be found that there is a signiﬁcant increase in the average values. In fact, a
statistical analysis using t-test revealed signiﬁcant differences in both values
of the perception with respect to all the values of the effects. For “noisy
city”, a signiﬁcant difference with p < 0.001 was found in all cases. While
for “noisy street”, a signiﬁcant difference with p < 0.05 was obtained for
irritability and startle and p < 0.001 for the others. No signiﬁcant differ-
ences were detected between “noisy city” and “noisy street”.
Given that this study considered a number of speciﬁc noise effects, it
was difﬁcult to ﬁnd previous research in the scientiﬁc literature that treated
these effects in the same way in order to establish a direct comparison and
discussion. In the particular case of annoyance, some authors have reported
ratings of the general perception of noise annoyance that would somehow
cover all the speciﬁc noise effects addressed in the present study
(Öhrström et al., 2006; Gómez Escobar et al., 2012a). Taking this into
account, it would be expected that in general, the rating of overall
perceived annoyance in similar urban environments would be higher
than for each of the effects analysed separately. Other research also
analysed the annoyance perceived by people in green areas and urban
parks considering various types of sound sources, and distinctions have
been drawn, for example, between annoyance caused by construction,
screams, animals and road trafﬁc (Rey Gozalo et al., 2019).
3.2. Analysis of the relationships between subjective and objective variables
The outcomes obtained from the analysis of the correlations between
subjective and objective variables are shown in Table 2. With regard to
the urban variables, only two of the variables considered in this group
(average building height and street category) showed signiﬁcant correla-
tions with the subjective variables related to noise and its effects. The L,
W, and H/W did not show any signiﬁcant correlation with the subjective
variables (p > 0.05). Both urban variables (H and SC) differ in the number
of subjective variables with which they have signiﬁcant relationships and in
the intensity of these relationships. While the height variable is strictly geo-
metric, the street category is based on the functionality of the street as a
means of communication. According to the results reported in previous
studies, road categories signiﬁcantly stratify noise, so that noise values
decrease from Categories 1 to 5 (Barrigón Morillas et al., 2021a). The sub-
jective variables analysed here with respect to the effects and perception of
noise show a signiﬁcant negative correlation with respect to the road cate-
gory, except for items c) and h). The degree of association between the type
of road and the subjective variables related to raising the voice showed
associations with a degree of signiﬁcance of <0.001, which are comparable
to those found for the noise indicators. Concerning the environmental
variables, the temperature showed the greatest number of signiﬁcant
relationships with the answers given by the respondents. In addition, the
variables of atmospheric pressure and luminosity showed only one signiﬁ-
cant relationship, in these cases with the perception of how noisy the envi-
ronment was in the street and city, respectively. The RH and WS did not
show any signiﬁcant correlation with the subjective variables (p > 0.05).
Finally, the acoustic variables generally showed signiﬁcant negative rela-
tionships with all or almost all of the subjective variables studied with a
greater or smaller degree of association. This indicates that, as expected,
there is a close relationship between the sound indicators and the effects
of noise. It can be noted that the LAeq, LAF10 and LAF50 indicators are associ-
ated with all the subjective variables analysed here; the LAF90 indicator is
associated with almost all of them, while LAFMax is the indicator that
shows the weakest relationship with the subjective variables.

When the results obtained for average building height (H) are analysed,
it is possible to see that this variable can explain between 14 % and 20 % of
the variation in the subjective variables related to the effects of noise on the
population in urban environments, such as irritability (14 %), startle
(17 %), annoyance in the ears (20 %) and the perception of how noisy
the street is (17 %). Considering results obtained in previous studies, the
explanation of the variability of subjective variables by the predictor H is
not low. It should be noted that these previous studies used multiple predic-
tor variables and acoustic variables. This study has the novelty of analysing
the perception of speciﬁc noise effects and the speciﬁc contribution of pre-
dictor variables based on urban and environmental features. Average build-

variable, the slope of the linear regression equation is positive for all issues
related to the effects of noise or the perception of the studied environment;
that is, the value indicated by the respondents for the subjective variables
increases as the sound indicators take on a higher value. However, this is
not the case for the subjective variable perception of how noisy the city is
(h), where the negative value of the slope reﬂects an inversely proportional

variables to the acoustic variables could be considered valid for many
other cities of different sizes.
3.3. Multivariate regression analysis
In addition to the bivariate regression analysis presented in the previous
sections for each group of objective variables, a multivariate analysis was
also carried out using stepwise regression. The street category (SC) was
recoded as a dummy variable in order to include it as an independent
variable in the regression analysis. Consequently, the ﬁve different street
categories were grouped into main streets (Categories 1 to 3) and
neighbourhood streets (Categories 4 and 5). Neighbourhood streets were
considered as the reference level (value = 0) for this binary variable (SCb).
Since Table 1 revealed that the acoustic variables are those that individ-
ually explained a higher percentage of the variability of the subjective
variables related to the effects of environmental noise and the perception
of noisy urban spaces, a stepwise regression was ﬁrst proposed in this
section considering only the urban and environmental variables. The aim
was to study the extent to which combinations of these objective variables
would allow for a prediction of the effects of environmental noise and its
perception in urban environments with no need for in situ acoustic measure-
ments and, therefore, reducing the production costs of carrying out this
type of research associated with acoustic measurement equipment.
