A B S T R A C T
Physical inactivity poses a signiﬁcant public health challenge. Numerous studies have argued that
urban parks can promote physical activity (PA). However, evidence of physical activity observed
over long periods of time in parks is still very lacking. Our serial cross-sectional study within urban
parks in Shanghai investigated seasonal and daily variations in PA. Employing a geographically
and temporally weighted regression (GTWR) model, we established spatiotemporal relationships
between urban park characteristics and PA levels. We found that (1) Highest PA levels occur in
winter, while the lowest levels are observed in summer, with a V-shaped daily pattern. (2) Robust
and signiﬁcant associations between speciﬁc park characteristics and PA levels, while other
characteristics exhibited varying associations in different temporal and spatial contexts. These
insights offer valuable guidance for urban planners and park designers aiming to create more
active and sustainable urban environments.
1. Introduction
1.1. Green space and physical activity
The World Health Organization (WHO) has underscored the signiﬁcance of physical inactivity as one of the top ten global risk factors
for mortality, contributing to the burden of non-communicable diseases and affecting overall global health (World Health Organization,
2010). Insufﬁcient physical activity (PA) increases the risk of various non-communicable diseases (Lee et al., 2012). Existing studies
have demonstrated that adequate PA can mitigate the risk of numerous chronic diseases (Bush et al., 2007; Coutts, Horner, & Chapin,
2010).
A substantial body of empirical research worldwide indicate that urban green spaces can enhance the health of urban residents
(Bratman et al., 2019; Lai, Flies, Weinstein, & Woodward, 2019; Zhang, Yu, Zhao, Sun, & Vejre, 2020). Urban green spaces, primarily
composed of natural environments, serve as crucial places for urban residents to connect with and experience nature. These spaces not

Consensus has been reached in existing research regarding the role of urban parks in promoting PA (Bedimo-Rung, Mowen, & Cohen,
2005; Ohta, Mizoue, Mishima, & Ikeda, 2007; Hu, Liebens, & Rao, 2008; Hunter et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2021). Natural environments
not only provide opportunities for beneﬁcial “green exercise” like walking (Pretty, Grifﬁn, Sellens, & Pretty, 2003), but also increase the
likelihood of individuals in greener areas meeting recommended daily activity levels compared to those living in less green regions
(Mytton, Townsend, Rutter, & Foster, 2012).
1.2. Association between park characteristics and physical activity
Numerous studies have explored the various factors inﬂuencing PA. McCormack et al. (2004) presented a conceptual framework
encompassing environmental factors affecting PA, which included functionality, safety, aesthetics, and destination aspects. Research has
identiﬁed that park use is inﬂuenced by sports facilities, water features, and biodiversity (Hamstead et al., 2018; Song, Richards, & Tan,
2020). Studies have shown that PAs are inﬂuenced by ﬁtness facilities (Stewart, Moudon, Littman, Seto, & Saelens, 2018; Cranney et al.,
2016; Krenichyn, 2006; Hofﬁmann, Barros, & Ribeiro, 2017). Likewise, vegetation has been found to be associated with PA (Wang et al.,
2021; Zhang, Tan, & Richards, 2021; Zhang & Zhou, 2018; Schipperijn, 2010). Moreover, park accessibility is likely factors inﬂuencing
PA levels (Zhang and Zhou, 2018; Chen et al., 2021), suggesting a geographic connection between PA and parks.
However, existing research may have the following limitations. Firstly, most studies have been conducted at the city scale (Akpinar &
Cankurt, 2016; García de Jalon et al., 2021; Grigoletto et al., 2022; Petrunoff et al., 2022; Zhang and Zhou, 2018; Zhou, Fu, Xue, Wang,
& Zhang, 2021), which limited our understanding of how green spaces inﬂuence PAs within urban parks. Consequently, there is a
relative scarcity of research on the inﬂuence of internal park characteristics on PAs. Furthermore, while most studies have suggested a
positive relationship between urban parks and PAs, the strength of this relationship varied considerably among different studies. This
discrepancy may arise from the fact that much of the data collection in these studies was concentrated within a few days or a single
quarter (Evenson, Jones, Holliday, Cohen, & McKenzie, 2016), resulting in temporal bias during analysis (Wang et al., 2021). To our
knowledge, only one study conducted in Barcelona collected a year's worth of PA data (Puig-Ribera et al., 2022). Longitudinal
observational research on PA in China remains notably scarce.
1.3. Physical activity assessment
Firstly, existing research has developed numerous methods for investigating PA, including questionnaires (Grigoletto et al., 2022;
Zhang et al., 2021) and mobile phone signal data (Guan et al., 2020) as well as geolocation-based social media data (Song et al., 2020;
Chuang, Benita, & Tunçer, 2022). Big data, such as mobile phone signal and social media data, offers a degree of behavioral insight, but
they also have notable limitations, such as an inability to provide precise activity types and relatively coarse location information.
Moreover, data collected through smart devices may lead to information gaps for some population segments. Methods like question-
naires, on the other hand, are constrained in scale due to limitations in resources and human power. However, on-site observations can
yield detailed information about PA (Chow, Mckenzie & Sit, 2016; Wang et al., 2021; Zhang, Zhang, Lai, & Kwan, 2022), which is
critical for understanding user PA levels within parks. To address this, we employed the System for Observing Play and Recreation in
Communities (SOPARC) to collect PA data. This approach was widely used in research involving the observation-based collection of PA
data (Evenson et al., 2016).
Secondly, previous studies have quantiﬁed PA from the perspectives of visitor numbers (Hunter et al., 2015; Joseph & Maddock,
2016; Zhang & Zhou, 2018; Zhou et al., 2021), duration (Grigoletto et al., 2022; Petrunoff et al., 2022), intensity (García de Jalon et al.,
2021; Joseph & Maddock, 2016), among others. Metabolic equivalents (METs), widely used in sports science and epidemiology, were
employed to measure PA levels (Guo et al., 2022; Matthews et al., 2007; Mendes et al., 2018). The Compendium of Physical Activities,
jointly developed by Stanford University, Arizona State University, University of Wisconsin, etc., links types of PA to their respective
METs intensity values, thereby providing the potential for MET estimation from observational studies. Extensive research has examined
the temporal and spatial usage patterns of urban parks at the city level. However, studies speciﬁcally addressing the temporal and spatial
dynamics of PA within these settings are scarce. To our knowledge, only a few articles have explored this topic. For instance, Zhou, Hu,
Chen, Liu, and Wang (2024) identiﬁed time as one of the critical factors inﬂuencing leisure time physical activity; Pinto, Ferreira, and
Pereira (2024) observed signiﬁcant differences in group activities across different parks varying by season and time of day; Similarly,
Hu, Wu, Sun, Zhao, and Hu (2023) note a signiﬁcant reduction in speciﬁc activities on weekdays compared to weekends, underscoring
time as a crucial element affecting PA. The inherent characteristics of parks lead to diverse activity patterns across different areas,
resulting in spatial disparities in PA (Zhai, Li, Wu, & Wu, 2021). Consequently, both time and space emerge as critical determinants of
PA.
Thirdly, the use of regression-based predictive methods can be categorized into two main categories: global regression models and
local regression models. However, a major issue with global approaches is that parameters often vary within the study area, making
assumptions of temporal and spatial stationarity or structural stability unrealistic (Huang, Wu, & Barry, 2010). Hence, methods such as
hierarchical linear models (Goodman & Thibodeau, 1998), geographically weighted regression (Brunsdon, Fotheringham, & Charlton,
2010), and geographically and temporally weighted regression (GTWR) model (Huang et al., 2010) were employed to estimate pa-
rameters. The GTWR model is advantageous for capturing spatiotemporal relationships. According to Huang et al.'s (2010) study,
compared to global ordinary least squares models, the GTWR model reduced absolute errors by 46.4%. The GTWR model has been
widely used to explain relationships in various domains, including environmental pollution, epidemiology, and urban studies (Ling,
Qian, Guo, & Ukkusuri, 2022; Mirzaei, Amanollahi, & Tzanis, 2019; Wang et al., 2020). However, there were limited literature exploring

1.4. Our study
We conducted our survey in three parks within Shanghai, with observations taken at 10 sampling points within each park. Data on
PA were collected continuously from January to December 2021. In addition, we collected a range of park characteristics, encompassing
four aspects: (i) site characteristics, (ii) vegetation characteristics, (iii) facility characteristics, and (iv) aesthetic characteristics.
This study contributed to the existing research in several ways. Firstly, it employed a year-long serial cross-sectional survey to
observe PA within parks, aiming to reduce the potential errors resulting from short-term observations in previous studies. Secondly, by
amassing a substantial dataset of PAs, more urban park characteristics could be integrated into the research framework, enabling a
comprehensive discussion on the relationships between urban park characteristics and PAs. Thirdly, we utilized the GTWR model to
estimate the associations between park characteristics and PA levels, considering the spatial heterogeneity and temporal non-
stationarity of park spaces. In summary, our objective is to provide more scientiﬁc evidence for park designers in high-density urban
settings by exploring urban park characteristics that promote PA.
2. Methodology
2.1. Study sites
Shanghai, located in the Yangtze River Delta region, is the second-largest city in China, covering an area of 6300 km2 with a
population of 24 million, making it the third most populous city in the world. The population density of the central city is 34,000 people
per km2. Through a pre-study, Xujiahui Park, Fuxing Park, and Lujiazui Central Green were selected as the study sites. These parks are
situated near mixed-use areas, including commercial and residential zones, attracting a diverse user base. Additionally, the park
characteristics varied internal spaces, enabling residents to engage in a wide range of activities. This diversity is advantageous for
conducting comprehensive observations of PA.
We determined a minimum sample size of 30 groups of at least 30 people in each group to estimate regression coefﬁcients (Kreft & de
Leeuw, 1998) and selected 10 sample plots in each park. The sample plots were distributed evenly throughout the park area and
included at least one complete activity area. Five of the 10 sample plots in each park were linear spaces, and the remaining 5 were
faceted spaces, allowing us to investigate the effect of spatial patterns on physical activity. Fig. 1 shows the location and sample sites of
the three parks.
2.2. Data collection
2.2.1. Physical activity
From January to December 2021, trained observers collected data on PA and park characteristics in three parks. The research team
provided training to the observers on evaluating park characteristics of the parks and accurately recording the type of PA. Observations
were conducted on three days per month, including two weekdays and one weekend day, on 10 sample plots in each park. During an 8-
min period, observers independently recorded the number of people and speciﬁc types of PA within each sample plot. Observers also

2.2.2. Urban park characteristics
Building upon prior research (Hamstead et al., 2018; McCormack et al., 2004; Song et al., 2020), we classiﬁed urban park char-
acteristics into four distinct categories: site characteristics, vegetation characteristics, facility characteristics, and aesthetic
characteristics.
For site characteristics, we introduced the sample plot length-to-width ratio (RATIO_ Length-width) as an independent variable,
recognizing that the length and width of a park can inﬂuence its utilization (Zhai, Korça Baran, & Wu, 2018). The accessible site area
(AREA_ Accessible) constituted the second factor, encompassing not only hardscape but also softscape elements like accessible lawns.
Location evaluation was performed through two indicators: integration (INTG_ Sample plots) and connectivity (CNT_ Sample plots),
both grounded in fundamental spatial syntactic theory. Integration gauges the extent of spatial connectivity or isolation, with higher
integration levels correlating with enhanced accessibility. Connectivity, on the other hand, measures the number of directly linked
spaces to the sample plot, signifying greater linkage for higher values (Zhai et al., 2018). Additionally, we accounted for four distinctive
sample plot topographies: concave, convex, even, and uneven. The uneven topography was utilized as the reference variable, while the
remaining types were introduced as independent variables. Lastly, we posited that proximity to water (PROX_ Water) also inﬂuences PA
and hence included it in our model, with “no proximity” serving as the reference variable.
To encompass vegetation characteristics comprehensively, we employed three indicators: the green coverage ratio (RATIO_
Coverage), Shannon's diversity index (SHDI), and major plant combinations (PC). The green coverage ratio provides a two-dimensional
assessment of vegetation characteristics (Wang et al., 2021), while Shannon's diversity index serves as a measure of plant diversity. The
major plant combinations encompass grasses (G), shrubs (S), trees (T), trees and shrubs (TS), trees and grasses (TG), and trees and shrubs
and grasses (TSG). We treated these major plant combinations as dummy variables and incorporated them into the model, designating
shrub type as the reference variable. Furthermore, we considered the number of seats (NUM_ Seats) and streetlamps (NUM_ Street-
lamps). The former offers users opportunities for resting, while the latter contributes to nighttime security.
Aesthetic characteristics encompass the green view ratio (RATIO_ Green view) and seasonal plant ratio (RATIO_ Seasonal plants).
The green view ratio denotes the proportion of green color in an individual's ﬁeld of vision, while the seasonal plant ratio refers to the
proportion of plant species exhibiting noticeable seasonal changes relative to the total plant species.
Field research provided the necessary data, including accessible site area, sample plot topography, sample plot length-width ratio,
proximity to water, and seasonal plant ratio. The plant species and their quantities within the sample plots were documented, and
subsequently, Shannon's diversity index and the green coverage ratio were computed. To assess connectivity and integration, road
vector data was manually delineated with AutoCAD and integrated into Depth Map. Each sample plot was captured from eight distinct
perspectives, facilitating the calculation of the green view ratio using the GlunoCV model. Table 1 presents the deﬁnitions of the
dependent and independent variables.

variables exhibit signiﬁcant variation, afﬁrming the appropriateness of establishing the GTWR model.
3. Results
3.1. Descriptive statistics
The study encompassed 23 PA categories, comprising walking (n ¼ 103,745; 74.44%), sitting (n ¼ 12,335; 8.85%), running (n ¼
5121; 3.67%), standing (n ¼ 4652; 3.34%), square dancing (n ¼ 3364; 2.41%), aerobics (n ¼ 3011; 2.16%), photography (n ¼ 2068;
1.48%), parent-child activities (n ¼ 1836; 1.32%), tai chi (n ¼ 1126; 0.81%), chess or board games (n ¼ 733; 0.53%), cycling (n ¼ 494;
0.35%), badminton (n ¼ 252; 0.18%), yoga (n ¼ 128), instrument performance (n ¼ 125), singing (n ¼ 106), sword dancing (n ¼ 79),
skateboarding (n ¼ 57), rope skipping (n ¼ 34), frisbee (n ¼ 30), ball games (n ¼ 28), roller skating (n ¼ 16), painting (n ¼ 13), and
handwriting (n ¼ 10). Notably, walking emerged as the most prevalent PA, followed by sitting, standing, and running, collectively
accounting for over 90% of all observed activities. Conversely, ball games, roller skating, painting, and handwriting represented the
least frequent activities. Speciﬁc PA categories and their corresponding METs can be found in Table S6.
In this study, we computed the PA_METs for each sample plot across distinct time intervals and documented the corresponding park
characteristics. The descriptive statistics can be found in Table 2, while Fig. 2 illustrates the temporal ﬂuctuations of PA_METs. Over the
course of a day, PA_METs exhibited a diurnal V-shaped pattern, characterized by peak levels in the morning and evening, and trough
levels during the afternoon and early evening. Notably, winter recorded the highest PA_MET levels, in contrast to summer, which
exhibited the lowest levels.

3.3. Robustness testing
Robustness testing was conducted on a randomly selected 80% of the samples, and the results are presented in Table S7. It appeared
that the regression coefﬁcients and their signiﬁcance remain stable, with only minor variations observed in the associations. Conse-
quently, the regression results were robust and reliable.
4. Discussion
4.1. Main ﬁndings
This study employed a serial cross-sectional ﬁeld observations to collect data on PA and park characteristics. The relationship be-
tween PA metabolic equivalents and park characteristics was established using the GTWR model. Walking was the most prevalent form
of PA. Additionally, distinctive Chinese activities such as square dancing, sword dancing, and Tai Chi were observed, consistent with
prior research ﬁndings (Wang et al., 2021; Zhai, Li, Wu, & Wu, 2023). PA levels exhibited a distinctive diurnal variation, with the lowest
levels observed during the late afternoon to early evening (14:00–16:00), and comparatively higher levels in the early morning
(6:00–8:00) and evening (18:00–20:00). Notably, PA levels were highest during the winter and lowest during the summer. Concur-
rently, the study revealed robust associations between speciﬁc urban park characteristics and PA levels, while other associations
ﬂuctuated temporally and spatially, with spatial variability being more pronounced than temporal variation overall. Our research
ﬁndings elucidate the speciﬁc urban park characteristics that are signiﬁcantly associated with residents' PA levels, providing empirical
evidence for urban planners and park designers on how to design and retroﬁt parks in high-density urban environments.
Season may serve as a factor inﬂuencing PA. Chen et al. (2021) reported that parks may have more users during the spring and
autumn seasons. However, our study suggested that residents may be inclined to engage in higher metabolic-equivalent activities during
the winter, while the opposite trend is observed in the summer. We speculated that this might be related to temperature ﬂuctuations
throughout the year. Additionally, diurnal variations in PA levels are potentially related to park usage patterns and temperature (Lin &
Feng, 2023).
In regard to site characteristics, this study has identiﬁed a signiﬁcant association between the area of accessible spaces (AREA_
Accessible) and higher levels of PA. Existing research has indicated that open spaces can support PA (Baran et al., 2013; Edwards et al.,
2015; Rivera, Timperio, Loh, Deforche, & Veitch, 2021; Van Hecke et al., 2016), and our research reveals that a larger area of accessible
spaces may lead to METs of PA. The ratio of a sample plot length to width (RATIO_ Length-width) was an essential variable in promoting
PA levels, which may be related to the types of activities occurring within the site. Within our observations, walking comprises the
majority of PA, and sites with a larger aspect ratio might promote METs by facilitating activities such as walking and running. Song et al.
(2020) found that the length of sidewalks affects the walkability of a community, thus potentially exerting a more pronounced impact on
PA related to transportation. Connectivity of sample plots (CNT_ Sample plots) exhibited a signiﬁcant negative correlation with PA
METs, implying that connectivity may act as a detrimental factor inﬂuencing PA levels. In other words, sites with a higher number of
spatial connections tended to have lower levels of PA occurring within those spaces. This may be attributed to the fact that sites with
high connectivity offer more adjacent spaces, thereby dispersing residents to engage in PAs in the surrounding areas (Zhai et al., 2018).
Topography demonstrates a signiﬁcant association with PA levels, with considerable variation in this relationship. Generally, concave
and convex topography (TOPO_ concave & TOPO_ convex) tend to enhance PA levels, while even topography (TOPO_Even) may act as a
negative factor inﬂuencing PA. Although some studies have suggested that visitors may prefer activities on ﬂat surfaces (Gearin & Kahle,
2006), Lin and Feng (2023) have discovered that children and adolescents were more inclined to engage in high levels of PA in areas
with topographic variations. Proximity to water (PROX_ Water) signiﬁcantly reduces PA levels. Petrunoff et al. (2022) found a corre-
lation between the presence of water features and increased park sports activities, while Schipperijn, Bentsen, Troelsen, Toftager, and
Stigsdotter (2013) also established a positive association between water features and PA. Although our research differed from previous
ﬁndings, this disparity could be explained: water characteristics may attract more users, but such features might be linked to low
metabolic equivalent activities, such as standing and photographing.
There existed a consistent and positive correlation between the green coverage ratio (Ratio_Coverage) and PA levels, in line with the
ﬁndings of Tsai, Floyd, Leung, McHale, and Reich (2016). Increased PA was often associated with higher vegetation coverage, and urban
parks with denser vegetation appear to be more motivating for PAs compared to parks with lower vegetation density (García de Jalon
et al., 2021). The association between the major plant combinations and PA varied across time and space. Trees (PC_T), trees and grasses
(PC_TG), and trees and shrubs (PC_TS) exhibited signiﬁcant positive associations with PA levels, whereas the combination of trees,
shrubs and grasses (PC_TSG) demonstrated a signiﬁcant negative correlation. Grasses (PC_G) displayed relatively consistent associations
with PA levels during the spring, summer, and autumn, albeit with weak and non-robust associations due to regression coefﬁcients
ﬂuctuating near zero. In contrast, grasses exhibited a stable negative impact on PA during the winter. The attraction of visitors through
shade provision and noise reduction, as discussed by €Ohrstr€om (2004) and Gidl€of-Gunnarsson and €Ohrstr€om (2007), which partially
explained the positive link between tree-related plant combinations and higher PA levels. However, it was surprising that trees, shrubs
and grasses combinations (PC_TSG) were signiﬁcantly negatively correlated with PA levels. We speculated that trees, shrubs and grasses
combinations might create a more enclosed space, possibly leading to feelings of insecurity (Mak & Jim, 2018) and stress (Jiang, Chang,
& Sullivan, 2014), which could deter PA. Although large grassy open spaces were considered a vital characteristics in promoting PA

(Rivera et al., 2021), we observed a negative association between grasses and PA levels. This might be attributed to the fact that in the

Regarding facility characteristics, the number of seats (NUM_Seats) exhibited a negative association with PA levels. This was likely
attributed to the fact that, in comparison to sample plots lacking seats, seats might facilitate low metabolic equivalent activities such as
sitting and instrument performance. Furthermore, a higher quantity of seats might increase this supportive effect. An Australian study
found that parks support group activities by providing facilities such as benches and seats (Rivera et al., 2021). The number of
streetlamps (NUM_Streetlamps) had a positive impact on PA levels during the spring, summer, and autumn seasons, aligning with our
expectations. As an indicator of park safety, the presence of lighting, surveillance cameras, and emergency equipment had been reported
to signiﬁcantly increase PA (Zhang et al., 2022). However, in the winter, the inﬂuence of streetlamps shifted from positive to negative,
consistent with several studies on PA among older adults in Asian cities (Cerin et al., 2013; Inoue et al., 2011). Possible reasons were that
these studies were conducted in various social and geographic contexts, each with its unique safety concerns. China, being one of the
safest countries globally, did not consider nighttime safety in urban parks a signiﬁcant factor inﬂuencing PA. Moreover, users' perception
of streetlamps was subject to the time of day, which can introduce bias into the effect of streetlamps on PA. Our research ﬁndings also
underscored the ongoing issue of the lack of consensus in the current literature regarding the relationship between park safety and PA.
In the realm of aesthetic characteristics, consistent with prior research, we observed a positive association between the green view
ratio (RATIO_Green view) and PA levels, as previously reported by Lu (2019). However, Wang et al. (2021) and others have reported a
negative correlation between the green view ratio and the PA diversity. Another contributing factor to these discrepant ﬁndings could be
the non-linearity of the relationship between the green view ratio and PA. It was plausible that the positive impact of the greenery ratio
on PA might exhibit an optimal range, and excessively high green view ratio might elicit feelings of insecurity (Mak & Jim, 2018) and
stress (Jiang et al., 2014), thus negatively impacting PA participation. The proportion of seasonal plants (RATIO_ Seasonal plants)
exerted a signiﬁcant positive inﬂuence on PA levels. Vegetation that exhibited seasonal changes tended to attract visitors, with this
association becoming particularly pronounced during the winter. This could be attributed to the relaxing sensory qualities of plant
aromas and colors, which can contribute to mental and physical relaxation and stress reduction, consequently enhancing engagement in
PAs (Lin & Feng, 2023).
4.2. Research limitations
This study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, although the use of a long-term and high-frequency
observation method aimed to minimize errors due to seasonal and other factors, the cross-sectional nature of the study restricted the
ability to establish a causal relationship between urban park characteristics and PA levels (Song et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). The
study's ﬁndings only revealed an association between the two. Future longitudinal studies with a tracer study design could be conducted
to address this limitation. Secondly, due to the limitations of ﬁeld observation records, the study did not include observer characteristics
such as age, household income, and socioeconomic status, which have been shown to inﬂuence PA in previous studies. Furthermore, the
study only analyzed Shanghai parks, and because of cultural and geographic differences, the ﬁndings may not be generalizable to other
regions. Future cross-cultural studies could be conducted to investigate activity patterns under the inﬂuence of diverse cultures. All
indicators used in this study were objective, and the preferences of visitors for the park's landscape, which may have inﬂuenced the
occurrence of PA, were not considered. Future research should consider a combination of subjective and objective indicators to develop
a more comprehensive research framework. Lastly, certain park characteristics, such as the number of seats, do not change over time,
which can lead to a decline in the performance of the GTWR model (Huang et al., 2010).
5. Conclusion
This study employed a serial cross-sectional ﬁeld observation approach to collect data on the relationship between physical activity
levels and urban park characteristics, utilizing the geographically and temporally weighted regression model to delve into their asso-
ciations. Our investigation yielded the following key ﬁndings: Firstly, walking was established as the most prevalent form of physical
activity in urban parks. Secondly, our research unveiled diurnal variations in PA levels, manifesting a pronounced V-shaped pattern. PA
levels were at their lowest during the late afternoon to early evening, while mornings and evenings witnessed higher levels. Further-
more, seasonality exerted an inﬂuence, with higher physical activity levels during the winter and lower levels during the summer. These
ﬁndings imply that urban planners should take these variations into account, for example, by providing sufﬁcient shaded areas during
hot summer periods and ensuring adequate exposure to sunlight in winter, in order to encourage year-round outdoor activity partici-
pation among residents.
Furthermore, we discovered complex and intriguing relationships between urban park characteristics and physical activity levels.
Variables such as the area of accessible spaces, the ratio of a sample plot length to width, connectivity, proximity to water, the green
coverage, trees, trees and shrubs, trees and shrubs and grasses, the number of seats, the green view ratio, and the seasonal plants ratio
were found to have robust impacts on physical activity. In contrast, the effects of topography, grasses, trees and grasses, and the number
of streetlamps displayed signiﬁcant temporal and spatial variability. Hence, urban planners should consider varied usage needs
throughout the day and seasons, along with diverse activity preferences of different groups, to enhance urban residents' quality of life
and foster healthier, vibrant, and sustainable urban environments.
Lastly, despite certain limitations inherent to our study, such as the cross-sectional research design and the exclusion of subjective
indicators, our research outcomes offered valuable empirical evidence. This evidence aids urban planners and park designers in gaining
a deeper comprehension of the urban park characteristics closely associated with residents' PA levels. These insights encourage urban
