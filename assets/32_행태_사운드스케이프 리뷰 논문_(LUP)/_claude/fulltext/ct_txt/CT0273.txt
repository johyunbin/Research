and recreational trail-based activities in urban natural areas: A regional 
study leveraging user-generated big data and machine learning

Department of Geosciences and Natural Resource Management, University of Copenhagen, Frederiksberg C, Denmark

A B S T R A C T
Human recreational behaviours in nature represent context- and scale-dependent phenomena. Sustainable urban 
planning and natural resource management call for effective interventions that target local contexts, which 
require a refined understanding of human-nature interactions. However, existing studies have failed to 
concurrently examine multiple spatial scales, nonlinearity and spatial variation in these associations. This study 
leveraged big data (i.e., Strava sports tracking data for running and cycling) and advanced machine learning 
techniques (partial dependence plots and geographically weighted random forest) to disentangle the complex 
relationships between environmental characteristics and recreational activities. Key findings from our study 
include: (1) the influence of environmental variables on running and cycling varies across perceptual scales, as 
represented by route-based and survey-based navigation; (2) moreover, specific scales are more appropriate for 
characterising these relationships; and (3) important variables were identified both at the local level and across 
the region for running and cycling. Our study proposed multiscale approaches for modelling and understanding 
the effects of environmental characteristics. The results reinforce the need for context-specific strategies in urban 
planning. Practical considerations are provided for the planning and design of urban natural areas to promote 
recreational trail-based activities.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background and motivation
There are many benefits of outdoor sports for society (Donnelly and 
MacIntyre, 2019; Eigenschenk et al., 2019), and natural environments 
today provide the context for a large proportion of recreational physical 
activity in countries such as England (White et al., 2016) and Denmark 
(Rask et al., 2021). This trend has likely accelerated due to the 
COVID-19 pandemic, at least in Sweden, where “outdoorification” of 
sports during the pandemic has been reported (Hedenborg et al., 2024).
Spatial patterns of nature visitation demonstrate strong distance 
decay from peoples’ homes to the most commonly used nature sites 
(Jensen and Koch, 2004; Neuvonen et al., 2007; Termansen et al., 2004). 
Hence, urban and peri-urban natural areas are particularly used for 
nature-based exercise (White et al., 2016). Trail-based activities 
encompass recreational activities undertaken on trails, such as biking, 
running, and walking. In Greater Copenhagen, decades of promotion 
and planning efforts have been made to make urban and peri-urban 
natural areas more accessible and attractive to the public (Caspersen 
et al., 2006). Similar trends have been reported globally in case studies 
and planning initiatives (Bell et al., 2007). However, diversified and 
conflicting demands call for a rational and deliberate way of planning 
natural resource allocation (Alexander, 2000). Seeking an effective so­
lution with minimal resource investment reflects the principles of sus­
tainability (Bibri, 2019; Caparros-Midwood et al., 2015). This goal 
requires contextualized and localized solutions and prioritization.
Trails in natural areas serve as an important element when planning 
for daily nature contact and trail-based activities. An increasing body of 
literature has recognized that the relationships between trail-based ac­
tivities and the associated environmental characteristics vary spatially 
(Abildtrup et al., 2013; Winters et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2017), with 
various methods employed to probe such spatial heterogeneity (Feuillet 
et al., 2015; Munira and Sener, 2020; Qui˜nones et al., 2021). In light of 
this, this study aims to explore how various environmental character­
istics contribute to the recreational usage of trails across the capital

This study builds on the premise that the trail usage level for recre­
ational activity is influenced by the qualities of the surrounding envi­
ronment and the natural landscape features (Akers et al., 2012; Huang 
et al., 2022). However, the influences of environmental variables are 
affected by scale effects (Yang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024a). A simple 
example is that the impact of environmental features varies with prox­
imity (Schirpke et al., 2021), and that some spatial relationships imply 
distance decay relationships (Skov-Petersen, 2002). Therefore, it is 
relevant to explore how distance modulates people’s underlying per­
ceptions and, thus, the spatial relationships of the environmental char­
acteristics and the trail-based activity level (recreational trips on 
segments of the trail network).
Human cognition of the environment should be integrated into the 
theoretical analysis and modelling of urban systems (Portugali, 2018). 
The hypothesis above around scales in relation to the studied phenom­
enon taps into two types of navigation methods involving route- and 
survey-based perspectives (Shelton and Gabrieli, 2002; Tversky, 1993), 
which might contribute to site and route selection differently. It is pre­
mised on the notion that perceived route knowledge—at a relatively 
small scale and within short distances—influences route selection on the 
basis of the immediate surroundings of a trail, whereas survey knowl­
edge—at larger scales/within larger distances and stored internally as 
spatial knowledge/cognitive maps—contributes to site selection for an 
activity, which obviously lies beyond what is immediately perceptible, 
as illustrated in Fig. 1. A nuanced understanding of the relationships 
between environmental variables and trail-based activities at different 
scales is relevant to behavioural science, theories of human–nature in­
teractions and sustainable urban planning.
This study uses data from the Strava platform to uncover trail-based 
activities. Strava is a global online community built-up around self- 
monitoring and sharing of tracks recorded by GPS and performance 
parameters with high spatial and temporal accuracy and resolution 
(Venter et al., 2023). Strava data are available in an aggregated form 
where participants in different activities (e.g., running, walking, and 
cycling) are aggregated for individual segments of the global road 
network from OpenStreetMap (OSM) (Strava, 2022). Strava has become 
a rich data source and has been used in multiple previous studies of 
trail-based activities in city regions (e.g., Dadashova et al., 2020; Jiang 
et al., 2022; Lee and Sener, 2021; Munira and Sener, 2020).
1.2. Research questions
Although several studies have revealed the significance of scale in 
the representation of spatial relationships (Hengl, 2006; Murakami 
et al., 2019; Oshan et al., 2022), few have focused on the scale effects of 
relationships between trail-based activities and the natural environment 
(see also Section 1.3.2). Despite the increasing use of Strava data in 
recreation studies, studies that ground their approaches in relevant 
theories, such as spatial cognition and behaviour, remain scarce, 
potentially overlooking key human dimensions and limiting the depth of 
interpretation of the results. To address this, we developed models that 
incorporate varying perceptual scales of the surroundings, thereby 
providing empirical support for the theories underpinning our approach. 
By explicitly considering various scales in relation to environmental 
cognition, this study aims to demonstrate the effects of multiple scales 
related to trail usage and to identify spatially varying and 
scale-dependent relationships between environmental characteristics 
and aggregated trail-based activity levels facilitated by machine 
learning. Moreover, we examine the potential of new tools and methods 
conducive to sustainable and localized trail planning. The specific 
research questions are as follows: 
By taking into account the effects of different scales, what are the 
relationships between environmental variables and the levels of trail- 
based activities (running and cycling)?
When analysing use levels disaggregated to individual segments of 
trail networks, what are the appropriate scales for representing 
explanatory environmental variables?
According to the assessment of the effects of multiple scales, which 
explanatory environmental variables contribute the most to the 
levels of trail-based activities at different locations and across the 
region?
1.3. Related work
1.3.1. Scales in relation to two navigation methods
Behavioural geography studies human (mental and physical) re­
sponses to natural and man-made environments (Montello, 2018). The 
use levels of trails for various activities are influenced by environmental 
factors corresponding to the immediate perceptions of the surroundings 
(route knowledge) and/or the spatial knowledge/cognitive maps of in­
dividuals (survey knowledge). The spatial scales at which individuals 
perceive the environment play an important role in understanding the

processes of choosing sites and routes. People who find their way 
through a trail network are influenced by the immediate surroundings 
from a ground viewpoint (route-based knowledge) but are, at the same 
time, referring to known/surveyed overall characteristics of the land­
scape with landscape features further away, which cannot be immedi­
ately perceived. While choosing sites and making their way through the 
landscape, people switch between—and indeed mix—the two perspec­
tives, and the roles played by the characteristics of the environment 
change (Morales Saiki et al., 2011). This is also reflected in ongoing 
research efforts to understand environmental influences through the 
lens of visual (micro-scale) and neighbourhood (macro-scale) percep­
tions (Yang et al., 2024a; Jiang et al., 2022).
The methodologies in this field have included questionnaires (e.g., 
Zhong, 2020; Zhong and Kozhevnikov, 2016) and experimental tasks 
such as simulated or real-world navigation, text-based navigation, 
locating landmarks/objects, shortest path finding, and map sketching 
(Barhorst-Cates et al., 2021; Jabbari et al., 2022; Kozhevnikov and Puri, 
2023; Lopez et al., 2021; Muffato and Meneghetti, 2020; Paz-Argaman 
et al., 2024). These tasks have been designed to examine how 
different environmental features or representations influence navigation 
strategies. However, previous studies have often framed wayfinding as a 
cognitive function rather than an experiential process and were con­
strained by small sample sizes. To address this gap, there is a need to 
investigate behavioural patterns on a larger scale from a landscape 
experience perspective. Novel user-generated recreational data from 
platforms like Strava, with their extensive volume and geographic 
coverage, provide an objective measure of aggregated trail usage and 
enable scalable analyses of the relationship between navigation behav­
iour and the environment.
1.3.2. Scale effects and nonlinear relationships
When characterizing spatial relationships between targets (e.g., 
visitor volumes on trail segments) and surrounding explanatory vari­
ables, the most common approach is to define buffer zones or sharp 
thresholds, beyond which potential effects are disregarded. An obvious 
challenge is determining the most relevant buffer size. While most 
studies employ a single buffer size in spatial modelling, a few have 
addressed scale effects by incorporating multiple buffer sizes. For 
instance, Liu and Griswold (2009) and Tabeshian and Kattan (2014)
examined scale effects and identified optimal models. Similarly, Mir­
anda-Moreno and Fernandes (2011) adopted buffer sizes of 50, 150, and 
400 m around road intersections to represent the immediate surround­
ings, proximities and neighbourhoods where features might affect 
pedestrian activities. Hasani et al., (2019) found that different variables 
became influential at different buffer sizes, underscoring the inherent 
spatial scales of spatial processes. Moreover, studies by Browning and 
Lee (2017) and Murakami et al., (2019) highlight the impact of varying 
spatial scales on associations and predictors. In terms of analysis, 
multilevel modelling (MLM) has been employed to account for variables 
at both the segment and neighbourhood levels (Huang et al., 2023).
However, statistical analyses in earlier studies often relied on pre­
defined functions applied to global datasets, limiting their ability to 
capture highly complex spatial processes. Recently, machine learning 
techniques have gained recognition for their superior ability to model 
complex, nonlinear, and localized relationships. In particular, explana­
tory and spatialized machine learning approaches, such as Gradient 
Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT), SHapley Additive exPlanations 
(SHAP), and geographically weighted random forests (GRF), have been 
employed to assess the global and local importance of built environ­
mental features on jogging activity (Yang et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 
2024b).
Despite advancements in modelling techniques, few studies, to the 
best of our knowledge, have simultaneously accounted for the three 
critical effects - scale, nonlinearity, and spatial variation - of environ­
mental characteristics on recreational behaviours, which vary by ac­
tivity type. To address this gap, we applied the spatialised machine 
learning technique, GRF, and adopted incremental buffer sizes to mea­
sure environmental characteristics, capturing varying perceptual scales 
of surroundings in route- and survey-based navigation. These underex­
plored effects were central to our research questions.
2. Methods
2.1. Study area
As the capital city of Denmark, Copenhagen is the socioeconomic 
centre of Denmark, with a population of 2.1 million inhabitants in the 
metropolitan area. The region has various natural resources, including 
coastlines, forests, beaches, grasslands, lakes, and canals. Accessibility 
to nature, including urban nature, is a top priority in Nordic green 
infrastructure planning (Nordh and Olafsson, 2021). Regional develop­
ment for Copenhagen has been referred to as the “Finger Plan”, which 
outlines the direction and expansion of urban development along 
transport corridors expanding from the city core while preserving green 
wedges in between to enable easy access to recreational resources 
(Caspersen et al., 2006). Our study area covers the Finger Plan, which 
spans from the core area in the city of Copenhagen to the agricultural 
and natural landscape and coastline in North Zealand (hereafter referred 
to as the Copenhagen region). Several previous studies have docu­
mented the recreational use and value of natural areas in the study area 
(Caspersen and Olafsson, 2010; Korpilo et al., 2022; Vejre et al., 2010). 
Accordingly, the region is regarded as a relevant study area for exploring 
the relationship between environmental characteristics and trail-based 
recreational behaviour in natural areas across the urban, peri-urban 
and urban hinterland contexts. The main focus is on the natural areas 
in the region. The natural areas were delineated and defined by the 
selection of land use classes (‘forests’ and ‘open nature’) from a 
high-resolution land use raster dataset called Basemap (10 ×10 m) 
(Levin, 2022). Patches within 100 m were merged, and areas smaller 
than 2000 m2 were removed, resulting in the delineation of the studied 
areas in Fig. 2(a). Within these areas, OSM trails with activity data from 
Strava were extracted, as shown in Fig. 2(b). We focused on the sizable 
patches of natural areas in the region to control for external confounding 
physical factors. The selected natural areas were covered foremost by 
forest (76 %) and spanned 494 km2 out of a total regional area of 
6839 km2.
2.2. Overview of the analysis process
This section presents a methodological overview of the study (Fig. 3). 
In the variable processing, the explanatory variables were divided into 
‘scaling variables’ to acknowledge the varying scales (‘buffer sizes’) 
within which people perceive the environment (denoted as ‘var_100m’, 
‘var_250m’, ‘var_500m’, ‘var_1000m’) and ‘nonscaling variables’ 
(described in detail in Section 2.4.2). The nonscaling variables were 
directly aggregated into the grid cells along the road segments, and the 
scaling variables were computed at the road segments within given in­
cremental buffer sizes.
To cater to the expected nonlinear relationships, pairwise relation­
ships between the explanatory variables (for each buffer size) and the 
target variable (recreational activity level) were visualized with partial 
dependence plots (PDPs) (Section 2.5), integrated via the random forest 
(RF) algorithm, and interpreted through the lens of navigation behav­
iours (addressing research question 1).
To capture the spatial variations in the relationships, the present 
study used a spatialized machine learning model. Geographically 
weighted random forest (GRF) (Section 2.5) was employed to quantify 
the local contributions of the explanatory variables. In each grid cell, the 
variable that obtained the highest importance numeric measure among 
all considered variables was identified as the ‘locally important vari­
able’. The explanatory variables were ranked on the basis of the number 
of cells in which they were identified as the locally important variable

2.3. Target variable processing
Strava is an online social platform for sports where users can record 
and upload exercise routes, which has gained increasing popularity, 
with over 100 million users worldwide (Strava, 2024). We collected 
monthly aggregated data for recreational running (which also included 
walking and hiking activities) and cycling activities. The dataset covers 
the Copenhagen region for the period from 2019 to 2022. Demograph­
ically, the dataset is biased towards men and the middle-aged group, 
with 70 % and 84 % of Strava trips performed by men for running and 
biking, respectively, and nearly 50 % performed by people aged 35–54 
for both activities (Appendix A.1). The applied dataset comprised 217, 
183 road segments, with a total count of 664 million trips for running 
and 1718 million for cycling.
2.4. Variable selection and calculation
2.4.1. Variable selection
The selection of explanatory variables (Table 1) was inspired by 
previous research on forest recreation and trail preferences, i.e., ‘canopy 
cover’ (Edwards et al., 2012), ‘broadleaved ratio’ (Agimass et al., 2018; 
Hegetschweiler et al., 2017; Termansen et al., 2013; Wartmann et al., 
2021), ‘tree age’ (Jensen, 1999; Rosa et al., 2020; Sacher et al., 2022; 
Simkin et al., 2020), water area (lakes, rivers, sea, bogs, etc.) (Harden 
et al., 2022), ‘road property’ (Deelen et al., 2019; Munira and Sener, 
2020; Schirck-Matthews et al., 2022), ‘recreational facilities’ (Christie 
et al., 2007; Skov-Petersen, 2002), ‘accessibility’ (Skov-Petersen, 2002;
Jessen et al., 2023), ‘natural area edges’ (Termansen et al., 2004), and 
‘visibility’ (Edwards et al., 2012).
2.4.2. Scaling and nonscaling variables
Incremental buffer sizes were applied around road segments in the 
model building process to account for the different perspectives 
involved in route- and survey-based navigation. Small buffer sizes are 
assumed to represent the perceptual scales - i.e. route-based navigation - 
of the surrounding environment, whereas larger buffer sizes assumably 
represent spatial knowledge that cannot be sensed by immediate 
perception and must accordingly, depend on survey knowledge.
In Table 1, variables for exploring the varying influences at different 
perceptual scales are listed, which are referred to as ‘scaling variables’. 
Four buffer sizes were applied around the road segments in the variable 
calculation.
In addition, we investigated other environmental attributes without 
applying buffers, as these variables were considered the properties of a 
given road segment per se instead of surrounding environmental vari­
ables. These ‘nonscaling variables’ were ’physical accessibility’, ’major 
road type’, ’major road surface’, and ’length of the natural area edge’. 
Notably, ‘number of recreational facilities’ was included as a ‘nonscaling 
variable’ because a mixture of recreational features was included in this 
category, which was created by the Danish Nature Agency. This category 
consists of benches, campsites, hiking/cycling tracks, scenic areas, etc. 
Therefore, the incrementation of buffer sizes to conceptually associate 
singular environmental attributes were considered not applicable.

scripts, and PostGIS. Owing to concerns about computational efficiency, 
the raster data were converted into points and then joined within each of 
the buffer sizes around the road segments. The values computed were 
assigned to road segments after being weighted by the segment lengths

2.5. Geographically weighted random forest as a spatial extension of 
random forest
The values of the input variables did not follow a normal distribution 
(Appendix A.2); hence, machine learning was applied because of its 
ability to handle nonnormal statistical data distributions (Catani et al., 
2013).
Machine learning methods have been extensively applied across 
various domains because of their superior accuracy in predicting com­
plex phenomena involving high-dimensional nonlinear relationships 
and relaxation of assumptions of data distributions (Breiman, 2001; Guo 
et al., 2021; Humston et al., 2010; Zhou et al., 2019). Among machine 
learning methods, tree-based algorithms, including random forest (RF), 
excel at explaining the underlying associations by discerning the relative 
contributions of the explanatory, independent variables to the depen­
dent, target variable (Jiang et al., 2022). For a detailed description of RF, 
readers are referred to Appendix B.1.
By incorporating spatial dependence and heterogeneity, Georganos 
et al., (2021)
and Georganos and Kalogirou (2022)
proposed 
geographically weighted random forest (GRF), which applies the 
framework of geographically weighted regression (GWR) to RF 
(Kalogirou, 2013). Instead of the whole extent of the dataset, GRF cre­
ates a submodel of the RF for each spatial data point with nearby data 
points. The number of included points is called the ’bandwidth’, which 
is an important hyperparameter that can be adjusted to achieve the best 
out-of-bag (OOB) R-squared value. We conducted a bandwidth search 
from 50 to 400 for the GRF modelling (see Appendix C.1 for the 
hyperparameters). The contribution of each explanatory variable can be 
evaluated by the average decrease in impurity, i.e., data variance, in the 
target variable, expressed in Eq. (1), at all the internal nodes split by the 
explanatory variable. The GRF algorithm was implemented via the R 
package SpatialML 0.1.6 on a high-performance computing server.

3. Results
3.1. Recreational running and cycling intensity
The spatial patterns of running and cycling intensity exhibited 
certain differences (Fig. 4). Collectively, runners appeared to traverse 
the road network within natural patches, and their trips more commonly 
occurred in the inner city. In contrast, cycling hotspots exhibited a 
pattern of extending from urban to rural natural patches, with several 
long-distance trip trajectories cutting across multiple natural patches, 
particularly those around water features and coastal areas.
3.2. Visualization of the relationships between the variables with PDPs
The partial dependence plots (PDPs) in Fig. 5 were generated for 
each buffer size. Subplots are arranged horizontally by increasing buffer 
size, with explanatory variables listed vertically. The subplots reveal 
nonlinear relationships that vary across buffer sizes. Since the exact axis 
values were scaled to fit in the combined subplots, our discussion 
(Section 4.1) focuses on the observed trends.
3.3. Appropriate scale sizes for representing the environmental variables
This section describes how the significance of scaling variables vary 
across buffer sizes (Table 2). Variables were ranked based on the number 
of cells where they were identified as locally important. The goal was to 
determine the appropriate buffer size for each scaling variable in spatial 
processes.
Table 2 illustrates the scale effects of the associations. ‘Tree cover’ 
maintained relatively stable ranks for running activity across different 
scales. Some variables, such as ‘broadleaved ratio’ and ‘visibility’, ach­
ieved the highest rank at larger buffer sizes (1000 m), whereas others, 
like ‘total length of neighbouring roads’, peaked at smaller scales 
(100 m).
3.4. Modelling performance and hyperparameters
The bandwidth search process and the resulting R-squared values 
from both GRF and RF (as a baseline) are documented in Appendix C.2
and C.3. The best bandwidths for the GRF models are presented in 
Table 3. The appropriate buffer sizes for the scaling variables were 
applied in the final GRF model, GRF_Optimal. This model achieved the

scales of relationships enhances model accuracy.

4. Discussion
4.1. Explained variance by the models
We consider the overall R-squared values obtained in this study 
satisfactory compared to recent literature, where R-squared values 
typically range between 40 % and 75 % (Yang et al., 2024c; Dadashova 
et al., 2020; Hong et al., 2020; Munira and Sener, 2020; Yang et al., 
2022). Noticeably, in a study by Yang et al., (2024a)
using 
Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT), the R-squared reached 
92.6 %. However, their model included 23 variables, whereas our study 
used only 11. While adding more variables may increase R-squared, our 
aim was not solely to maximize predictive performance but to under­
stand the impact of key explanatory variables and scale effects. There­
fore, we consider our R-squared values (74.03 % for running and 
68.21 % for cycling) acceptable, reflecting a balance between model 
performance and parsimony.
4.2. Relationships between the explanatory variables and trail-based 
activity levels at different scales
The PDPs depict the nonlinear relationships between the explanatory 
variables and the trail-based activity levels, which vary across different 
perceptual scales of the environment during navigation. Here, we 
interpret smaller buffer sizes as corresponding to route-based naviga­
tion, whereas larger buffer sizes imply survey-based navigation. In the 
following sections, we discuss the influences of the explanatory vari­
ables in relation to different perceptual scales. By comparing our find­
ings with existing literature, we emphasize that scale effects may explain 
the heterogeneity observed in outcomes across studies.
For the plot numbers used in the following sections, refer to Fig. 5
above.
4.2.1. Length of the neighbouring roads
For cycling, there is a drop from zero across all the buffer sizes. We 
speculate that an isolated trail could become dominant when no other 
alternative roads are available. For the smaller buffer size (plot (1a)), the 
initial decrease is followed by a notable upward trend. This suggests a 
positive influence of the road density within smaller distances (e.g., 
100 m) for cyclists in route-based navigation. This result aligns with the 
finding from previous studies that identified the local effect of road 
density, influencing movement patterns within smaller and specific 
areas (Tcymbal et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024a).
In contrast, the relatively low value at zero suggests that runners 
might be less likely to choose isolated trails in survey-based navigation 
(plot (4a)), e.g., when there is only one trail within 1000 m, instead 
tending to select sites with better connected trails, as indicated by the 
upward part of the curve.
The curves level off across the four plots, suggesting that beyond a 
certain threshold, road density has minimal influence on running and 
cycling volumes. This reflects the trade-off between denser road net­
works that enhance connectivity but also increase safety risks, and the 
balance between potential interruptions and the lively atmosphere 
created by the presence of other trail users (Tcymbal et al., 2020; Yang 
et al., 2024b; Yang et al., 2024c).
4.2.2. Water
The positive effect of water areas was robust across all five buffer 
sizes. A sharp increase in the volume of runners, from zero, indicates 
that the presence of water, even small areas, could encourage trail-based 
activities. However, the curves that gradually level off suggest that there 
might be a threshold for this effect, beyond which further increasing the 
amount of water area may not lead to effective promotion. However, for 
buffer sizes of 100 m and 250 m (plots (1b) and (2b)), another increase 
was observed for running. In addition, water bodies within relatively 
long distances had similar impacts on cycling and running activities 
(plot (4b)), indicating a steady positive influence. Overall, the results 
show that the presence of water had an instant positive effect on trail- 
based activities. Previous studies have generally identified a positive 
impact of blue spaces (Agimass et al., 2018; Harden et al., 2022; 
Hochmair et al., 2019). Our study further quantified this association by 
considering water surface size and different perceptual scales in the 
route- and survey-based navigation.

4.2.4. Broadleaved ratio
Similar polarized effects of the ‘broadleaved ratio’ are observed in 
plots (1d) and (2d), with the lowest point around 0.5. This suggests that 
highly blended tree types are less attractive when viewed from close 
proximity during route-based navigation. In survey-based navigation 
(plot (4d)), opposing preferences between running and cycling were 
observed. The negative effect on cycling contrasts with most studies, 
which found that mixed and broadleaved stands are preferred over co­
nifers for recreational value (Abildtrup et al., 2013; Filyushkina et al., 
2017; Gerstenberg and Hofmann, 2016; Nielsen et al., 2007). This is 
consistent with a prior study in the same geographical context, which 
reported a preference for conifers in the northern area, dominated by 
broadleaved forests (Termansen et al., 2008). Thus, the preference for 
forest types is context- and activity-specific, influenced by perceptual 
scales.
4.2.5. Tree age
The impact of ‘tree age’ follows similar trends across different scales. 
In plot (1e), it is clear that ancient trees hold significant recreational 
value in close proximity (Edwards et al., 2012; Pukkala et al., 1988; 
Simkin et al., 2020), with their complex morphology contributing to a 
sense of place (Suchocka et al., 2022) and serving as landmarks for 
navigation (Edwards et al., 2012; Pukkala et al., 1988; Simkin et al., 
2020). This effect diminishes as the scale increases (plots (2e), (3e), and 
(4e)). However, the plots also show a negative relationship between tree 
age and activity levels for stands younger than 30 years. The negative 
impact of older trees could be due to concerns about safety and the 
challenge of navigating through dense forests (Wojnowska-Heciak et al., 
2020). Edwards et al., (2012) suggest that large stands may be perceived 
as intrusive. These results reflect the heterogeneous findings in studies 
on tree age preference, which are influenced by scale effects.
4.2.6. Visibility
The relationships between visibility and activity level showed similar 
trends across the four buffer sizes, with a clear positive correlation, 
especially for cycling. This suggests that higher visibility could be a 
general positive factor, as indicated by previous studies (Schultze et al., 
1983; Zhang et al., 2024b).
4.2.7. Nonscaling variables
The plots of the nonscaling variables were highly similar across the 
different scales, indicating the robustness of the model.
‘Physical accessibility’ has been emphasised as a key factor influ­
encing the recreational use of nature (Caspersen and Olafsson, 2010; 
Koppen et al., 2014). Our study confirmed this relationship for running 
activities (Yang et al., 2024c), while the effect appeared neutral for 
cyclists with greater mobility. Notably, the considerable increase 
observed between 4000 and 6000 on the x-axis suggests a threshold 
effect, which may be important to consider in efforts to promote 
trail-based activities. The presence of an effective range was also re­
ported in prior studies (Yang et al., 2024b; Yang et al., 2025).
‘Recreational facilities’ emerged as a positive factor, consistent with 
findings from a recent study on outdoor jogging (Yang et al., 2025). 
However, we observed a stronger effect among cyclists, who are likely to 
travel longer distances to access such amenities. However, the plots 
suggest that beyond a certain point, an excessive number of facilities 
within a spatial range (e.g., 500 m in our analysis) does not significantly 
increase trail-based activity levels. Future studies could explore this 
trade-off to better understand its implications.
Finally, the two types of activities exhibited similar relationships 
with the ’length of the natural area edges’. This variable was influenced 
by 1) the location of a grid cell - whether it was inside or at the periphery 
of the natural patches and 2) the morphology of the natural area edges 
intersecting a grid cell. As the plots depict, the highest level of the ac­
tivities was observed at zero on the x-axis, indicating a higher activity 
level inside natural areas (Hansmann et al., 2007). Nevertheless, the 
following upwards trends indicate that a positive effect emerges as the 
length of natural edges increases. This shows that edges with complex 
morphologies might also hold recreational value (Agimass et al., 2018; 
Baumeister et al., 2020).
