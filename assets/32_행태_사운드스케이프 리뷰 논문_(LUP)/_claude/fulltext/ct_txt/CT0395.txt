A B S T R A C T
Research on human–environment interactions remains fragmented, with limited exploration of how diverse 
park features jointly relate to multiple physical activity (PA) levels and sensory perceptions. Large-scale textual 
data offer new opportunities to capture public experiences of parks. However, prevailing natural language 
processing approaches, such as lexicon-based and prompt-based methods, often lack contextual sensitivity 
and accuracy. To address these limitations, we developed a landscape character named entity recognition 
(LCNER) model fine-tuned on a manually curated dataset to simultaneously extract park features, sensory 
perceptions, and three PA levels from online reviews. Among six pre-trained language models evaluated, 
the DeBERTa-large–based LCNER achieved the highest mean F1 score (0.896 ± 0.001) and outperformed 
domain-lexicon baselines, with the largest improvements observed for the best-performing entity categories: 
+0.558 in precision, +0.231 in recall, and +0.395 in F1 score. Quasi-binomial analyses revealed that facility-
related features provided better model fit for moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) and sound perception than 
for other activities and sensory types. Several features exhibited opposite associations with MVPA and 
sedentary behavior. Moreover, certain activity-oriented facilities were negatively associated with sensory 
perceptions, suggesting a potential trade-off between active engagement and sensory awareness. Overall, 
LCNER demonstrates the potential to unify the extraction of park features, activity levels, and sensory 
perceptions from online texts, advancing understanding of how park environments shape human experiences 
and behaviors. Code and prompts are available at https://github.com/Wenpeimuzi/Landscape-NLP.

Bai et al., 2023). The World Health Organization’s Global Physical 
Activity Action Plan (Organization, 2019) emphasizes the importance 
of modifying the built environment such as parks to support active

Correspondingly, research has progressed along two parallel trajecto-
ries: one aiming to enhance physical activity through park design (Ives 
et al., 2017; Kabisch et al., 2015; Knobel et al., 2021; Tsai et al., 2019), 
and the other seeking to improve sensory environments to promote 
more pleasant and supportive public spaces (Zheng et al., 2024; Korpilo 
et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025). Yet emerging evidence indicates that 
human activities and sensory perceptions are interrelated in park envi-
ronments (Deng et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2025), raising questions about 
whether design strategies that promote one dimension may reinforce 
or conflict with the other. Understanding these interconnections is 
therefore essential for designing parks that maximize overall health and 
experiential benefits, but research integrating these dimensions remains 
scarce.
Micro-scale park features, such as path width, vegetation arrange-
ment, shading, and seating, are particularly relevant because they 
are more feasible and cost-effective to modify than macro-scale or 
surrounding environmental factors (e.g., increasing park density or 
number) (Zhang et al., 2023; Abu-Omar et al., 2017; Petrunoff et al., 
2022). Existing research on the relationship between park features and 
physical activity are not consistent, which may partly result from the 
omission or inconsistent operationalization of micro-scale character-
istics, underscoring the need to examine these features more system-
atically (Markevych et al., 2017; McCormack et al., 2010; Kaczynski 
et al., 2009). Similarly, sensory perceptions have been shown to relate 
differently to various park elements (Farahani et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 
2023; Jeon and Hong, 2015), highlighting the need for more fine-
grained classifications of features to enable more accurate examination 
of their sensory correlates (Zhang et al., 2025a). Therefore, a more fine-
grained and consistently operationalized measurement of micro-scale 
features is essential for accurately examining their relationships with 
both physical activity and sensory perceptions.
However, current methods for measuring park features, physical 
activities, and sensory perceptions face limitations. Sensory perceptions 
are typically assessed through questionnaires or Public Participation 
Geographic Information Systems (PPGIS), physical activity through 
self-reports, field observations, or GPS tracking, and park features 
through field audits and questionnaires (Zheng et al., 2024; Chan 
et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019; Mepparambath et al., 2024; Korpilo 
et al., 2023). These approaches rely on predefined frameworks that are 
largely top–down and are prone to recall bias (Butler, 2016; Brown 
and Weber, 2011; Bruns and Stemmer, 2018; Koblet and Purves, 2020). 
Their high data-collection costs also lead to limited sample sizes, con-
strained spatial and temporal coverage, and narrow representation of 
landscape character dimensions (Van de Mortel, 2008; Zhang et al., 
2024b; Koblet and Purves, 2020; Wang et al., 2021a; Zhang et al., 
2022b; Li et al., 2024b). Such limitations contribute to variability 
across studies and restrict comparability (Zhang et al., 2019; Floyd 
et al., 2009; McCormack et al., 2010). These challenges underscore the 
need for scalable, bottom-up, and more efficient approaches for measur-
ing park features, physical activities, and sensory perceptions (Remme 
et al., 2021).
Geo-located textual data from online review platforms provides a 
promising complement by offering cost-effective and large-scale, nat-
urally occurring descriptions of park features, activities, and sensory 
impressions (Väisänen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2020b). 
These resources can uncover relationships that smaller surveys or ob-
servational studies often struggle to detect, complementing traditional 
data (Hao et al., 2023; Huai et al., 2023). Recent advances have 
demonstrated that textual big data can be used to predict sensory 
perceptions and to link physical elements to sensory experiences (Zheng 
et al., 2024; Koblet and Purves, 2020; Wang et al., 2025b). Research 
indicates that PA-related tweets correlate with county-level physical 
activity rates and are negatively associated with inactivity (Liu et al., 
2019; Cesare et al., 2019), further confirming the utility of textual 
big data in analyzing activity and sensory dimensions (Sun et al., 
2023; Liu and Young, 2018; Ghermandi and Sinclair, 2019). Despite 
this progress, existing research typically treats sensory perceptions or 
physical activities in isolation, leaving cross-dimensional relationships 
largely unexplored.
Methodologically, landscape studies still rely largely on unsuper-
vised or rule-based natural language processing (NLP) approaches such 
as topic modeling and lexicon-based methods (Li et al., 2024b; Gugulica 
and Burghardt, 2023; Lore et al., 2024; Song et al., 2020; Li et al., 
2024a; Kong and Sarmiento, 2022; Tang et al., 2023). These ap-
proaches have difficulty capturing overlapping and context-dependent 
concepts (Li et al., 2024b; Huai and Van de Voorde, 2022; Lore et al., 
2024). For example, mentions of ‘‘physical activity’’ and ‘‘activity-
related facilities’’ frequently appear together, which reduces classi-
fication accuracy. Generative large language models (LLMs) such as 
GPT-4 can mitigate some of these issues with minimal annotation (Luo 
et al., 2025; Zhang and Su, 2024), yet they often underperform domain-
specific supervised models (Kalyan, 2024; Liu et al., 2021), incur high 
computational costs, and risk inconsistency due to ongoing model 
updates (Kalyan, 2024; Wang et al., 2024). These limitations highlight 
the need for more robust, reproducible, and fine-grained NLP methods 
capable of reliably extracting diverse environmental attributes.
Supervised models trained on domain-specific datasets offer a
promising alternative. Although traditional supervised deep learning 
requires extensive manual labeling, transfer learning with Bidirec-
tional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and its vari-
ants (Devlin et al., 2018; Conneau et al., 2019; He et al., 2020; Warner 
et al., 2024) greatly reduces annotation efforts compared with con-
ventional architectures (Howard and Ruder, 2018; Devlin et al., 2019; 
Kalyan, 2024; Wang et al., 2023b). Existing applications in landscape 
research primarily adopt fine-tuned sentence-classification models to 
identify specific types of activities (Fan et al., 2023), assess park 
satisfaction (Wang et al., 2021b), or characterize park accessibility and 
attractiveness (Zhao et al., 2025). However, these approaches require 
training a separate model for each feature or perception category, re-
sulting in high computational and operational costs. In contrast, named 
entity recognition (NER) provides a more efficient and fine-grained 
solution by extracting multiple features simultaneously. Although NER 
has shown promise in various urban applications such as ecosystem 
services identification (Zhang et al., 2024b), flood location extrac-
tion (Yan et al., 2024), agriculture (Yao et al., 2024), and aeronautical 
intelligence (Baigang and Yi, 2023), it remains underutilized in land-
scape studies due to the lack of domain-specific annotated datasets. 
This scarcity not only restricts the development of specialized NER 
models but also restricts broader comparisons across emerging NLP 
approaches.
Importantly, NER provides the foundation for higher-level tasks 
such as recommendation systems, knowledge graph construction and 
aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) (Zhang et al., 2022a; Baigang 
and Yi, 2023; Wang et al., 2018). ABSA is particularly valuable because 
visitors often express multiple and sometimes contrasting sentiments 
toward different park features within a single review (Zhang et al., 
2022a; Scaria et al., 2023). However, most urban studies still rely on 
sentence-level sentiment scores or lexicon-based ABSA, which provide 
limited granularity and often require substantial manual effort (Li et al., 
2024a; Wang et al., 2023a; Dai et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Sadick 
and Chinazzo, 2025). Effective ABSA depends on accurately identifying 
aspect terms, which is precisely the function of NER (Jayakody et al., 
2024; Scaria et al., 2023). Developing custom NER models can there-
fore address major NLP challenges in landscape research by facilitating 
fine-grained feature extraction and fine-grained sentiment analysis.
To address these gaps, this study introduces a Landscape Charac-
ter Named Entity Recognition (LCNER) model that enables, the fine-
grained, simultaneous extraction of park features, physical activity 
levels, and multi-sensory perceptions from large-scale textual data. This 
integrated approach allows these dimensions to be analyzed within 
a unified framework, revealing cross-dimensional relationships that 
were previously difficult to capture using traditional measurement. This 
study seeks to answer three key questions:

2. Human–environment interaction via textual big data
In this section, we situate our work within the broader field of 
human–environment interaction research and textual big data analyt-
ics.
Textual data from social media platforms (e.g., Twitter/X) are com-
monly used to study visitors’ preferences, activities, and perceptions of 
parks, capturing spatiotemporal variations (Li et al., 2024b; Ghermandi 
and Sinclair, 2019), such as changes in activities pre- and post-COVID-
19 (Cui et al., 2025) and seasonal patterns (Roberts et al., 2019). 
In contrast, review platforms (e.g., TripAdvisor, Google Maps, Ctrip) 
offer longer texts, where users tend to focus more on landscape fea-
tures (Kong and Sarmiento, 2022; Villeneuve and O’Brien, 2020; Huai 
and Van de Voorde, 2022). These characteristics make review data 
particularly suitable for identifying perceived elements and examining 
multi-element relationships (Huai and Van de Voorde, 2022; Wang 
et al., 2021b). These platforms have proven valuable for scalable, 
longitudinal explorations across various contexts, including urban parks 
and streetscapes, with spatial scales ranging from neighborhood to 
national levels (Sun et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Grzyb et al., 2021).
Current methods for measuring human–environment interactions 
typically rely on sentence classification, matching keywords or phrases 
derived from clustering and topic modeling to sentence content (Huai 
and Van de Voorde, 2022; Liu et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2022; Wang et al., 
2022). However, these methods require extensive manual interpreta-
tion and often identify latent rather than target features. Lexicon-based 
strategies are more targeted but lack contextual sensitivity (Huai and 
Van de Voorde, 2022; Kong et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024a). Semi-
supervised methods, such as lexicon-based pseudo-labeling, reduce an-
notation costs but still face generalization limitations (Zheng et al., 
2024). Supervised classifiers, like ERNIE-based models for physical 
activity recognition (Gugulica and Burghardt, 2023; Fan et al., 2023), 
show promise but remain constrained by single-task settings, high 
annotation costs, and limited transferability. A supervised NER model 
linking sensory perceptions to cultural ecosystem services (CES) has 
demonstrated the potential of entity-level extraction (Zhang et al., 
2024b), yet there remains a need for domain-specific NER capable of 
simultaneously identifying features, activity levels, and sensory percep-
tions.
Sentiment scores associated with target elements provide another 
way to represent human–environment interactions (Zhang et al., 2025b; 
Wang et al., 2023a; Dai et al., 2024). However, most existing studies 
use sentence-level sentiment scores (Li et al., 2024a; Ballesteros-Olza 
et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhang and Su, 2024; Sadick and 
Chinazzo, 2025), which lack the granularity needed for feature-level 
analysis. Rule-based dependency parsing in ABSA struggles with ambi-
guity, multi-aspect sentiments and reproducibility (Wang et al., 2023a; 
Dai et al., 2021; Jayakody et al., 2024). Although GPT-based mod-
els perform well in sentiment polarity detection, they underperform 
in aspect extraction and sentiment intensity detection (Luo et al., 
2025; Zhang and Su, 2024). By enabling explicit feature extraction, a 
domain-specific NER model such as LCNER could enhance the granu-
larity of ABSA and support more fine-grained, entity-specific sentiment 
mapping.
Beyond quantitative measurement, recent studies increasingly ex-
plore the underlying mechanisms of human–environment interactions. 
Methods like co-occurrence networks, community detection algorithms, 
and association rule mining reveal relationships between features (Wan 
et al., 2021). Zheng et al. (2024) used the Walktrap algorithm to study 
how physical features influence multisensory perceptions, while Wan 
et al. (2021) found that natural elements enhance well-being and 
artificial features enhance aesthetic perceptions. Other studies integrate 
text-derived indicators with spatial data such as street-view imagery 
to explore multi-source interaction pathways (Wang et al., 2023a; 
Tang et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023). For example, Sun et al. (2023) 
linked physical activity to blue-space visibility and street-level features, 
and Roberts et al. (2017) identified amenity quantity as a significant 
predictor. However, differentiating physical activity into varying in-
tensity levels remains rare (Grzyb et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2022; Sun 
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025b), limiting our understanding of how 
specific park features relate to diverse behavioral responses. These 
findings highlight the potential of big data to support urban planning 
and targeted environmental interventions.
3. Methodology
We begin by developing a workflow to fine-tune a supervised NER 
model, LCNER, to extract park features, sensory perceptions, and phys-
ical activity levels from textual data, as shown in Fig. 1. We then 
evaluate the final LCNER model, selected from six large language model 
backbones, against widely used lexicon-based methods to demonstrate 
its improvements. Finally, we apply LCNER to all public parks in Sin-
gapore to explore the correlations between park features and physical 
activity, as well as between park features and sensory perceptions.
3.1. Research site
Singapore, one of the most densely populated and urbanized cities 
globally, has undergone significant transformations in its green space 
(GS) forms and distribution due to industrial, commercial, and infras-
tructural development (Zhang and Su, 2024; Richards et al., 2020). 
Approximately 80% of the population resides in government-subsidized 
high-rise Housing & Development Board (HDB) flats, which typically 
lack private gardens. As a result, the government has made continu-
ous efforts to provide high-quality public parks that meet residents’ 
needs (Yuen and Soh, 2017). Additionally, Singapore’s high mobile 
connectivity and widespread social media engagement, with over 70% 
of the population actively uses social platforms (Richards and Tunçer, 
2018), make it an ideal setting for leveraging big data to monitor 
human–environment interactions in near real-time.

two widely recognized platforms for capturing public perceptions and 
experiences of urban spaces (Yang and Zhang, 2024; Huai and Van de 
Voorde, 2022; Park et al., 2022; Ballesteros-Olza et al., 2024). In Sin-
gapore, parks include both managed areas and unmanaged ones, such

May 2025 were included for further analysis of park features related 
to sensory perceptions and physical activities. As the study focuses on 
advancing NLP techniques for landscape-character extraction, we rely 
solely on textual data and retain TripAdvisor reviews, which were too 
sparse for quantitative analysis, to diversify the annotated corpus and 
improve model robustness.
The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the first author’s organi-

analysis complied with the terms of use and fair-use guidelines of 
the respective platforms (Google, 2025; TripAdvisor, 2025). To protect

anonymized prior to analysis. The original raw data were deleted 
after the completion of analysis, and only the anonymized version was 
retained. Data was stored in an encrypted, access-controlled repository 
accessible only to the research team. The storage environment complies 
with institutional data-protection policies and supports secure sharing 
upon formal request.
3.3. Indicators description
Building on environmental audit and observation tools, such as 
the Natural Environment Scoring Tool (NEST) (Gidlow et al., 2018), 
Neighborhood Green Space Tool (NGST) (Gidlow et al., 2012), and En-
vironmental Assessment of Public Recreation Spaces (EAPRS) (Saelens 
et al., 2006), along with existing literature (Zhang et al., 2019; Joseph 
and Maddock, 2016; Väisänen et al., 2021; Ghermandi et al., 2022), 
we developed a comprehensive taxonomy of entities related to human–
environment interactions. The LCNER dataset and model framework 
categorize these entities into three primary dimensions: park features 
(further divided into natural elements and artificial facilities), physical 
activities, and perceptions. These dimensions are further organized into 
24 categories, reflecting widely recognized landscape characters, as 
summarized in Table A (see Appendix).
Natural park features are subdivided into seven categories based on 
commonly accepted classifications linked to health outcomes. Facilities 
are grouped by their functional use into categories such as moderate-
to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) facilities (F_MVPA), sedentary-
activity facilities (F_sedentary), recreational facilities (F_recreation), 
cultural and aesthetic facilities (F_cultural), catering (F_catering), and 
transport (F_transport). We also specifically include signage (F_sign), 
paths (F_path), and lighting (F_lights), given their documented rele-
vance to physical activity and park use in several studies (McCormack 
et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2019). This flexible classification allows 
researchers to adapt annotated entities to their specific study goals. 
Physical activity is categorized into three intensity levels, as in tradi-
tional research, enabling a more nuanced analysis of environmental in-
fluences. Regarding sensory perceptions, we focus on sound, olfactory, 
and tactile modalities, which though often underexamined, are salient 
in park settings (Zheng et al., 2024). Visual perception was excluded 
because it is implicitly embedded in landscape descriptions and is more 
appropriately assessed through image-based analyses (Zhang et al., 
2023; Zhou et al., 2023). We also consider psychological perceptions, 
particularly perceived crowding. Together, these perceptions provide 
rich information that shapes visitors’ experiences and well-being.
3.4. Indicators measurement
3.4.1. Supervised methods
This study presents a formalized NER framework tailored to the 
landscape architecture domain, which required the creation of an an-
notated corpus and guidelines due to the absence of suitable domain-
specific datasets. To comply with fair use policies and improve analyti-
cal feasibility, park reviews were segmented into individual sentences.

categories and span boundaries, were developed collaboratively. Initial

reviewed by another; discrepancies were resolved through consen-

Cohen’s 𝜅, where 𝜅> 0.8 indicates reliable annotation.
To reduce the cost of data annotation, we adopted an iterative LLM-
assisted annotation workflow (Fig. 1). The process began with training 
an initial LCNER model using a seed set of 7000 GPT-4 annotated and 
manually corrected sentences. This model was then used to pre-label 
subsequent data batches, which were verified and corrected by humans, 
with a particular focus on low-frequency entity types. Each iteration 
incorporated the expanded dataset into model re-training, progressively 
improving model accuracy and label coverage. This process was re-
peated until further gains were marginal, resulting in a robust and 
diverse dataset for final evaluation. The final LCNER dataset consists of 
10,635 sentences containing 34,008 annotated entities, of which 60.7% 
originated from Google Maps reviews.

layer for structured span extraction, as the GP-based framework outper-
forms CRF-based and token-level classification approaches, particularly 
on complex and imbalanced datasets (Su et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2024).

and long-range entities. In post-processing, when multiple spans of the 
same category are detected within a sentence (e.g., both ‘‘playground’’ 
and ‘‘water playground’’ tagged as F_recreation), we retain only the 
longest span (‘‘water playground’’). Additionally, once a span is labeled 
with a given category (e.g., F_recreation), it is prevented from being 
labeled under any other category sharing the same ‘‘F_’’ prefix, ensur-
ing exclusive assignment within the same dimension. This restriction 
applies only within the same prefix group (dimension) and does not 
affect cross-dimension nested entities (e.g., F_recreation nested within 
Act_light). Under identical data and hyperparameter settings, this rule 
slightly increased precision (from 0.894 to 0.900) while marginally 
decreasing F1 (from 0.8965 to 0.8963), indicating a small precision-
oriented trade-off that improves internal consistency without substan-
tially affecting overall performance.
The annotated dataset was divided into training, validation, and 
test sets using a group-based split by park name, ensuring that all 
sentences from the same park were assigned to one subset to prevent 
data leakage. A greedy balancing algorithm assigned parks to subsets 
in an approximate 70/10/20 ratio, producing three disjoint partitions 
with comparable linguistic contexts.
To determine a strong supervised baseline for subsequent exper-
iments, we compared six transformer-based NER models using the 
70/10/20 group-based split. These included four widely used general-
purpose architectures (BERT-large, XLM-RoBERTa-large, DeBERTa-
large, and ModernBERT-large) and two NER-specialized models, (
SpanBERT-large and LUKE) (Devlin et al., 2018; Conneau et al., 2019; 
He et al., 2020; Warner et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2020; Yamada 
et al., 2020). Each model was trained under identical settings using 
a linear decay learning rate schedule, a dropout rate of 0.1 within 
transformer layers, a learning rate of 3.5e−5, a batch size of 16, and 15 
total training steps, and the training was repeated across five random 
seeds(41–45) to account for initialization variability. For each seed, the 
checkpoint achieving the highest validation F1 score was retained, and 
its performance was evaluated on the test subset. Model comparison 
was based on the mean F1 score, precision, and recall across the 
five seeds. We further evaluated the domain robustness of the LCNER 
model using the Google-Maps-only subset of the held-out test set, which 
originally comprised both Google Maps and TripAdvisor reviews, with 
approximately 60.7% of the samples coming from Google Maps.
After identifying the best-performing supervised model, we con-
ducted hyperparameter optimization through an exhaustive grid search 
over learning rates (5.0e−5, 4.5e−5, 4.0e−5, 3.5e−5, 3.0e−5, 2.5e−5) 
and total training steps (5, 10, 15). Based on the optimal configu-
ration, we then implemented 5-fold cross-validation to assess model 
generalization. The training and validation sets were merged into a 
unified training pool, and GroupKFold (by park) was reapplied to avoid 
lexical leakage. Model performance was summarized as the mean ± SD 
across folds, and per-class F1 scores were reported with 95% confidence 
intervals (CIs) derived from fold-wise performance distributions.
3.4.2. Unsupervised method
In landscape studies, lexicon-based methods are the dominant un-
supervised approach due to their low annotation requirements and
operational simplicity. These methods treat the quantification of
environment–human interactions as a sentence classification task,
where sentences are assigned to predefined categories based on the 
presence of context-relevant keywords.
To benchmark our supervised NER approach against this stan-
dard, we constructed a domain-specific lexicon and expanded it us-
ing Word2Vec (Vargas-Calderón and Camargo, 2019). Initial sentence 
labels were assigned through exact word matching, serving as our 
lexicon-only baseline. To enhance this baseline, we implemented a 
two-stage ‘‘lexicon + LLM’’ pipeline: sentences flagged by the lexicon 
were further validated by a zero-shot large language model (Laurer, 
2023). Only sentences confirmed by both approaches were retained, 
yielding an improved unsupervised baseline, which has been shown to 
significantly increase classification accuracy.
3.4.3. Model comparison
Although LCNER is fundamentally an entity-recognition model
rather than a sentence classifier, we used two evaluation granularities 
to facilitate a comprehensive comparison with lexicon-based methods: 
entity identification and sentence classification. In entity identification 
(LCNER-span), each extracted entity span is assessed individually. In 
sentence classification (LCNER), a sentence is deemed related to a given 
category if it contains at least one correctly identified entity of that 
type. This span-to-sentence mapping converts LCNER’s outputs into 
sentence-level labels, aligning with standard text-analysis practices.
We evaluate all methods — lexicon-only, lexicon + LLM, LCNER-
span, and LCNER — using precision (P), recall (R), and F1 score (F1), 
providing a fair benchmark for the supervised NER framework against 
established lexicon-based baselines.
The choice of baseline is informed by field conventions: lexicon-
based methods provide the most accessible and reproducible solution 
when annotated data is scarce or unavailable. By demonstrating sig-
nificant improvements in classification accuracy with the supervised 
LCNER model, we highlight the added value and potential of LLMs for 
domain-specific applications.
3.5. LCNER application
The fine-tuned LCNER model was applied to 314 parks to demon-
strate its practical utility. LCNER extracted mentions of landscape 
features, sensory perceptions (olfactory, sound, tactile), and physical 
activity categories (MVPA, light PA, sedentary) from each park’s Google 
Maps reviews. For each park, the proportion of reviews mentioning 
each feature was calculated and used as a quantitative indicator in 
subsequent analyses.
Quasi-binomial generalized linear models (GLMs) with a logit link 
were employed to examine associations between park features and 
different activities or sensory perceptions. This model is well suited 
for proportion outcomes that often exhibit overdispersion and het-
eroskedasticity, particularly when review volumes vary widely across 
parks. Unlike beta-based models that require the exclusion or transfor-
mation of boundary values (0 or 1), the quasi-binomial specification 
accommodates such proportions directly through a dispersion parame-
ter that adjusts for extra-binomial variance. The regressions’ goodness 
of fit is evaluated using a pseudo 𝑅2 of McFadden, which cannot be 
interpreted as the variance explained by the model (Chagas et al., 2024; 
McFadden, 1972).
In each model, the dependent variable represented the proportion 
of reviews mentioning a given activity or perception, and independent 
variables were the proportions of park features grouped into three 
categories (natural elements, facilities, and crowd). Predictors with 
a variance inflation factor (VIF) above 5 were removed to reduce 
multicollinearity (O’brien, 2007). To examine standardized coefficients,

In addition to assessing annotation consistency, we examined how

model convergence and stability.
Fig. 5 reports three key evaluation metrics (F1-score, recall, and 
precision) for all models, averaged over five random seeds to ensure ro-
bustness. DeBERTa-large achieves the highest average F1-score (0.896 
± 0.001), followed by XLM-RoBERTa-large and BERT-large. In contrast, 
LUKE (0.876 ± 0.002) and ModernBERT-large (0.878 ± 0.007) yield 
lower F1-scores, suggesting greater difficulty in balancing precision 
and recall, with ModernBERT-large exhibiting the highest variability. 
For recall, DeBERTa-large (0.895 ± 0.002) again ranks first, indicat-
ing stronger sensitivity to true entities. XLM-RoBERTa-large (0.882 ±
0.008) and BERT-large (0.882 ± 0.001) follow, while ModernBERT-
large records the lowest recall (0.851 ± 0.014). Regarding precision, 
ModernBERT-large (0.908 ± 0.005) and XLM-RoBERTa-large (0.901 
± 0.004) lead, highlighting their effectiveness in minimizing false 
positives. BERT-large (0.895 ± 0.005) and DeBERTa-large (0.897 ±
0.002) show slightly lower but stable values, while SpanBERT-large 
and LUKE perform less favorably. Overall, DeBERTa-large offers the 
best balance between precision and recall and is therefore selected for 
subsequent analyses. The relatively small differences in precision across 
models indicate that DeBERTa-large controls false positives comparably 
to others while delivering superior overall accuracy.
Among the six baseline models evaluated, the LCNER variant with 
seed 43 achieved the highest F1 score and was used to identify the 
optimal hyperparameter configuration through grid search. Using this 
configuration (learning rate 3.0e−5, ten training iterations, and ten 
epochs), the model was retrained on the combined training and vali-
dation sets to produce the final version for downstream analyses and 
deployment. This final model achieved an micro F1 score of 0.895, with 
precision and recall of 0.896 and 0.895, respectively. When evaluated 
on the Google-Maps-only test subset, the model achieved a slightly 
lower F1 score of 0.897, with precision and recall both near 0.897, 
suggesting minor stylistic differences between the two platforms and 
consistent performance on Google Maps reviews.
