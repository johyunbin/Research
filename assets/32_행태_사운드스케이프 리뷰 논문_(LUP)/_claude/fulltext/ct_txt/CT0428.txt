A B S T R A C T
People perceive waterfront walking spaces through continuously changing visual landscapes and soundscapes. 
However, existing audio-visual assessments largely rely on fixed static scenes or isolated acoustic indicators. This 
study proposes a scalable framework for explaining dynamic landscape preference in waterfront walking envi­
ronments by integrating distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape characteristics. Taking Dalian as a 
case study, 59 participants provided dynamic perceptual evaluations of 36 one-minute walking videos. Deep 
learning models were applied to extract distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape features, while a 
structural causal model (SCM) and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis were combined to examine 
dose–response patterns and interaction effects. The comparison between SHAP-based predictive attribution and 
SCM-based effect-strength ranking suggests that the intervention-oriented relevance of the equivalent continuous 
sound pressure level (LAeq) may be underestimated when preference is interpreted only through general pre­
dictive importance. Different variables exhibited distinct nonlinear patterns. The contribution direction of 
midground disturbance to preference was moderated by LAeq and showed opposite patterns. The interaction 
between background openness and foreground enclosure further revealed spatial configuration mechanisms in 
dynamic walking environments. Notably, features such as human sounds, background openness, and natural 
sounds showed clear contextual heterogeneity across functional areas and park types. These findings provide 
measurable empirical evidence for the synergistic audio-visual design of waterfront walking environments.
1. Introduction
Waterfront parks are important urban blue–green spaces for daily 
recreation, leisure walking, and the promotion of physical and mental 
well-being [1,2]. Their capacity to attract visitors and support 
well-being depends not only on the presence of water and greenery, but 
also on how individuals perceive these environments during movement 
[3]. Landscape preference is a key perceptual indicator linking land­
scape quality and restorative experience with the design and manage­
ment of urban natural environments [4,5]. Waterfront walking 
experience involves continuous changes in visual, auditory, and spatial 
exposure that are not fully represented by fixed-scene evaluations [6]. 
Therefore, re-examining landscape preference in waterfront parks from 
a dynamic, multisensory perspective has clear theoretical and practical 
significance.
Landscape preference is shaped by multiple environmental cues 
operating across visual, auditory, and spatial domains. In the visual 
domain, it is significantly associated with both compositional attributes, 
such as visible greenery, water visibility, and color diversity, and 
organizational attributes, including openness, scene depth, and enclo­
sure [7–9]. In the auditory domain, soundscape quality also significantly 
influences restorative experiences and aesthetic preference [4,5]. 
Furthermore, audio-visual interactions can alter overall scene evalua­
tion [10–13]. Taken together, these studies indicate that landscape 
preference in waterfront parks should be understood as a holistic 
response shaped during dynamic exposure, rather than as an isolated 
judgment of a single visual element.
Despite these insights, existing research still has three main limita­
tions. First, many studies still rely on static photographs, street-view 
images, or fixed scenes. These materials allow controlled comparison 
of visual attributes, but they provide limited information about the 
temporal progression of waterfront walking experience. Along a route, 
pedestrians may encounter changing openness, enclosure, water visi­
bility, activity intensity, sound-source composition, and sound pressure 
level. Such sequential exposure is difficult to represent through isolated 
scenes [6,14]. Second, existing visual quantification mostly focuses on

For pedestrians, foreground elements shape immediate enclosure, 
disturbance, and bodily proximity; midground elements often form the 
main visual focus of walking experience; background elements 
contribute to openness, prospect, and spatial continuity. Therefore, 
distance-layered spatial structure indicators are needed to represent the 
organizational attributes of waterfront walking environments in a more 
perceptually meaningful way [8,15]. Third, analytical methods in 
landscape-preference research still face challenges in explaining 
nonlinear and coupled environmental effects. Correlation analysis and 
conventional regression models help identify environmental variables 
associated with preference, but they have limited capacity to reveal 
threshold responses, nonlinear relationships, and cross-modal in­
teractions. Machine-learning models can improve predictive perfor­
mance under complex environmental conditions, but their feature 
importance usually reflects the contribution of variables to prediction 
and is difficult to translate directly into priorities for design interven­
tion. In waterfront walking environments, spatial structure, visual con­
tent, soundscape characteristics, and contextual factors are often closely 
coupled. Therefore, it is necessary to combine a structural causal model 
based on a prespecified causal graph with interpretable machine 
learning to identify dose–response patterns, interaction effects, and key 
environmental cues with design-intervention relevance.
To address these research gaps, this study develops an integrated 
multisensory framework for explaining dynamic audio-visual preference 
in waterfront walking environments. The framework links route-based 
first-person audio-visual stimuli with continuous preference ratings, 
distance-layered spatial structure, soundscape indicators, and an 
explanatory modeling strategy. It is designed to clarify how foreground, 
midground, and background spatial configurations, together with 
acoustic exposure and sound-source characteristics, contribute to land­
scape preference under dynamic walking conditions. Specifically, the 
research objectives are as follows. 
(1) To develop a scalable workflow for evaluating dynamic prefer­
ence responses under synchronized visual and acoustic exposure.
(2) To quantify distance-layered spatial structure features and 
multidimensional soundscape indicators, and to estimate their 
design-relevant associations with landscape preference under a 
prespecified causal graph and observed-covariate adjustment.
(3) To identify nonlinear dose–response patterns, cross-modal in­
teractions, and contextual differences that can inform fine-scale 
renewal strategies for waterfront walking environments.
2. Literature review
2.1. Dynamic landscape preference and audio-visual interactions
Landscape preference is not a simple response to static visual infor­
mation, but an integrated perceptual outcome shaped by dynamic 
exposure and multisensory integration. Because environmental experi­
ence is inherently multisensory, auditory stimuli can modify visual 
landscape evaluation; natural and congruent sounds generally enhance 
aesthetic preference and restorative experience, whereas incongruent 
audio-visual stimuli are more likely to elicit negative evaluations [4,5,
11,13,16]. Dynamic perception may be relevant to landscape preference 
because route-based exposure presents sequential changes in spatial 
layout, visibility, and environmental cues during movement. Such 
moving visual sequences may help capture spatial continuity, explora­
tion, and changing audio-visual context that are less explicit in isolated 
static scenes [14,17].
Taken together, landscape preference is shaped by visual informa­
tion, dynamic experience, and audio-visual interaction; however, most 
studies still examine visual content and soundscape characteristics 
separately, leaving unclear how spatial organization modifies sound­
scape effects during dynamic exposure.
2.2. Spatial-structure metrics for landscape preference
Visual assessment of landscape preference provides an important 
approach to understanding human responses to the environment [18,
19]. From a design perspective, the visual attributes shaping landscape 
preference can be divided into compositional attributes, which define 
landscape content, and organizational attributes, which describe how 
that content is spatially configured [8,15]. Previous studies have shown 
that compositional attributes, such as green view index, plant richness 
[20], artificial facilities [21], perceived color diversity [22], and 
element combinations [23], have been associated with landscape pref­
erence and visual behavior.
Although organizational attributes such as scene depth, sense of 
order, and scene type can moderate the effects of compositional attri­
butes on preference, their measurement remains insufficient because 
existing visual metrics are still largely limited to two-dimensional pixel 
proportions and provide limited representation of openness, scene 
depth, and enclosure [9,15].
Prospect–refuge theory suggests that people tend to prefer environ­
ments that balance visual access and a sense of protection [24]. 
Empirical studies based on viewshed analysis, photographs, virtual re­
ality (VR), and field experiments have linked preference to visible area 
[25,26], scene type [27], openness [23], scene depth [9,28], and spatial 
form [29]. Recent image-based studies have attempted to quantify 
prospect and refuge using low-level visual features, but evidence re­
mains mixed regarding whether openness and enclosure have stable and 
independent effects on preference [9,30,31].
Moreover, compositional and organizational attributes have often 
been examined separately, while limited attention has been paid to how 
landscape elements are spatially organized. Because the acquisition of 
distance-layered spatial structure information often relies on controlled 
experiments, subjective assessments, or costly scanning devices, low- 
cost and scalable quantification of compositional and organizational 
attributes in real-world settings remains a key challenge [32,33].
2.3. Machine learning and causal explanation in landscape preference 
research
In recent years, machine learning and deep learning techniques have 
provided new methodological pathways for landscape preference 
research. Compared with traditional linear regression models, methods 
such as XGBoost and random forest can better capture complex 
nonlinear relationships and often achieve higher predictive accuracy. 
Interpretation tools such as SHAP can also help reveal the predictive 
logic within models, thereby supporting design decision-making and 
management. Scalable feature-extraction methods, including low-level 
visual analysis, semantic segmentation, monocular depth estimation, 
and sound classification, make it possible to integrate visual [34], spatial 
[35], and auditory indicators [36] for landscape-preference research.
Although these models have clear advantages in feature extraction 
and predictive accuracy, the black-box nature of machine learning 
models makes it difficult for them to provide directly actionable evi­
dence for planning and design. This problem is especially evident when 
environmental elements are highly coupled, because multicollinearity 
and confounding may prevent correlation analysis or conventional 
machine learning from identifying the independent role of a single 
element. Causal inference has therefore been introduced into urban and 
environmental studies to estimate intervention-oriented effects under 
explicit assumptions, and some studies have further combined SCM with 
machine learning models [37–39]. However, in landscape perception 
research, the integration of causal inference with high-dimensional au­
dio-visual features and interpretable machine learning remains limited.

Such integration can help address confounding, identify key environ­

Based on the above gaps, this study addresses the following research 
questions: 
(1) How can dynamic audio-visual preference in waterfront walking 
environments be measured and represented through route-based 
stimuli, continuous ratings, spatial-structure metrics, and 
soundscape indicators?
(2) Which distance-layered spatial structure and soundscape vari­
ables show design-relevant associations with landscape prefer­
ence under a prespecified causal graph and observed-covariate 
adjustment?
(3) How do these variables vary across exposure levels, interact 
across sensory domains, and differ across park and functional 
contexts?
This study provides an empirical test of how spatial structure and 
soundscape interact in dynamic settings. Under explicit assumptions, it 
also seeks to advance an intervention-oriented explanation of observed 
relationships, thereby providing evidence to support fine-scale renewal 
and design decision-making in waterfront spaces.
3. Methods
The analytical workflow comprised four main steps. First, route- 
based first-person audio-visual stimuli were collected in five water­
front parks in Dalian and used in a real-time landscape-preference 
assessment experiment conducted in a Cave Automatic Virtual Envi­
ronment (CAVE). Second, computer-vision, audio-processing, and 
acoustic models were applied to extract spatial structure, low-level vi­
sual, and soundscape features from the audio-visual stimuli. Third, 
dose–response relationships were estimated conditional on a pre­
specified directed acyclic graph (DAG) and measured covariates, and 
their robustness was assessed using multiple refutation tests and a video- 
level block bootstrap based on Unique_Scene_ID. Finally, variables that 
passed robustness screening, together with the selected confounding 
controls, were used to build predictive models. SHAP analysis was then 
employed to interpret main effects, interaction patterns, and contextual 
heterogeneity in feature attribution.
3.1. Data collection
3.1.1. Study area
To identify key environmental cues associated with landscape pref­
erence under dynamic audio-visual conditions, the experiment used 
route-based first-person videos as stimuli. This design allowed partici­
pants to experience continuous visual–acoustic changes while reducing 
potential confounding effects from ambient thermal and meteorological 
conditions. Five waterfront parks in Dalian, Liaoning Province, China, 
were selected as study sites (Fig. 1): Dalian Botanical Garden (Park_1), 
Xishanhu Park (Park_2), Baiyun Yanshui Fitness Park (Park_3), Qiang­
guan Central Urban Wetland Park (Park_4), and Zelonghu Park (Park_5). 
These sites represent botanical, suburban waterfront, sports and fitness, 
urban wetland, and community waterfront park settings. They also vary 
in sound-source composition, spatial openness, activity intensity, and 
waterfront interface morphology, thereby increasing the environmental 
heterogeneity of the stimulus sample.
3.1.2. Audio-visual stimuli
Field data were collected under clear daytime conditions from late 
May to early June 2025. The data acquisition system was mounted at an 
adult eye-level height of 1.6 m to approximate first-person audio-visual 
perception during movement. Continuous visual footage was recorded 
using a DJI Osmo Action 4 action camera at 4K resolution and 60 fps. An 
Aihua ISV 1102 sound level meter was used to measure the A-weighted 
equivalent continuous sound pressure level (LAeq) during video 
recording. Sound-source information was captured using a TASCAM DR- 
05X recorder at 96 kHz/24-bit sampling resolution. Spoken start and 
end commands were recorded to mark each recording session, and the 
audio-visual information was precisely aligned during post-processing 
based on timestamps.
Noise reduction was first applied to the raw audio materials in Adobe 
Audition 2022, after which the audio and video streams were temporally

3.1.3. Dynamic landscape preference experiment
Participants were recruited online and then completed an in-person 
laboratory experiment. A total of 62 university students from landscape- 
related and non-landscape-related disciplines were recruited. After data 
cleaning, 59 valid participants were retained for analysis. To control the 
duration of a single experimental session and reduce participant fatigue, 
the 36 videos were divided into two subsets, and each participant 
viewed only 18 videos. The presentation order of videos within each 
subset was randomized. Because group assignment and video subset 
were not fully separable by design, Group was treated only as a 
contextual control and was not interpreted as an independently identi­
fiable experimental effect.
Previous research has reported that CAVE-based environments can 
provide good ecological validity in eliciting psychological responses 
[40]. Accordingly, the audio-visual stimuli in the landscape preference 
experiment were presented using a CAVE system. Preference ratings 
were recorded using a mobile-based continuous rating tool developed 
for this study. Before the formal experiment, participants completed a 
5-min practice session using a similar video that was not included in the 
experimental stimuli. Preference was rated on a continuous 1–7 scale, 
ranging from “dislike” to “like”. A 5-s neutral gray screen was presented 
after each video, and a 2-min break was provided after every six videos 
to minimize cumulative fatigue. The study-site examples and segmen­
tation of the dynamic audio-visual stimuli are shown in Fig. 2.
During the experiment, preference ratings were recorded at 1-s in­
tervals, and video-frame and acoustic features were extracted at the 
same interval. For analysis, subjective ratings and environmental fea­
tures were temporally matched and aggregated into adjacent 3-s time 
windows to reduce second-by-second rating noise and partially account 
for short-term response lag. Each 1-min video therefore generated 20 
consecutive video-window observations, yielding 720 video-window 
observations from the 36 videos. These video-window observations 
were used as the statistical units for subsequent causal inference and 
machine-learning analyses. These 720 records represented video- 
window analytical units derived from 36 videos × 20 time windows. 
They should not be interpreted as independent participant-level obser­
vations. The main SCM analysis therefore estimated group-level average 
response patterns at the video-window level. In the dose–response 
analysis and related robustness checks, Unique_Scene_ID was used as the 
video-level block resampling unit. Thus, the main analysis identified 
group-level average preference patterns at the video-window level 
rather than individual-level responses.
The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the authors' 
affiliated institution (Approval No.: YSSJ-20250415), and all partici­
pants provided written informed consent before the experiment. All data 
were anonymized before analysis, and the study was conducted in 
accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
3.2. Feature engineering
To identify the key drivers of dynamic landscape preference, this 
study constructed a multidimensional indicator system covering spatial 
structure, low-level visual features, and acoustic attributes. The ratio­
nale for feature selection and the variable definitions and calculation 
formulas are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S2.
For spatial-structure and low-level visual-feature extraction, video 
frames were first extracted at 1-s intervals using FFmpeg. Low-level 
visual features, including brightness, saturation, contrast, number of 
colors, and edge complexity, were then calculated from the extracted 
frames using OpenCV. Next, Mask2Former, pretrained on the ADE20K 
dataset [41], was applied to estimate the semantic-class proportions of 
sky, vegetation, water, roads, buildings, people, and other scene ele­
ments (for detailed mapping rules, see Supplementary Table S11). These 
semantic outputs were further aggregated into composite indicators, 
including green view index, blue view index, enclosure, openness, visual 
imageability, and disturbance.
Subsequently, the metric-depth version of Depth Anything V2 was 
used for outdoor scene depth estimation [42]. This version was 
fine-tuned on the Virtual KITTI 2 dataset and can generate metric depth 
maps in meters. Infrared distance measurements were also conducted in 
the field to compare actual distances with model-estimated distances 
and to assess the accuracy of the depth estimates. Based on the estimated 
depth values, the scene was divided into three distance layers: fore­
ground (0–10 m), midground (10–30 m), and background (>30 m).

The three distance layers were used as a pedestrian-scale oper­
ationalization of distance-dependent spatial perception rather than as 
universal psychophysical cutoffs. From an ecological perspective, visual 
perception during walking is shaped by the relationship between the 
moving body and the surrounding environment; therefore, spatial ele­
ments at different distances may provide different perceptual and 
behavioral affordances [43]. In depth-perception theory, the distinction 
between action space and vista space suggests that the range within 
approximately 30 m is more closely related to locomotion, avoidance, 
and action planning, whereas more distant space is perceived primarily 
through global layout, openness, and background continuity [44]. 
Accordingly, the >30 m layer was interpreted as the background or vista 
field. Within the action-related range, the 0–10 m and 10–30 m layers 
were further distinguished to reflect pedestrian-scale spatial experience. 
The 0–10 m layer represents the immediate near field where enclosure, 
disturbance, vegetation, facilities, path-edge conditions, and social 
presence directly shape bodily proximity and walking experience. This 
near-field interpretation is informed by proxemics, which emphasizes 
culturally and socially structured interpersonal distance, and by 
human-scale public-space theory, which highlights how pedestrian 
experience is shaped by eye-level spatial cues and social activities in 
public space [45–48]. The 10–30 m layer represents the middle-distance 
field in which activity patterns, water edges, vegetation groups, and 
spatial boundaries remain legible during movement [45,47]. This 
operationalization is also consistent with landscape visual-structure 
studies that distinguish foreground, middleground, and background 
views, while adapting this logic to the finer spatial scale of waterfront 
walking environments [49,50].
To examine whether the main findings depended on the exact dis­
tance thresholds, we conducted an additional distance-threshold sensi­
tivity analysis. The baseline scheme was S0 = 0–10 m / 10–30 m / 
>30 m. Three alternative schemes were tested by slightly shifting the 
foreground–midground and midground–background boundaries: S1 
(0–5 m / 5–20 m / >20 m), S2 (0–8 m / 8–25 m / >25 m), and S3 
(0–15 m / 15–35 m / >35 m). For each scheme, the distance-layered 
spatial variables were recalculated, the SCM-based dose–response 
curves were re-estimated, and the results were compared with the 
baseline scheme using effect strength, effect direction, Spearman cor­
relation, and root mean square error (RMSE). Detailed results are pro­
vided in Supplementary Section S8 and Table S12, with supplementary 
curves for variables ranked 5–14 shown in Fig. S4.
Outputs from Mask2Former and Depth Anything V2 were then 
combined to extract spatial-structure features across different distance 
layers. Because depth prediction is less stable in distant sky regions, such 
pixels were assigned to the open background through a rule-based 
procedure. Therefore, the resulting indicators should be interpreted as 
proxies for spatial structure rather than as an absolute 3D 
reconstruction.
For auditory-feature extraction, YAMNet was used to derive the 
mean confidence scores of Sound_Nature, Sound_Traffic, Sound_Human, 
and Sound_Quiet, representing natural sounds, traffic/mechanical 
sounds, human sounds, and quietness, respectively [51]. These outputs 
reflect the relative confidence of class labels and were used as proxy 
indicators for sound source types and soundscape perception. YAMNet is 
a pretrained acoustic recognition model based on the MobileNetV1 ar­
chitecture. It performs automatic multi-label classification of 521 sound 
classes in the AudioSet dataset by analyzing log-mel spectrogram fea­
tures. In addition, sharpness, roughness, and fluctuation strength were 
calculated using the MoSQITo Python library, following the psycho­
acoustic approach described in previous work [52].

3.3. Causal inference and SHAP interaction analysis
As illustrated in the overall research workflow (Fig. 3), this study 
adopted an analytical framework integrating an SCM with machine 
learning to estimate intervention-oriented associations between spatial- 
structure and soundscape features and landscape preference under the 
predefined DAG and observed covariates. The SCM was used for DAG- 
based adjustment and dose–response estimation, while SHAP was used 
to interpret predictive interactions and contextual heterogeneity. All 
core causal inference and statistical analyses were conducted in Python 
using DoWhy and related libraries.
3.3.1. Causal graph construction and dose–response curves
First, a predefined DAG was constructed to represent the hypothe­
sized relationships among variables, based on environmental perception 
theory and prior knowledge. The complete DAG, key pathways, and 
theoretical rationale are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and 
Table S3.
To clarify the assumptions underlying the SCM, the directed edges in 
the prespecified DAG were used as theory- and evidence-informed 
structural assumptions for DAG-based adjustment and intervention- 
oriented dose–response estimation. The main pathways were classified 
into three assumption types: theory-supported perceptual pathways, 
physical-process pathways, and empirical-assumption pathways. The 
perceptual pathways assumed that spatial structure, visual features, and 
soundscape exposure influence landscape preference through openness, 
enclosure, visual disturbance, landscape legibility, acoustic comfort, and 
audio-visual integration [3,11]. The physical-process pathways assumed 
that vegetation, ground surfaces, water surfaces, hard pavements, and 
built interfaces may influence acoustic and psychoacoustic indicators 
through sound absorption, reflection, scattering, shielding, and attenu­
ation [53,54]. The empirical soundscape pathways assumed that spatial 
form, functional area, activity intensity, and ecological background are 
associated with sound-source composition, including natural, human, 
traffic/mechanical, and quietness-related sounds [55,56]. In particular, 
the pathway from spatial structure to LAeq was specified through two 
linked assumptions: a physical-propagation assumption, in which spatial 
materials and interfaces may modify sound transmission, and a 
sound-source-distribution assumption, in which spatial form and func­
tional area may shape activity patterns, crowd aggregation, traffic 
proximity, and sound-source composition. Detailed explanations of the 
pathway assumptions, supporting references, and interpretation 
boundaries are provided in Supplementary Table S3.
Individual characteristics were not included as conventional con­
founders in the main SCM because the exposures in the main analysis 
were video-window-level environmental variables, whereas the 
outcome was the group-level mean preference score for the same video 
window. Variables such as noise sensitivity, professional background, 
aesthetic preference, or individual response lag may influence partici­
pants’ rating baselines and sensitivity to visual or acoustic cues, but they 
do not determine the LAeq, openness, water visibility, or visual distur­
bance level of a given video-window observation. These factors were 
therefore treated as potential sources of individual-level heterogeneity

To estimate intervention-oriented effects, each candidate environ­
mental variable was treated as an exposure T, and landscape preference 
was treated as the outcome Y. Under the prespecified DAG and the 
corresponding adjustment set Z, the target quantity was the expected 
preference score under intervention:

where do(T = t) denotes an intervention that sets the exposure to a given 
dose level. For each exposure, the observed range was divided into 100 
dose levels. Dose–response curves were estimated using SCM-based 
nonlinear prediction with 300 video-level block bootstrap resamples 
based on Unique_Scene_ID. Random forest models were used to fit 
nonlinear relationships among non-root nodes. The response amplitude 
within the central 95% exposure range was used as the effect-strength 
index. Detailed estimation procedures are provided in Supplementary 
Section S4.
3.3.2. Robustness checks
Four refutation tests were used to assess the robustness of the esti­
mated dose–response curves: placebo treatment, data subset refutation, 
random common cause (RCC), and mechanism-change tests. Placebo 
curves were expected to show near-null effects, whereas the other 
refutation curves were compared with the baseline curves using 
Spearman correlation and TOST equivalence testing. All refutation 
procedures were implemented with video-level block resampling. 
Detailed criteria are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

4. Results
4.1. Data preprocessing and methodological checks
In the multicollinearity test, three variables—Green_Fore, Enclose_­
Mid, and Vis_Entropy—had variance inflation factor (VIF) values greater 
than 10 and were therefore removed before machine-learning and 
causal-inference analyses (Table S6). After their removal, the VIF values 
of all remaining variables were below 10. Based on the predefined data- 
quality criteria, three participants were excluded: two due to abnormal 
recordings from the rating device and one due to an invalid response 
pattern in the rating sequence. The final valid sample included 59 par­
ticipants, with 32 in Group_1 and 27 in Group_2 (Table 1). Because the 
primary statistical unit in this study was the aggregated group-level 
average score, the average-measures intraclass correlation coefficient 
(ICC) was used to assess the reliability of group-level average ratings. 
The results showed that the ICC was 0.870 for Group_1 and 0.919 for 
Group_2, both statistically significant at p < 0.001, indicating good 
reliability of the group-level average ratings (Table S7). In the subse­
quent main analyses, all results were based on 720 video-window ob­
servations. These records were used only as units of analysis, and video- 
level block resampling based on Unique_Scene_ID was applied to pre­

The depth-layer validation results are reported in Supplementary 
Fig. S2 and Table S8. Based on 191 annotated validation points, the 
overall classification accuracy of foreground, midground, and back­
ground layers was 87.43%. Within the 0–80 m analysis range, model- 
predicted depth was strongly and significantly correlated with field- 
measured distance (r = 0.896, p < 0.001). Stratified validation by 
scene type further showed classification accuracies of 83.33% for 
wooded / shaded vegetation areas, 92.50% for open green / path- 
corridor areas, 87.88% for paved and facility-dominated areas, and 
86.05% for open water / waterfront-edge areas. These results indicate 
acceptable and reasonably stable distance-layer classification perfor­
mance across the tested scene types.
To further examine whether the main findings depended on the 
temporal aggregation window or the distance-layer thresholds, two 
sensitivity analyses were conducted. The temporal-window sensitivity 
analysis compared 1-s, 2-s, 3-s, and 5-s aggregation windows, whereas 
the distance-threshold sensitivity analysis compared the baseline 
0–10 m / 10–30 m / >30 m scheme with three alternative distance-layer 
schemes. The sensitivity analyses showed that the main dose–response 
patterns of the top-ranked variables remained generally stable under 
alternative temporal-window and distance-threshold specifications 
(Fig. 6). In particular, Aud_LAeq, Disturb_Mid, Disturb_Fore, Open_Back, 
Sound_Human, and Water_Mid retained consistent effect directions 
across the tested specifications, although moderate vertical shifts and 
local curve deviations were observed. The complete numerical results of 
the temporal-window sensitivity analysis are reported in Supplementary 
Table S13, with supplementary curves for variables ranked 5–14 shown 
in Fig. S5 and the integrated summary shown in Fig. S6. The complete 
numerical results of the distance-threshold sensitivity analysis are re­
ported in Supplementary Table S12, with supplementary curves for 
variables ranked 5–14 shown in Fig. S4 and the integrated summary 
shown in Fig. S6.

4.2.3. Dose–response relationships, SHAP interactions, and contextual 
heterogeneity
As shown in Fig. 5a–f, the dose–response curves of different variables 
exhibited varying degrees of nonlinearity. Aud_LAeq showed an overall 
negative dose–response pattern, with a more pronounced decline in 
preference above approximately 68–70 dBA. Disturb_Mid and Dis­
turb_Fore generally reduced preference as exposure increased, and the 
response amplitude of Disturb_Mid was higher than that of Disturb_Fore. 
Open_Back showed an overall positive effect, with preference increasing 
rapidly within the range of approximately 20%–30%, followed by a 
weakening marginal gain. Sound_Human showed an overall negative 
response pattern. Water_Mid showed an overall positive association with 
preference, with a more pronounced increase within the range of 
approximately 4%–10%.
