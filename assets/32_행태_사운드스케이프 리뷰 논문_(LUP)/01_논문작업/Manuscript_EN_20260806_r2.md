## Abstract

Soundscape research has established how people *judge* acoustic environments; what they *do* in them
has never been synthesised. We systematically reviewed and meta-analysed the relationship between the
acoustic environment and observable behaviour in urban open space, treating it as bidirectional.
Three databases were searched (2 August 2026) and supplemented, as registered, by citation tracking
of every included study and by a fourth index; the protocol was registered before screening
(osf.io/7ew8q). Ninety-eight studies were eligible. Quality was appraised with MMAT 2018 and effects
pooled using random-effects REML with the Hartung–Knapp adjustment. All four clusters pointed in the
predicted direction, but only two excluded zero for the mean effect: social interaction (*k* = 4,
*g* = +0.65, 95% CI +0.19 to +1.10) and the perception–behaviour correlation (*k* = 7, *r* = +0.41,
95% CI +0.23 to +0.56). **Prediction intervals included zero in every cluster**, and with a maximum
*k* of 7 no test of reporting bias was possible, so pooled estimates are upper bounds. The
social-interaction result comes from two field experiments thirteen years apart using different noise
sources; because no study contrasts natural sound against quiet, adding pleasant sound and removing
unpleasant sound cannot be separated. The widely cited walking-speed effect rests entirely on
low-quality studies, three of four effects manipulating audio through headphones rather than the
space itself, and largely from one research programme on one route. Of the domain-level records
extracted, 40% run in reverse — activity generating the soundscape — with near-parity in space use,
activity and social behaviour. Measurement generations accumulate rather than replace one another.
Ten planning levers are derived with graded confidence; none reaches high confidence, and the
best-supported action is controlling operational noise where people are meant to interact.

**Keywords**: soundscape; behaviour; urban open space; systematic review; meta-analysis; landscape planning

## 1. Introduction

**1.1 The gap.** Soundscape research has matured around perception. Since ISO 12913 formalised
pleasantness and eventfulness as the descriptive axes, a large literature has established *how people
judge* acoustic environments. What people *do* in them has been studied far less systematically, and
never synthesised. Yet planning decisions are made about behaviour — whether a square is used, whether
people linger, whether strangers speak to each other — not about ratings.

**1.2 Why this review, why now.** Seventy-one per cent of the eligible literature appeared in 2020 or
later (70 of 98 studies). The field crossed a measurement threshold: GPS traces, video analytics and
wearables made behaviour observable at scale for the first time. A synthesis is now possible that was
not possible five years ago.

**1.3 The bidirectional framing.** The dominant framing treats sound as exposure and behaviour as
outcome. Our corpus shows that framing is incomplete: 40% of the extracted domain-level records run
in the reverse direction — crowd density, activity programming and companionship *generate* the
acoustic environment. Space use, activity and social behaviour show near-parity between the two
directions. A review that only asks "what does sound do to people" misses half of what has been
measured.

**1.4 Positioning.** The nearest adjacent review (Cities & Health, 2026) covers physical activity only
and is therefore a subset. Zhang et al. (2025, *Landscape and Urban Planning*) meta-analysed
landscape → perception; this review takes the next link, perception/exposure → behaviour, and adds a
methodological axis that no prior synthesis has: how behaviour itself has been measured.

**1.5 Research questions.**
- **RQ1** — What is the magnitude and direction of the association between the acoustic environment and
  observable behaviour in urban open space, and does it differ by behavioural domain?
- **RQ2** — How much of the evidence runs in the reverse direction (behaviour → soundscape), and in
  which domains?
- **RQ3** — How has behavioural measurement changed, and does newer instrumentation replace or
  accumulate on older methods?
- **RQ4** — What can landscape planning actually prescribe from this evidence, and at what confidence?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Registration and reporting

The review was prospectively registered on OSF Registries (osf.io/7ew8q) using the Generalized
Systematic Review Registration form before screening began, and is reported following PRISMA 2020.
All deviations from the registered protocol are listed in Supplementary S1 and summarised in §2.7,
including those that changed a result. Analysis rules — effect-size metrics, contrast framing,
handling of multiple effects within a study, and pseudoreplication — were fixed in a separate
protocol document *before* any effect size was computed (Supplementary S2).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all five criteria:

1. **Population** — human participants (studies of animal behaviour, including soundscape ecology,
   were excluded).
2. **Setting** — outdoor or semi-outdoor urban/landscape public space: parks, streets, squares,
   waterfronts, outdoor campus and residential open space, and recreation areas. Laboratory and
   virtual-reality studies were eligible when the stimulus reproduced an outdoor scene, and were
   included in the main synthesis with a sensitivity analysis excluding them. Indoor, in-vehicle,
   hospital and workplace settings were excluded.
3. **Exposure** — the acoustic environment (noise, natural sounds, music, or soundscape as a
   composite) as an exposure, **or** the reverse pathway in which behaviour and activity shape the
   acoustic environment.
4. **Outcome** — at least one **observable behavioural outcome**: movement (walking speed, route,
   crossing), staying (dwell time, sitting), space use (visitation, occupancy), social interaction, or
   physical activity and leisure. Self-reported behaviour was eligible. Studies reporting only
   perception, preference, annoyance, restorativeness or physiological response were excluded; studies
   whose sole behavioural outcome was stated *intention* were retained for sensitivity analysis only.
5. **Design** — empirical, peer-reviewed journal article in English.

Two scope decisions were fixed at registration and are consequential for interpretation: Chinese-language
literature and conference proceedings were excluded. Both are addressed in the Limitations.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Three databases were searched on 2 August 2026: Web of Science Core Collection, Scopus, and PubMed.
The search used three concept blocks combined with AND — acoustic environment × behaviour × urban
outdoor setting — with the full strings given in Supplementary S3. The strategy was validated before
execution against five benchmark papers known to be eligible; all five were retrieved.

Two supplementary identification routes were registered and both were executed. **Backward and forward
citation tracking** was performed on every included study using the OpenAlex API. Separately, OpenAlex
was searched as a **fourth index** to identify records absent from the three databases; 352 unique
records were found and screened (Fig. 1).

### 2.4 Selection process

**Database branch.** After duplicate removal, 1,316 records were screened on title and abstract
against seven numbered exclusion criteria, using AI-assisted pre-classification with human
verification. Records that remained uncertain were **not excluded**; they were carried forward to
full-text assessment, because in this literature behavioural outcomes are frequently measured but not
mentioned in the abstract.

**Citation-tracking branch.** Backward references and forward citations of the 84 studies then
included yielded 2,073 records new to the screening pool. Because citation tracking has low topical
specificity, the registered three-block search logic was applied to titles as a priority filter;
1,645 records matching at most one block, or concerning animal acoustics, were deprioritised. The
remaining 428 were screened on title, then 146 on abstract, then sought in full text. This automatic
prioritisation was not specified at registration and is recorded as a deviation (S1, D1-3).

**Supplementary-index branch.** Of the 352 OpenAlex-unique records, 23 were duplicates of the pools
above and 329 were screened. Two proved eligible. This branch also produced the review's clearest
warning about metadata: of the five full texts obtained, **two were recorded as `language=en` but
were written in Korean and Japanese** (English appeared only in title and abstract). Document type
and language cannot be determined from index metadata alone.

**Boundary rules.** Three recurring boundary cases were resolved by rules fixed before individual
adjudication: residential noise exposure with non-specific physical activity → excluded; behavioural
intention as the sole outcome → sensitivity analysis only; reverse-direction studies with appraisal
outcomes → included, since the registered definition treats the reverse pathway as eligible. A fourth
rule — laboratory reproduction of outdoor scenes → sensitivity analysis only — was introduced during
the citation-tracking branch and therefore applied to that branch alone; the resulting inconsistency
between branches is recorded in the deviation log (S1, D6-1) and is addressed below (§3.3).

### 2.5 Data collection and quality appraisal

One reviewer extracted data from full texts into a fixed 16-field schema (setting, design, sample,
exposure, behavioural domain, measurement method, direction, key finding, and **verbatim effect
statistics with their location in the source**). Effect statistics were transcribed exactly as
printed; no conversion or estimation occurred at extraction.

Methodological quality was appraised with the **Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) 2018**. Because
the two randomised studies in the corpus reported neither the randomisation procedure nor blinding,
a separate RoB 2 assessment would have added no information and was not performed; this is recorded
as a deviation (S1, D2-2).

**One appraisal-specific caveat deserves emphasis.** Full texts were machine-extracted, and an initial
extraction cap truncated 13 of the 16 citation-tracked studies. MMAT scores a criterion "can't tell"
when reporting is absent, so truncation is indistinguishable from under-reporting and biases scores
*downward*. All truncated studies were re-appraised on complete text; seven changed at least one item
and four changed tier, three of them by two tiers (S12). We report this because any review using
automated full-text extraction is exposed to the same artefact, and we have not seen it reported
elsewhere.

### 2.6 Synthesis

Effects were pooled where at least three independent estimates shared a behavioural outcome and a
contrast frame. Standardised mean differences were expressed as Hedges' *g* with the small-sample
correction; 2 × 2 frequencies were converted via the odds ratio using Chinn's transformation
(*d* = ln(OR) × √3/π); correlations were pooled on Fisher's *z* and back-transformed. Pooling used
random-effects REML with the **Hartung–Knapp** adjustment, which is appropriate for the small numbers
of studies available here. Heterogeneity is reported as τ², *I*² and Q.

Where a study contributed more than one estimate to the same cluster and contrast frame, one effect
was selected by a fixed priority (most direct and objective measure > primary analysis > full sample).
Where two publications shared a sample, only one entered any pool. For pseudoreplicated designs the
participant *n* was used, not the observation *n*.

Because heterogeneity was substantial in two clusters, we report **95% prediction intervals**
(Higgins–Thompson–Spiegelhalter) alongside confidence intervals. The confidence interval describes the
precision of the mean; the prediction interval describes the range in which the effect of a new study
would be expected to fall, and is the quantity relevant to whether a planning decision can rely on the
result.

**Reporting bias could not be assessed.** The registered rule restricted funnel plots and Egger's test
to clusters with *k* ≥ 10; the largest cluster here has *k* = 7, so no test was performed in any
cluster. Small-study effects can therefore neither be detected nor excluded, and pooled estimates
should be read as upper bounds.

**Subgroup analyses were registered but not performed.** Splitting clusters of *k* = 3–7 by setting,
design or measurement generation yields subgroups of one to four estimates, below the pre-specified
minimum of three, and Hartung–Knapp intervals become uninterpretable at *k* = 2. Sources of
heterogeneity were instead examined narratively.

Eight sensitivity analyses were run: leave-one-out; exclusion of MMAT-low studies; recomputation on
observation *n*; exclusion of rank correlations; exclusion of inputs that required an assumption to
reconstruct; an alternative definition of the walking-speed pool; exclusion of studies that reproduced
outdoor scenes in the laboratory; and — added post hoc — exclusion of the citation-tracking branch, to
test whether conclusions depended on the supplementary search route. Full results are in S8.

### 2.7 Departures from the registered protocol

Ten departures are recorded in full in Supplementary S1. Four affected a reported result and are
therefore stated here; the remainder concern procedure and are summarised in the table.

**(1) MMAT categories were reassigned from full-text reading** rather than from the extracted design
string (D2-1). Category determines which items apply, so this changed tiers. The most common
misassignment was treating "objective acoustic measurement plus questionnaire" as mixed methods when
both components are quantitative.

**(2) The pooled walking-speed cluster excludes a music-stimulus study** (D3-6). Including it moves the
estimate from *g* = −0.500 (*p* = .179) to −0.742 (*p* = .082) — that is, **exclusion is the conservative
choice**, not one that favours our conclusion.

**(3) A sign-coding error of our own was found and corrected** (D3-4). The social-interaction cluster
initially returned *I*² = 93.6%; tracing it revealed that we had coded the odds ratio of Moser (1988)
in the wrong direction. After correction, *I*² = 43.5%. We record this because the heterogeneity
statistic functioned as an error detector, which is an argument for reporting it prominently at small *k*.

**(4) One eligible new effect was excluded from the significant cluster although including it would
have strengthened the result** (D5-4). CT0414 (Nanjing, MMAT high) contrasts an "auditory space"
against visual and other sensory spaces — not sound against sound — and its observation records
(2,249) exceed its observation units (1,167), so independence fails. Adding it would move
*p* = .021 → .005 and *I*² = 49% → 77%. It is reported as a sensitivity analysis only.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Study selection

Database searching returned 2,073 records (Web of Science 1,010; Scopus 850; PubMed 213). After
removing 757 duplicates, 1,316 records were screened, 1,127 were excluded, and 189 reports were sought.
Eighty-nine could not be obtained — 84 of which had been classified as uncertain at screening — leaving
100 full texts assessed. Sixteen were excluded and three reserved for sensitivity analysis, yielding
**81 studies** from this route.

Citation tracking of those studies produced 2,073 records new to the pool (413 backward, 1,660 forward).
After priority filtering, 428 were screened on title, 146 on abstract, and 79 sought in full text; 54
were obtained and assessed, of which 38 were excluded and one reserved for sensitivity analysis. This
route contributed a further **15 studies** — 19% of the database yield.

The supplementary index contributed 329 screened records, 15 sought, five assessed and **two included**
(Fig. 1). Neither contributes a poolable effect. The exclusion profile of this branch is itself
informative: 147 of 314 exclusions (47%) were non-empirical — book reviews, editorials, commentaries
and obituaries — because OpenAlex indexes far more broadly than the three databases. Absence from
WoS/Scopus/PubMed is therefore weak evidence of a missed study.

The final corpus is **98 studies**, with four additional studies reserved for sensitivity analysis
(102 analysed in total). Study characteristics are given in Table 1 and listed in full in S5.

The two large branches differed instructively in yield. Records for which an abstract could be retrieved
mechanically and confirmed a behavioural outcome converted at 46% (17 of 37); records for which no
abstract was machine-retrievable, and which therefore passed on title alone, converted at **0%**
(0 of 17). Abstract availability, not topical plausibility, governed screening accuracy — which also
implies that the 22 unretrieved title-only records are unlikely to have changed the corpus.

### 3.2 Study characteristics

The corpus is young and unevenly distributed. Seventy of 98 studies (71%) appeared in 2020 or
later; the median publication year is 2022, and the range extends back to 1975. Where a participant
count is reported (56 studies) the median is 124 (IQR 30–400); the remaining studies report
non-participant units — street segments, GPS trajectories, street-view sample points — the largest
being 81,403 cycling trips.

Settings are dominated by streets (41) and parks (32), with squares (10), campuses, residential open
space and waterfronts contributing the remainder. Geographically the evidence is concentrated:
**38 of 98 studies (39%) were conducted in China**, followed by Spain (7), the United Kingdom (6) and
Germany (5) (Fig. 7a). Given that soundscape appraisal is culturally contingent, this is the single
most important constraint on generalisation. Figure 7b shows a second, less obvious asymmetry:
reverse-direction studies appear only from the mid-2010s, so the bidirectional evidence base is
younger still than the corpus as a whole.

Most consequentially for what can be concluded, **only 19 studies (19%) manipulated the acoustic
environment in the field.** The modal design is a mixed observational-and-survey study (34), followed
by survey (25). The literature observes the acoustic environment far more often than it changes it.

### 3.3 Methodological quality

Of the 98 included studies, 22 were rated high, 40 moderate and 36 low on MMAT (Fig. 8). Two findings
in the item-level pattern matter more than the tier distribution.

First, **the two randomised studies are given no credit for their design.** Neither reports how
randomisation was performed, whether groups were comparable at baseline, or whether assessors were
blinded; all three items are scored "can't tell", leaving them with one and two criteria met
respectively. The strongest design in the corpus yields among its weakest appraisals, purely through
under-reporting. They are not, however, the corpus's weakest studies in absolute terms: four studies
met no criterion at all.

Second, and generalising that observation, **the largest deficits are deficits of reporting rather
than of conduct.** Sample representativeness was clearly met in 10 of 51 quantitative descriptive
studies (20%) and low non-response bias in 16 of 51 (31%); confounding was adequately controlled in
9 of 22 non-randomised studies (41%). In a field that reports sound pressure to three decimal places,
response rates are routinely absent.

Excluding the five studies that reproduced outdoor scenes in virtual reality changes no pooled
estimate, because none of them contributed a poolable effect; they enter the narrative synthesis only.
This also resolves the branch inconsistency noted in §2.4 (D6-1): the rule was applied to only one
branch, but since no such study is poolable, the inconsistency has no quantitative consequence.

### 3.4 Meta-analyses

Four clusters met the pooling threshold (Fig. 2, Table 2).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Walking speed (natural sound vs noise) | 4 | *g* | −0.500 | −1.411, +0.412 | −2.56, +1.56 | .179 | 75.7% |
| Staying / dwell time (positive sound vs control) | 3 | *g* | +0.313 | −0.076, +0.702 | −0.79, +1.41 | .074 | 54.4% |

All four estimates point in the theoretically predicted direction: noise accelerates passage, positive
sound extends stay, quiet and natural sound increase social interaction, and appraisal covaries with
behaviour at moderate strength. **The clusters differ sharply, however, in whether that direction is
established with any precision.**

Social interaction reaches significance on the strength of two field experiments thirteen years apart —
Mathews and Canon (1975), in which a lawnmower raised the ambient level from ~50 to ~87 dB(C) and
helping behaviour fell from 20 of 40 to 5 of 40 (OR = 7.00, *d* = +1.07), and Moser (1988), which used
roadworks noise in a Paris street, applied the same 2 × 2 logic, and reached the same sign. The two used
unrelated noise sources and different helping paradigms, which makes the agreement more informative than
*k* = 4 alone suggests. (Mathews and Canon do not state where data were collected, so we do not claim
cross-national replication.)

**This significance is nevertheless contingent on one study obtained by citation searching.** The full
leave-one-out profile for this cluster is: removing Chen and Kang (2023) gives *g* = +0.551
(*p* = .057); removing Chen et al. (2024), +0.740 (*p* = .071); removing Moser (1988), +0.767
(*p* = .044); removing Mathews and Canon (1975), +0.569 (*p* = .053). **Three of four removals cross
back over *p* = .05.** The cluster is significant as a set rather than on the strength of any single
study, and the citation-searched study is the one whose removal returns the cluster to its pre-retrieval
state. We report this as a limitation of the result and, simultaneously, as evidence that the registered
supplementary search route was not a formality.

The correlational cluster, by contrast, is insensitive to search route (*r* = .409 with citation
tracking, .426 without) and to any single study (leave-one-out range *r* = .372 to .449) but carries
very high heterogeneity (*I*² = 90.5%), which is expected given that it mixes forward and reverse
pathways and several behavioural outcomes.

**Prediction intervals qualify both significant results.** The two intervals that exclude zero do so
for the *mean* effect; the interval within which a new study's effect would be expected to fall
includes zero in every cluster, including social interaction (−0.06 to +1.35) and the correlational
cluster (*r* = −0.08 to +0.74). Neither result licenses the expectation that a further study will
reproduce the sign. Reporting bias compounds this: with a maximum *k* of 7, no funnel plot or Egger
test was possible, so small-study effects can be neither detected nor excluded, and every pooled
estimate should be read as an upper bound.

**The walking-speed cluster is the weakest in the corpus, and its weakness is instructive.** All four
contributing effects come from studies rated low on MMAT; excluding low-quality studies leaves nothing
to pool (*k* = 0). Three high-quality walking studies exist in the corpus, but none could enter the
pool — one manipulated music tempo rather than environmental sound (Franěk et al., 2014), one reported
no direct test of noise against speed (Franěk & Režný, 2021), and one analysed augmented footstep audio
delivered through headphones (Schrapel et al., 2022). The most frequently cited claim in this
literature — that noise makes people walk faster — rests on the least secure evidence in it.

### 3.5 Direction of the relationship (RQ2)

Across the 212 domain-level records extracted from the 98 studies, 113 ran forward (sound → behaviour),
74 ran in reverse (behaviour → sound) and 25 were bidirectional. **Reverse-direction records are 40%
of the directional records** (74 of 187 forward-or-reverse; 35% if the bidirectional records are placed
in the denominator) (Fig. 4). The unit here is the study × behavioural-domain record, not the study.

The reverse pathway is not spread evenly. Movement (33 forward vs 8 reverse) and staying (16 vs 8) are
dominated by forward designs — the behaviours that are easiest to manipulate experimentally. Space use
(26 vs 23), activity (18 vs 21) and social behaviour (20 vs 14) approach parity. Where behaviour is
collective and sustained, researchers have measured how it *produces* the acoustic environment: crowd
density predicting sound pressure, activity programming generating broadcast music, companionship
altering what is noticed. A unidirectional framing would discard nearly half of what this literature
has measured.

### 3.6 Measurement generations (RQ3)

Sensor, GPS, video and big-data measurement of behaviour grew from four studies in 2010–2019 to 20 in
2020 onward. Over the same period self-report grew from 14 to 39 and systematic observation from 11 to
28 (Fig. 5). **Newer instrumentation has not displaced older methods; it has been layered on top of
them.** Twenty-two studies use two or more generations concurrently. The answer to RQ3 is accumulation,
not succession — which matters because it means the field's capacity for triangulation is growing
faster than its raw output.

### 3.7 Coverage and gaps

Every behavioural domain × sound source cell in the evidence map is populated (Fig. 3), with one
exception: **aircraft noise appears in four domain-level records, from two studies.** Research around
airports has remained framed by health and annoyance, and has almost never measured what people do with
outdoor space.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 A bidirectional framework for soundscape and behaviour

Figure 6 sets out the framework this review arrives at. Two arcs close a loop. The **forward** arc runs
acoustic environment → appraisal → behaviour and is the one the field has assumed. The **reverse** arc
runs activity and occupancy → sound production → acoustic environment, and accounts for 40% of the
directional records. Between them sit moderators that determine whether a given sound produces
approach or avoidance: setting type, visual–acoustic congruence, purpose of stay, and cultural context.

Behaviour is not a single outcome but a **gradient of engagement** — avoid, pass, linger, interact,
appropriate — and the evidence attaches to that gradient unevenly. Placing the pooled estimates on the
gradient (Fig. 6) makes the field's shape visible in one view: the two ends are weakly evidenced, and
the strongest quantitative signal sits at *interact*.

The framework's value is diagnostic as much as descriptive. It shows that the literature has organised
itself around the forward arc while producing nearly half its measurements on the reverse one, and that
the behavioural gradient has been sampled without regard to which rungs planning actually acts on.

### 4.2 What the evidence supports, and how strongly (RQ4)

We translated the corpus into ten **planning levers** — things a designer or operator can change —
each carrying a confidence grade derived from study count, MMAT quality composition, whether the
interval excludes zero, setting diversity, and behaviour under sensitivity analysis (Table 3).

**No lever reaches high confidence.** Four are moderate (audio programming; natural sound introduction;
quiet route provision; control of mechanical and maintenance noise), five are low, and one is very low.
That distribution is itself the review's principal finding for practice: this field can currently
support *hypotheses to test with monitoring*, not design standards.

Two attributions matter more than the grades.

**First, the social-interaction result cannot be attributed to either adding natural sound or removing
noise.** It would be convenient to read the significant cluster as evidence for water features and
birdsong, and equally convenient to read it as evidence for silencing machinery. Neither reading is
supported. Splitting the four effects by exposure gives almost identical means (natural sound +0.76,
machinery noise +0.75), and removing the roadworks study *raises* rather than lowers the pooled estimate
(+0.65 → +0.77). More fundamentally, the two natural-sound studies contrast birdsong and water against
traffic and construction — they add natural sound *and* remove noise simultaneously. **The corpus
contains no contrast of natural sound against quiet**, so the two mechanisms are not separately
identified by any study in it. What can be said is narrower: the increment that carried this cluster
across the significance threshold came from one machinery-noise experiment retrieved by citation
searching.

**Second, the walking-speed literature is narrower than its citation count suggests.** Beyond the
quality collapse already reported, two structural features emerged during appraisal. Three of the four
pooled effects manipulated audio **through headphones** during a walk rather than changing the sound of
the space. And the walking evidence is substantially **one research programme**: three of the four
pooled effects come from two studies by the same Czech group (Franěk et al., 2018, 2019), and the two
new high-quality walking papers are from the same group on the same 1.75–1.8 km circuit, two of them
sharing an identical belt-camera and annotation protocol. The remaining pooled effect is an Algerian
field observation whose sign is opposite (Berkouk et al., 2020).

Outside the pool, one study did change the sound of a real street: Ba et al. (2024) ran covert
observation under traffic noise at 55.6 versus 70.5 dB(A), crossed with plant odour. Mean crowd speed
was about 1.14 m/s under the low level and about 1.21 m/s under the high level — roughly 0.07 m/s
faster under noise, in the direction the literature predicts, and the authors note the largest
difference between any two measurement grids was 0.06 m/s. It is rated low on MMAT. So the one
real-environment manipulation available produces an effect of a few centimetres per second. A claim
repeated on one route in one city, largely through headphones, is not a replicated finding, and the
meta-analytic *k* does not reveal this.

### 4.3 Implications for landscape planning

The practical reading of Table 3 is ordered by what can be defended.

**Act on operational noise first.** The best-supported behavioural consequence in this corpus is that
machinery noise suppresses social behaviour — including help offered to strangers. Ventilation plant,
mowing schedules and construction windows are within routine operational control, cost little to
change, and are supported by two independent field experiments using different noise sources.

**Treat added sound as a dwell-time instrument, not an attraction device.** Music extends the stay of
people already present; the one high-quality test of arrival (Aletta et al., 2016) found no effect on
the number of people stopping. Programming justified by projected visitor counts overstates what the
evidence shows.

**Introduce a speech-intelligibility criterion for social space siting** (lever L10, new in this
revision). Where people are meant to talk — bench clusters, terraces, meeting points — the relevant
threshold is not annoyance but whether conversation survives. The evidence supports a continuous
gradient rather than a cut-off: roughly a quarter-point increase in self-reported voice-raising per
decibel (Montes González et al., 2023). That is enough to compare candidate locations, not enough to
set a standard.

**Do not use walking speed as a design KPI**, and do not transfer headphone findings to public space.

The full set of prescriptions we consider *unsupported* is given in Table 3 notes; two are worth
flagging because they are common in practice. Masking traffic noise with water to increase use has
**no behavioural test in this corpus at all** — the belief is imported from perception studies. And
noise reduction as a route to physical activity is contradicted rather than merely unsupported: two of
the largest datasets here find noise positively associated with activity (Dzhambov et al., 2026; Huang
et al., 2023), because activity concentrates where cities are loud.

### 4.4 Methodological implications

**The field measures sound far better than it measures people.** The largest quality deficits are not
design flaws but reporting omissions — sample representativeness clearly met in one study in five,
low non-response bias in roughly one in three. A literature that reports sound pressure to three
decimals routinely omits its response rate. The two randomised studies score lowest for exactly this
reason.

**Measurement generations accumulate rather than replace.** Sensing has grown fastest, but self-report
and systematic observation grew with it, and twenty-two studies now combine two or more. The field's
capacity for triangulation is growing faster than its output — an opportunity, if studies report enough
for the layers to be compared.

**A caution for evidence synthesis itself.** Automated full-text extraction interacts badly with
appraisal instruments that score unreported items as "can't tell". Truncated extraction moved four of
sixteen studies down at least one quality tier, three of them by two tiers, and made a whole search
route look like it had retrieved weaker work. The bias is one-directional and invisible without a
deliberate audit. We recommend that reviews using machine extraction report their extraction limits and
verify that appraised studies were assessed on complete text.

**A second caution concerns index metadata.** Two of five full texts retrieved through the
supplementary index were indexed as English but written in Korean and Japanese. Reviews that enforce a
language criterion from metadata rather than from the document will silently violate their own
protocol.

### 4.5 Research agenda

1. **One well-reported walking experiment would change a whole cell.** Manipulate the sound of a real
   space (not headphones), randomise, report the procedure, baseline comparability and blinding. The
   current pooled estimate rests on studies that would each fail two MMAT criteria.
2. **Aircraft noise and behaviour is effectively empty** — two studies. Research around airports has
   stayed inside health and annoyance framings and has almost never asked what people do with outdoor
   space.
3. **Close the design → acoustic output → behaviour chain.** Natural-sound evidence is almost entirely
   loudspeaker playback. No study verifies that planting produces birdsong that then changes behaviour.
4. **Measure persistence.** Almost all interventions run for hours or days; the longest is one season.
   Whether behavioural change survives withdrawal, or habituates, is unknown — and it is what budget
   decisions turn on.
5. **Build reverse-direction prediction rules.** Space use, activity and social behaviour show
   near-parity between directions, yet only a handful of studies give rules for predicting the sound a
   design will generate from the activity it hosts.
6. **Report the basics.** A substantial share of otherwise eligible studies could not enter any pool
   because group *n*, SD, or the analytic unit was not stated.

### 4.6 Limitations

The corpus is geographically narrow (39% of studies from China) and young (71% since 2020), so pooled
estimates should not be read as culturally general. English-language and journal-only restrictions,
fixed at registration, exclude a literature in which Chinese-language contributions are substantial.
One hundred and twenty-two reports could not be retrieved across the three routes (89 database, 25
citation-tracking, 8 supplementary index); retrieval was 92% for citation-tracked records whose
abstracts confirmed a behavioural outcome, and the records that could not be retrieved were
overwhelmingly those screened on title alone, none of which proved eligible when obtained. Screening
and extraction were performed by one reviewer with AI assistance and verification rather than by two
independent reviewers. Two publication pairs share samples; only one member of each pair entered any
pool. Reporting bias could not be assessed in any cluster because *k* never reached the pre-specified
threshold of 10, so small-study effects remain a live possibility and the pooled estimates are upper
bounds. Registered subgroup analyses were not performed for the same reason — clusters of three to
seven estimates cannot be split. Prediction intervals include zero in all four clusters, so neither
significant result should be read as a forecast for a new study. Finally, the significance of the
social-interaction cluster depends on a single study obtained by citation searching, and would not
have been observed from database searching alone.

## 5. Conclusions

Sound changes what people do in urban open space, but the evidence for that claim is unevenly
distributed across behaviours, and its strongest and weakest parts are not where the literature's
citation patterns suggest.

Across 98 studies, four meta-analytic clusters all point in the theoretically predicted direction, yet
only two exclude zero for the mean effect — and none excludes zero in its prediction interval. The
best-established behavioural consequence of the acoustic environment is **social**: machinery noise is
associated with reduced interaction between strangers in two independent field experiments using
different noise sources. The most frequently cited claim — that noise makes people walk faster — rests
on four effects that are all rated low quality, mostly manipulate audio through headphones rather than
changing the space, and come substantially from a single research programme on a single route.

Nearly half the evidence runs in the reverse direction: people's activity produces the soundscape as
much as the soundscape shapes their activity. Any framework for planning that treats sound only as
exposure discards this.

For practice, the defensible actions are narrow but real. Control operational noise where people are
meant to interact. Use added sound to extend stay, not to attract arrivals. Judge social-space siting
by whether conversation survives. And do not prescribe from perception studies what has never been
tested on behaviour.

For research, the highest-value next study is unglamorous: one field experiment that manipulates the
sound of a real place, randomises properly, and reports its methods completely. It would move a cell
of this evidence base that half a century of citation has not.
